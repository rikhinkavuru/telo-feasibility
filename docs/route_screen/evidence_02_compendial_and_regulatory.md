## 1. The terminal-first rule, and the fact that it lives in different places in the two jurisdictions

**FDA.** The statement is in guidance, not in regulation and not in the compendium. From the frozen 2004 aseptic processing guidance (**S07**, `fda_aseptic_processing_2004.pdf`, section III): "It is a well-accepted principle that sterile drugs should be manufactured using aseptic processing only when terminal sterilization is not feasible." The inspection compliance program puts it as an expectation on the firm: "Terminal sterilization should be utilized when the product and container/closure system are able to withstand the terminal sterilization process" [F11-S15]. The same guidance defines the target: terminal sterilization is "The application of a lethal agent to sealed, finished drug products for the purpose of achieving a predetermined sterility assurance level (SAL) of usually less than 10-6" (**S07**, glossary; the "-6" is superscript in the source).

**EU, three layers, and the top one is compendial.** EU GMP Annex 1 paragraph 8.34: "Where possible, finished product should be terminally sterilised, using a validated and controlled sterilisation process, as this provides a greater assurance of sterility than a validated and controlled sterile filtration process and/or aseptic processing. Where it is not possible for a product to undergo terminal sterilisation, consideration should be given to using post-aseptic processing terminal heat treatment, combined with aseptic process to give improved sterility assurance" [F11-S04]. Paragraph 8.37: "Where possible, heat sterilisation is the method of choice" [F11-S04]. EMA's sterilisation guideline section 4.3 attributes the rule to the pharmacopoeia: "Finished products intended to be sterile should be terminally sterilised in their final container whenever possible, as clearly stated in the Ph. Eur., general chapter 5.1.1" [F11-S06]. Its section 3 gives the reason: "terminal sterilisation provides the highest assurance of sterility and should be used whenever possible" [F11-S06].

**USP does not carry this rule, and could not enforce it if it did.** The chapters that cover this subject are `<1211>` Sterility Assurance, `<1222>` Terminally Sterilized Pharmaceutical Products - Parametric Release, `<1223>` Validation of Alternative Microbiological Methods, and the `<1229>` family, whose scope is "an overview of the concepts and principles involved in sterilization (by various modes) of compendial articles that must be sterile" [RS2-S07]. All are numbered above 1000. USP's own FAQ lists "General Chapters numbered between `<1000>` and `<1999>`" among the sections that "contain informational official text intended to aid compendial users, but do not state requirements with which users must comply" [RS2-S06]. [inference] The only mandatory compendial chapter in this space is then `<71>` Sterility Tests, which is numbered below 1000 and so is not on USP's informational list, and it prescribes a test, not a route. **So the US route preference is guidance-level throughout, and the EU route preference is pharmacopoeial and GMP-level.** [inference, UNCERTAIN] This asymmetry means a US applicant's route choice is challenged, if at all, through the application review and inspection, not through a compendial requirement; an EU applicant must argue it in the dossier against a written decision tree.

**UNCERTAIN and not resolved here:** whether Ph. Eur. 5.1.1 is itself mandatory under Ph. Eur. General Notices, or informational until referenced by a monograph. The chapter is paywalled and was not fetched. What EMA does with it is documented above; what its own compendial status is, is not.

## 2. When aseptic filling is permitted instead, in the regulators' own words

EMA's guideline is the only instrument found that enumerates the permitted grounds. It first sets the burden: "During the manufacturer's evaluation of whether a terminal sterilisation cycle is possible, substantial efforts should be made to enable terminal sterilisation", and if the product degrades, "the efforts made to develop a formulation and container capable of undergoing terminal sterilisation should be presented in the development section. Such efforts could be selection of optimal pH, choice of excipients (qualitative and quantitative), container, optimisation of sterilisation method and manufacturing conditions" [F11-S06].

Then it closes four escape routes that a route-conversion screen would otherwise expect to be open:

- Shelf life. "An impact on the shelf-life or storage conditions caused by a terminal sterilisation process is not in itself a reason to exclude terminal sterilisation, unless the new storage condition or shelf-life would cause significant problems for the user" [F11-S06].
- Degradants. "Terminal sterilisation should not be ruled out purely on the basis of an increase in degradation products above the qualification thresholds in ICH Q3A/VICHGL10 ..., ICH Q3B/VICH GL11 ... or the impurity limits in ICH M7 ... without additional justification. If impurities are either metabolites or are generated at levels already qualified, then terminal sterilisation is still considered feasible" [F11-S06].
- Container. "The choice to use a heat-labile container cannot in itself be the sole reason for not applying a terminal sterilisation process and alternative materials should be investigated" [F11-S06].
- Heat-labile actives are the one clean exemption: "In case of medicinal products containing highly sensitive active substances, (e.g. proteins or other heat labile biological substance), where it is well known that terminal sterilisation is not possible, a justification based on a scientific rationale is generally acceptable" [F11-S06].

Where aseptic filling is accepted although the formulation itself could be autoclaved, the grounds are container-driven and user-driven: single-drop eye-drop containers, preservative-free multi-dose non-parenteral containers, "Enhanced ease of administration", safer handling of toxic products, and radiopharmaceuticals with a shelf life under one week [F11-S06]. FDA's version of the same carve-out is one sentence: "some final packaging may afford some unique and substantial advantage (e.g., some dual-chamber syringes) that would not be possible if terminal sterilization were employed. In such cases, a manufacturer can explore the option of adding adjunct processing steps to increase the level of sterility assurance" (**S07**, section III).

**Consequence for the screen, stated plainly.** For a simple aqueous small-molecule injectable in a glass vial, which is what five of the six longlist presentations are, none of the enumerated grounds obviously applies. That raises the prior that such products are terminally sterilized where the chemistry allows. **It is a prior and not a determination.** It is E6 reasoning under `method.md` section 3 and cannot exceed `weakly_inferred` on its own, and it is doubly weak in the US, where the preference is guidance rather than a dossier requirement, so a US holder's aseptic choice may never have been argued against a decision tree at all.

## 3. The decision tree, and where it actually is

The task asks for "EU Annex 1 2022 and the decision tree for route selection". **Annex 1 contains no decision tree.** The full 2022 text was fetched and searched; the route rule is the single paragraph 8.34 quoted above, and the only other route-selection sentence is 8.37 [F11-S04]. Annex 1's remaining relevance to route is downstream: 8.35 to 8.39 on cycle design, validated loading patterns and revalidation; 8.54 pointing parametric release to Annex 17; and 10.3 to 10.11 on release testing [F11-S04].

The decision trees are in EMA's sterilisation guideline, section 5, and the guideline says so: it "replaces the document Decision trees for the selection of sterilisation methods" [F11-S06]. Figure 1 is for aqueous products, Figure 2 for dry powders and non-aqueous liquids, Figure 3 for containers. The operative ordering rule is textual: "When moving down the decision trees, the methods generally show a decreasing assurance of sterility and therefore, the first feasible option should normally be chosen", with heat ranked above ionising irradiation because it "has lower risk (e.g. radiolysis impurities) and is more easily controlled" [F11-S06]. The steam envelope is numeric: "All steam sterilisation processes require a minimum lethality of F0 >= 8 minutes and a minimum process hold temperature of 110 degrees C"; the Ph. Eur. reference condition is ">=121 degrees C, >=15 min in all units", and for that reference condition "validation data for the sterilisation cycle is not required to be submitted in the quality dossier"; routine bioburden limit 100 CFU/100 ml for parenteral formulations [F11-S06].

**The three figures are raster images in the published PDF.** Their branch text could not be extracted by any tool used here and is therefore **not reproduced**, at any confidence. A qualified reviewer reading the PDF can transcribe them; this file does not guess them.

## 4. FDA parametric release: scope, requirements, filing

**Scope, verbatim, and it is narrow.** CPG 490.200: "This policy applies only to parenteral drug products that are terminally moist-heat sterilized. It does not apply to products sterilized by filtration, radiation, dry heat, or ethylene oxide" [F11-S02]. The CPG supersedes CPG 460.800 (1987) and is dated July 2012 [F11-S02]. The 2010 CMC guidance leaves a crack the CPG does not: "The principles in the guidance may also be applicable to products sterilized by other terminal sterilization processes, such as radiation sterilization, which may be suitable for parametric release. For these types of applications, we recommend the applicant discuss with the review division whether applying the guidance would be appropriate" [F11-S03]. **UNCERTAIN which instrument controls**; this is unchanged from family 11 and is for HA-31.

**What it is.** "Parametric release is defined as a sterility assurance release program where demonstrated control of the sterilization process enables a firm to use defined critical process controls, in lieu of the sterility test, to fulfill the intent of 21 CFR 211.165(a), and 211.167(a)" [F11-S03]. The regulations it displaces are in the frozen CFR snapshot (**S06**, `CFR-2025-title21-vol4.xml`): 211.167(a), "For each batch of drug product purporting to be sterile and/or pyrogen-free, there shall be appropriate laboratory testing to determine conformance to such requirements"; and 211.165(a), which requires "appropriate laboratory determination of satisfactory conformance to final specifications ... prior to release" and carves out only short-lived radiopharmaceuticals.

**How the substitution is made lawful.** "If a firm's sterility assurance program is in a demonstrated state of control, as defined above, an appropriate load monitor, considered a critical process parameter under a parametric release program, would satisfy the requirements for a laboratory test (21 CFR 211.167(a))" [F11-S02]. The four systems: "(1) sterilization process validation and control, (2) verification by load monitor(s), (3) a validated container/closure system, and (4) an effective Quality System" [F11-S02]. Validation must "demonstrate microbial bioburden reduction consistent with existing Agency guidance to achieve a probability of non-sterile unit of 1 in 10 6 units or better" [F11-S02; the "6" is superscript in the source].

**What the submission must contain** [F11-S03, section III]: a control strategy naming the critical process parameters with minimum and maximum limits, the container-closure system, the production loading patterns, and the pre-sterilization microbiological monitoring plan; an ICH Q9 risk assessment; a described and justified load monitor, with "a Class 5 indicator ... recommended for most situations"; and two written acknowledgements. The first is the one-way ratchet: "adherence to the critical parameters of the parametric release program will substitute for the performance of a sterility test as the primary release criterion for the product and that sterility test results from the finished product will not be used to overrule any failure to meet the acceptance criteria of the parametric release program. In the event of failure, the specific sterilizer load will be rejected by the quality control unit and will not be released unless there is a provision for reprocessing" [F11-S03]. The second keeps the compendial test alive off the release path: any specimen tested to the reference method "will meet the criteria for sterility (such as during testing for stability or postmarketing investigations)" [F11-S03]. Certificates of analysis and batch release records must be revised to say parametric release is the method used [F11-S03; F11-S46].

**Filing.** "For changes to an approved application, the request for parametric release should be submitted in a prior approval supplement under 21 CFR 314.70, 21 CFR 601.12 or 21 CFR 514.8(b)(2). The change to parametric release requires FDA approval before its implementation" [F11-S03].

**The abbreviated route, and the sentence that bounds it.** "If the applicant has current experience using parametric release with a comparable sterilization cycle **at the same manufacturing site**, and the proposed product's manufacturing process fits into the same validation protocol for parametric release (e.g., container closure system, load patterns, cycle process parameters, and cycle acceptance criteria), then the applicant should meet the filing requirements with a special report" under 21 CFR 314.81(b)(3)(ii) [F11-S03, emphasis added]. This is a second, independent instrument saying what the QbR FAQ said [F11-S46]: the shortcut is conditioned on one site.

**The precondition that a new plant cannot satisfy on day one.** FDA's lead author on the 2010 guidance writes that the historical data supporting the request "represent the experience using the terminal sterilization cycle with the specific product, specific container closure system(s), specific load size(s), specific sterilizer(s), and the same facility as proposed for parametrical release", and that "Generally 'experience' is acquired from the performance of successful terminal sterilization and release of a product based on the results of a sterility test for a period of time" [RS2-S08]. **UNCERTAIN**, because this is a trade article by an agency scientist and not agency policy, and it names no required number of batches or months. If it is right, `method.md` section 4's "third state", terminal route with a full 14-day sterility hold, is not a modelling option to be avoided but the **mandatory first phase** of every parametric release program.

## 5. EU parametric release: same mechanism, wider modality scope, explicitly site-bound

Annex 17 paragraph 4.1 defines it as "the release of a batch of terminally sterilised product based on a review of critical process control parameters rather than requiring an end-product testing for sterility" [F11-S05]. Paragraph 4.3: "Parametric release can only be applied to products sterilised in their final container using either moist heat, dry heat or ionising radiation (dosimetric release), according to European Pharmacopoeial requirements" [F11-S05]. The compendial source of that scope, quoted verbatim inside EMA's RTRT guideline: "When a fully validated terminal sterilisation method by steam, dry heat or ionising radiation is used, parametric release, that is the release of a batch of sterilised items based on process data rather than on the basis of submitting a sample of the items to sterility testing, may be carried out, subject to the approval of the competent authority" [RS2-S09 in RS2-S01]. Gas is excluded on both sides: EMA's sterilisation guideline states "Parametric release is not acceptable for gas sterilisation (according to Ph. Eur. chapter 5.1.1)" [F11-S06], and the US CPG excludes ethylene oxide [F11-S02].

Requirements track the US list: documented sterility assurance programme, GMP compliance history, quality risk management, per-batch pre-sterilization bioburden with worst-case sampling [F11-S05 4.4 to 4.9], sterilization records "checked for compliance to specification by at least two independent systems ... two people or a validated computer system plus a person" [F11-S05 4.17], and the same ratchet: "Non-compliance with the specification for parametric release cannot be overruled by a finished product passing the test for sterility" [F11-S05 4.18]. Annex 1 adds the per-batch bioburden assay for parametrically released product at 10.4, and the sub-batch sterility rule for terminally sterilized product at the note to 10.6 [F11-S04].

**The EU answers the portability question that the US instruments only imply.** RS2-S01: "The authorization of the RTRT programme will be granted for specified sites on the basis of an assessment of the product and process understanding together with the proposed control strategy as described in the submission and implemented in accordance with GMP", and "The approval of RTRT by the competent authority will be based on an assessment of the regulatory submission as well as the knowledge of sufficient experience with the process and the GMP compliance at the actual site" [RS2-S01]. Reversal is also priced: "If a company after approval of RTRT wishes to return to end product testing, a variation of the marketing authorization is required" [RS2-S01]. PIC/S adds an eligibility bar for cycles: "Only terminal sterilization processes that incorporate large safety margins will be considered for parametric release" [RS2-S10, **snippet only, source row marked unusable 2026-09-06**: no document id, no URL, no access date, never fetched in full. Carry this sentence as unverified and do not build on it].

**This narrows, without closing, open question 1 of `../design_space/prior_art_review.md` section 7.** Two independent instruments now condition the mechanism on a named site: the EU grants the authorisation "for specified sites" [RS2-S01], and the US abbreviated filing route requires "the same manufacturing site" [F11-S03]. Neither says an approved programme cannot be extended to a second site by its own prior approval supplement; both say it does not travel for free. [inference, UNCERTAIN] For a distributed network this is the per-node reading, and it is now supported by primary text rather than by the QbR FAQ alone.

## 6. Is there a public register of products approved for parametric release?

**No such register was found, in either jurisdiction.** Searched: FDA guidance and CPG pages, `accessdata.fda.gov`, Drugs@FDA and Orange Book snapshots (**S16**, **S17**), EMA medicines and EPAR resources, and general web search. FDA publishes guidance on how to obtain parametric release and no list of who has it. The frozen DailyMed snapshot (**S18**) carries SPL titles and set ids only, not label text, so it cannot even be used to test whether labels mention it.

The closest thing to a public enumeration is a **count without names**, from the FDA author of the guidance: "The Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) approved the first firm to use parametric release in 1985. In 1996, a second firm submitted an application for parametric release and received approval. Currently, 12 pharmaceutical firms have received approval from CDER for parametric release of one or more drug products" [RS2-S08, 2015]. The same article records the uptake rate since the guidance: "Despite the existence of these guidance documents, only 2 firms have pursued parametric release with a single firm being approved after the publication of these documents" [RS2-S08].

**What that number does and does not support.** It supports the claim that the mechanism is live and rare: on the order of twelve firms in thirty years, with roughly one approval in the five years after the 2010 guidance. It does **not** identify a product, a firm, a site or a therapeutic class, so it cannot be used to determine any longlist presentation's route, and it is eleven years old. Any external use of the figure must carry its date and its trade-article provenance. One caution on searching EPARs for the term. The Valtropin EPAR uses it loosely, for the diluent syringe rather than the drug product, and while still testing: "the filled syringe is terminally sterilised using a validated cycle and procedure. Therefore, the process assurance of sterility is high through “parametric release” in process, together with the standard QC sterility testing at lot release" [RS2-S11]. A term match in an assessment report is not evidence that sterility testing was waived.

## 7. Converting an approved product from aseptic to terminal: the pathway, marked UNCERTAIN throughout

This is a regulatory sketch for a reviewer to correct, not a plan. **Every item is UNCERTAIN pending HA-31.** `method.md` section 4 forbids the screen from outputting convertibility; this section describes the pathway that would follow a conversion decision made elsewhere, and takes no position on whether any product should be converted.

**Change category: prior approval supplement, and this part is binding.** 21 CFR 314.70(b)(2)(iii) lists among major changes "Changes that may affect drug substance or drug product sterility assurance, such as changes in drug substance, drug product, or component sterilization method(s) or an addition, deletion, or substitution of steps in an aseptic processing operation" [RS2-S03]. FDA's changes guidance names this exact conversion under Major Changes: "Changes in the sterilization method (e.g., gas, dry heat, irradiation). These include changes from sterile filtered or aseptic processing to terminal sterilization, or vice versa" [RS2-S02]. That guidance carries a binding footnote: "Insofar as this guidance adjusts reporting categories pursuant to section 506A of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act and 21 CFR 314.70, it does have binding effect" [RS2-S02]. For a generic, 21 CFR 314.97(a) applies the same regime: "The applicant must comply with the requirements of 314.70 and 314.71 regarding the submission of supplemental ANDAs" [RS2-S05].

**Two supplements, not one.** Route conversion and parametric release are separate approvals. The route change is a PAS under 314.70(b)(2)(iii) [RS2-S03]. Parametric release is a second PAS under 314.70, 601.12 or 514.8(b)(2) [F11-S03], and on the reading of RS2-S08 it cannot be filed until commercial batches have been made on the new cycle and released against sterility tests. **UNCERTAIN whether the two could be combined**; nothing read here says either way.

**Evidence burden, assembled from the instruments above.** Formulation and container development showing the product and its container survive the cycle, with the ICH Q3A/Q3B/M7 degradant argument made rather than asserted [F11-S06]. Sterilization cycle validation to a PNSU of 1 in 10^6 with heat distribution and penetration studies and biological indicator work [F11-S02; RS2-S01]. Validated loading patterns and periodic revalidation [F11-S04 8.38]. Container-closure integrity for the new thermal and pressure history [F11-S02; F11-S04 8.22]. New stability data on terminally sterilized batches, because the thermal history changes the degradant profile and therefore the expiry. A new bioburden control programme with limits before the cycle [F11-S04 10.3; F11-S06]. Replacement of the media-fill qualification programme with a cycle qualification programme under gate `G04`. Then, for parametric release, everything in section 4 above.

**A constraint specific to the generic pathway that the family-11 note does not carry.** If conversion needs a formulation change, for example the antioxidant plus chelator approach in the Sun Pharma norepinephrine patent [F11-S32], 21 CFR 314.94(a)(9)(iii) is the constraint the reformulation has to survive. It governs the content of an ANDA rather than a change to an approved one, and **UNCERTAIN whether a post-approval formulation change is judged against it directly or through 314.70**; the text reads: "Generally, a drug product intended for parenteral use must contain the same inactive ingredients and in the same concentration as the reference listed drug ... However, an applicant may seek approval of a drug product that differs from the reference listed drug in preservative, buffer, or antioxidant provided that the applicant identifies and characterizes the differences and provides information demonstrating that the differences do not affect the safety or efficacy of the proposed drug product" [RS2-S04]. An added **antioxidant** sits inside the carve-out. A **chelator** is not named in it. **UNCERTAIN whether FDA would read a chelating agent as an antioxidant for this purpose.** If it would not, a chelator-containing reformulation is outside the ANDA pathway and becomes a 505(b)(2), which changes cost, timeline and exclusivity, and moves architecture A of `../regulatory/approved_generic_cmo_map.md` rather than architecture B. This should be a question for HA-31.

**One asymmetry worth carrying into the network model.** For an aseptically processed product, moving to a new or dissimilar aseptic facility is a major change requiring a PAS [RS2-S02, VI.B.4], and a move to another aseptic facility otherwise is CBE-30 [RS2-S02, VI.C.1.b]. For a terminally sterilized product, transfer "to a newly constructed facility at a different manufacturing site" is a PAS, but "Once this change has been approved, subsequent site changes to the facility for similar drug product types and processes may be submitted as a changes-being-effected-in-30-days supplement", and a transfer to a new building at the **same** site is CBE-30 [RS2-S02, VI.B.5 and VI.C.1.a]. [inference, UNCERTAIN] Read together with section 5, this cuts both ways for a distributed network: the **route** may replicate across sites more cheaply than aseptic processing does, while the **parametric release programme** appears to be per-site. Those are two different assets and the model should not conflate them.

## 8. What this changes, and what it does not

1. **No determination is made and no confidence count changes.** This file is framework evidence. Rows are written in `determinations.md`, which does not yet exist.
2. **The E1/E2 ceiling in `method.md` section 3 survives contact with the compendia.** Nothing found here lets a route be established from a general rule. The terminal-first preference is a regulatory expectation about how the choice **should** be made, not a record of how any particular presentation **was** made. Reasoning from "regulators prefer terminal, this product tolerates heat, therefore it is terminal" remains E6 and remains capped at `weakly_inferred`, and the US half of that reasoning is weaker than the EU half because the US preference is guidance-level (section 1).
3. **The third state is probably mandatory, not merely possible.** `method.md` section 4 already refuses to read "terminally sterilized" as "better". RS2-S08 goes further: production experience released against sterility tests appears to be a precondition of the parametric release filing. If a reviewer confirms it, the model should carry terminal-without-parametric-release as a required first phase with the full 14-day component, an autoclave, and Annex 1's per-sub-batch sterility test, and only then a transition to the 9-day pole.
4. **Two new primary sentences bear on the per-node question** that `prior_art_review.md` section 7 item 1 left open: RS2-S01's "granted for specified sites" and F11-S03's "at the same manufacturing site". Neither settles portability. Both point the same way. This should be added to the HA-31 packet as a specific question with the quotes attached, rather than left as a general uncertainty.
5. **A generic-specific gate the package did not have:** 21 CFR 314.94(a)(9)(iii) may put a chelator-based autoclavable reformulation outside the ANDA pathway. This is a new question for HA-31 and, if answered against the ANDA route, it changes which architecture in `approved_generic_cmo_map.md` a conversion strategy would occupy.
6. **Absence result, and it is a result.** There is no public register of parametric-release-approved products in the US or the EU. The best public figure is twelve firms as of 2015 [RS2-S08]. The screen therefore cannot validate any determination against a list of known parametric releasers, and `determinations.md` must not imply that it can.

**Falsifiers for this file.** A fetched Ph. Eur. 5.1.1 reading differently from RS2-S09's quoted sentence. An FDA statement that an approved parametric release programme extends to a second site or autoclave without its own submission, which would overturn item 4. Any published FDA or EMA list naming products approved for parametric release, which would overturn section 6 and would materially raise the screen's power. An FDA decision or guidance treating a chelating agent as within the 314.94(a)(9)(iii) carve-out, which would settle item 5 in favour of the ANDA route. A transcription of EMA's Figures 1 to 3, which would let section 3 state the branch conditions instead of only the ordering rule.
