## 1. Scope

This family asks whether Telo's capital, demand, and regulatory risk can be carried by a public or collective institution rather than by a venture-funded private manufacturer: BARDA and ASPR procurement and industrial-base programs, the Strategic National Stockpile, state and regional purchasing or manufacturing programs, availability-based government contracts, manufacturing-readiness grants, onshoring programs, hospital cooperative ownership, and nonprofit or public-benefit corporate forms. In the simulation this family does not change chemistry or the sterility-related delay. It changes who pays `capital_usd_per_site` and `fixed_qa_labor_usd_per_site_year`, whether `node_commissioning_days` is realistic, whether a reserved-capacity fee exists at all outside a government contract, and whether a contractable demand channel exists for a new entrant. It also supplies the only externally sourced cost anchors found anywhere in this review for a from-scratch generic sterile plant. The task instruction to keep current program eligibility separate from hypothetical policy design is honored: section 3.2 is a live-eligibility table dated today, section 5 is law and rule, and nothing in this dossier assumes a program Telo cannot enter.

## 3. What already exists

### 3.1 Implemented programs and institutions

**Federal industrial-base investment through BARDA and ASPR IBMSC.** BARDA's Pharmaceutical Countermeasures Infrastructure division runs the MCM Manufacturing Industrial Base Expansion program and states that it has "invested $2B+ in key industry partners to expand domestic biopharmaceutical manufacturing surge capacity" and that its focus spans "from the raw materials needed to create drug products to aseptic fill/finish capacity" (F13-S01). The current acquisition vehicle is the BioMaP-Consortium, an other-transaction consortium managed by Advanced Technology International under an OTA with BARDA, with three domains: industrial base expansion, biomanufacturing capacity expansion and reservation, and advanced biomanufacturing technologies (F13-S01, F13-S14). Membership is free and processed in as little as 3 to 5 business days with final approval up to 3 weeks (F13-S14). ASPR separately runs IBx Connect, a market-research front door whose stated priority areas include "Distributed manufacturing of drug substances and drug products" (F13-S02).

**HHS DPA Title III essential-medicines portfolio.** HHS operates a Defense Production Act Title III program to expand domestic manufacturing of critical health resources (F13-S04). Its published essential-medicines portfolio is small and named: Antheia (oripavine and thebaine), API Innovation Center (albuterol, metoprolol succinate, lorazepam, propofol), and Manus Bio (shikimic acid), plus a medical-sterilization portfolio of Meissner, Steri-Tek, and Sterigenics (F13-S04). Award sizes are on the order of tens of millions: $14 million to APIIC in September 2024 and a further $8.3 million in March 2026, taking total federal investment in APIIC past $22 million (F13-S05); ASPR separately "awarded $9 million to Antheia Inc. to support the expansion of the domestic production of thebaine" in March 2026 (F13-S44). Antheia states this brought its second project agreement to $21 million total; no government record fetched here confirms that cumulative figure [unverified]. The March 2026 APIIC extension is the first use of DPA Title III to fund a finished dosage form rather than an ingredient (F13-S06).

**Point-of-need advanced manufacturing pilots.** DARPA's EQUIP-A-Pharma program, managed with ASPR, funds "up to four pilot agile pharmaceutical manufacturing sites that will generate the data required to inform the creation of a future regulatory framework" (F13-S12). The four 2025 partners are Battelle with Aprecia, BrightPath Laboratories, Rutgers University, and the Mark Cuban Cost Plus Drug Company, covering "eight (8) sterile injectable or oral medications and the active pharmaceutical ingredients for these medicines," with all partners "expected to submit Abbreviated New Drug Applications to the U.S. Food and Drug Administration within a year" (F13-S11). Separately, ASPR and DoD fund On Demand Pharmaceuticals' modular Pharmacy on Demand, deployed to a hospital, as "just one part of more than $1 billion HHS has invested in domestic manufacturing of APIs and FDPs" (F13-S13).

**Regional cluster funding.** The Richmond and Petersburg region holds an EDA Build Back Better Regional Challenge award of $52.9 million (F13-S26), an EDA Advanced Pharmaceutical Manufacturing Tech Hub designation (F13-S25), and as of July 2026 a $15.97 million EDA award for an End-to-End Commercialization Project led by Civica with Phlow and Occam Systems. That project targets exactly the product class Telo studied: "ketamine, midazolam, norepinephrine, and succinylcholine" (F13-S24). This is the single most competitively relevant item in the family. [inference] A publicly funded, already-built consortium is taking the same drug list Telo's beachhead work landed on, including the norepinephrine that Telo's own verification found was not in shortage (R-S5).

**Nonprofit and cooperative manufacturers.** GAO identified seven US nonprofit drug companies as of July 2024 and found that only three had drugs on the market: Civica, Medicines360, and Harm Reduction Therapeutics (F13-S22). Civica is a Delaware nonstock not-for-profit with hospital-system and philanthropic governing members, founded in 2018 (F13-S37, F13-S36; membership economics are covered in family 10, F10-S01 to F10-S04). Its first owned plant in Petersburg, Virginia is 140,000 sq ft with three lines and a stated capacity of more than 200 million units per year, and as of the 2025 fact sheet the site was "pending FDA approval" (F13-S28). The build cost is reported at $140 million: in January 2023 Richmond BizSense described "construction largely complete on the $140 million facility" (F13-S41). Neither F13-S27 nor F13-S28 carries that figure; the citation-audit pass found it had been attributed to them in error. The API Innovation Center is a 501(c)(3) nonprofit and public benefit organization whose funding includes state of Missouri grants plus the ASPR award (F13-S06). Phlow states it is "the first pharmaceutical manufacturer based in America to earn B Corp™ certification" (F13-S43) and holds ASPR contract 75A50120C00092, with $696,665,953.98 obligated and $594,810,032.34 outlaid, running 2020-05-18 to 2027-01-13 (F13-S29).

**Hospital cooperative ownership of a manufacturer.** Premier Inc. and 11 health systems took a minority equity stake in Exela Holdings in 2021, and "The participating health systems signed multi-year commitments to purchase a portion of their pharmaceutical products from Exela through Premier's group purchasing organization" (F13-S23). This is equity plus offtake in one instrument, distinct from Civica's membership model.

**State programs.** California's CalRx is the only state program that has put a state-branded medicine on shelves; the state contracted with Civica rather than building a plant, and the $50 million originally allocated for an in-state facility "was eliminated in Fiscal Year 2025/2026 due to budget savings measures" (F13-S19). California also holds an FDA biologics license in its own name for BabyBIG: FDA's approved-blood-products record for STN 125034 names the "Manufacturer: California Department of Public Health (CDPH)" (F13-S46), and CDPH runs the clinical channel through its Infant Botulism Treatment and Prevention Program (F13-S38). That is the clearest US precedent for a government entity as the regulatory owner of a drug. The earlier version of this dossier also called CDPH the sole producer and sole distributor; no fetched source states that, and the claim is withdrawn. Texas established a Texas Pharmaceutical Initiative in 2023 whose business plan must cover "manufacturing generic drugs and generic biological products," but the enrolled 2023 law contained a sunset ("This chapter expires September 1, 2025", F13-S20) and the 2025 bill extends the program and requires a biennial business plan rather than authorizing a plant (F13-S21). No state has built and licensed a generic sterile-injectable plant.

**Stockpile-adjacent live solicitation.** BioMaP RPP 26-12-VMI, released 2026-08-11 with proposals due 2026-09-14, seeks "vendor managed inventory (VMI) capabilities for active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) used in medical countermeasure finished dose form (FDF) drug products" (F13-S15). Per a consultancy summary that should be verified against the RPP itself, the scope includes conversion of stored API into finished dose within 10 days of notice, distribution to more than 2,000 US health systems, a 3 to 6 month API supply on a 60-month storage horizon, and a requirement that offerors be BioMaP members before award (F13-S16, snippet_only).

## 4. What was tried and failed or discontinued, and the documented reasons

**CIADM (2012 to 2021), the closest federal analogue to publicly funded flexible sterile capacity.** BARDA's own page now reads: "Since 2012 HHS has completed support of activities to retrofit and upgrade the manufacturing capacity at CIADM sites, increasing US-based capacity. HHS is actively engaging with one remaining CIADM partner" (F13-S35). Two of three centers are listed as former partners. Family 10 documents the Emergent termination and the reservation-fee terms in detail (F10-S31, F10-S32); the documented reasons there were sustained operational investment failure and lapsing, non-bankable reserved capacity. The family 13 addition is that BARDA characterizes the program as having "provided important insights on how HHS can better invest in expanding manufacturing capacity" and as having "guided and informed the direction of" BioMaP (F13-S35). The public-private flexible-capacity idea was not abandoned; the vehicle was replaced.

**Fill Finish Manufacturing Network (2013 and 2016).** BARDA established a pre-qualified aseptic fill-finish network, four contractors in 2013 and two more in 2016: "On September 27, 2013, the 4 Fill Finish Manufacturing Network (FFMN) original contracts were awarded to Cook Pharmica (Bloomington, IN), Patheon Pharmaceuticals (Greenville, NC), Par Pharmaceuticals (Rochester, MI) and Nanotherapeutics (Alachua, FL). In September 2016, two additional contracts were awarded" (F13-S35). The earlier version of this dossier stopped at the 2013 award and so understated the vehicle. GAO sizes the first tranche: "In fiscal year 2013, BARDA also awarded approximately $40 million to four companies to establish a network of facilities to provide packaging support to ready the product for distribution, known as Fill Finish Manufacturing Network" (F13-S42). The drug-shortage scope statement that makes this family 13's nearest precedent is GAO's, not BARDA's: "The Fill Finish Manufacturing Network may provide assistance in the fill finish manufacturing of other aseptic drugs and medicines during drug shortages" (F13-S42). That sentence was previously attributed to F13-S35, where it does not appear. GAO also states the contracting form: the initial contract amount funds up-front activities "to establish warm base facilities that can be used to provide fill and finish services during both pandemic and nonpandemic periods" (F13-S42). Status after 2016 is not stated on the current BARDA page. This is a gap, recorded in section 9. [inference] The FFMN is the nearest historical precedent to a Telo virtual-network or warm-standby architecture funded by government, and its outcome should be established before any strategy leans on that mechanism.

**California's state-owned plant.** California legislated an Affordable Drug Manufacturing Act, studied direct manufacturing, and declined. The report's own conclusion: "The State should proceed with leveraging public-private partnerships with drug manufacturers as this approach leads to the best balance of flexibility and control so long as there is detailed planning and strategic collaboration" (F13-S18). The named reasons are capital ($100M to $300M setup, $20M to $50M annual), a technical-expertise gap, regulatory and liability exposure, dependence on external vendors for container-closure systems and filters, and loss of flexibility because "inefficiencies and drug shortages in the pharmaceutical market are constantly changing" (F13-S18). The $50 million earmarked for the in-state facility was then eliminated for budget savings (F13-S19). A state with more capital and more purchasing power than Telo ran this analysis and chose not to own a plant.

**Texas.** The 2023 enabling law expired on its own terms ("This chapter expires September 1, 2025", F13-S20) and the 2025 reauthorization extends the sunset to 2031 while requiring a business plan. A manufacturing-facility authorization section appeared in an intermediate 2023 committee version but is not in the enrolled chapter (F13-S20 as fetched contains no such section). No Texas facility exists.

**Civica's own schedule.** Civica "announced its plans for a smaller-scale Petersburg facility and got construction underway in early 2021", and by January 2023 construction was "largely complete on the $140 million facility" (F13-S41). The plant was still "in the process of testing and regulatory approval" in August 2025 (F13-S27), and the company's own 2025 fact sheet carries the footnote "* Site is pending FDA approval" (F13-S28). That is more than 1,600 days, about four and a half years and counting, from ground to unapproved-but-operational, for an organization with pre-committed hospital demand, $140 million of capital, philanthropic backing, and a leadership team that Civica described in January 2023 as one "that has experience in pharmaceutical manufacturing" (F13-S41). Two statements in the earlier version are corrected here: the occupancy-permit date (late 2022) had no source anywhere in this dossier and is removed, and "roughly five years" overstated an interval that is about four and a half years. This is the single most useful failure datum in the family for calibrating commissioning delay.

**Domestic generic manufacture without a purchase commitment.** USAntibiotics reopened a bankrupt US amoxicillin plant in 2021 and reports it "serves approximately five percent of the U.S. market, even though we have the capacity to satisfy 100 percent of the country's demand" (F13-S31). Its stated cause is the absence of long-term buyers: "Generic manufacturers often operate without secure or long-term purchasing agreements" (F13-S31). Building capacity did not produce demand.

**Federal DPA Title III has barely been used for medicines.** Across fiscal years 2018 to 2024, "DOD made 208 of these investments, while DOE and HHS made 12 and two, respectively" (F13-S07). Two HHS Title III investments in seven years, against a $3.2 billion collective total for the three agencies (F13-S07). The onshoring narrative is loud; the Title III instrument behind it is thin.

**The 2026 tariff lever explicitly does not reach generics.** Proclamation 11020 states "I have further determined not to adjust imports of generic pharmaceuticals and their associated ingredients, including biosimilar products, at this time" and adds that this "includes purchases of generic pharmaceuticals and ingredients for the Strategic API Reserve" (F13-S09). Onshoring agreements under it are open only to companies importing patented products (F13-S10). Telo's entire product class is outside the largest 2026 onshoring instrument.

## 5. Regulatory basis and constraints

- **2 CFR 25.200(b)(1) to (b)(3)** requires every applicant for federal financial assistance without a §25.110 exemption to "Be registered in SAM.gov before submitting an application", to maintain that registration while an application is under consideration, and to include its UEI in each application (F13-S32). This is the rule behind the repo's registration-chain finding (R-S1). It applies to notices of funding opportunity including, by the section's own definition, a "broad agency announcement" (F13-S32).
- **13 CFR 121.702** sets SBIR and STTR size and eligibility: more than 50 percent direct ownership and control by US citizens or permanent residents (or qualifying small businesses), and not more than 500 employees including affiliates (F13-S34, snippet_only for the regulation text itself). NSF restates the operational form: fewer than 500 employees, at least 51 percent US ownership and control, PI more than 50 percent employed by the small business, and "NSF does not fund not-for-profit entities" (F13-S33). UNCERTAIN: whether a nonprofit or public-benefit Telo could be an STTR *research institution* partner rather than the awardee, and what that does to IP ownership, is a question for counsel. The repo's STTR recommendation (R-S1) is consistent with the rule as written but the entity-form interaction has not been reviewed by a qualified person.
- **50 U.S.C. 4552(7)**, the DPA definition of a "domestic source," appeared in unfetched search-result text from BARDA's LVP solicitation (section 9 item 4). The citation-audit pass established that the ASPR Title III page (F13-S04) neither cites the statute nor uses the term "domestic source", so this dossier has no verified source for the gate and the earlier attribution to F13-S04 is withdrawn. [unverified] UNCERTAIN: whether an early-stage company with no operating plant can satisfy a domestic-source finding, and at what maturity, needs a contracts specialist.
- **Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act, 19 U.S.C. 1862**, is the authority for Proclamation 11020 and for the company-specific onshoring agreements delegated to Commerce (F13-S09, F13-S10). Clause 11 of the proclamation exempts generics (F13-S09). UNCERTAIN: whether a later proclamation could extend to generics; the text says "at this time" (F13-S09), which is not a commitment either way.
- **Executive Order 14293 (May 5, 2025)** directs FDA, EPA, and the Army Corps to streamline review and permitting for domestic pharmaceutical manufacturing, including a direction to "expand, as practicable, existing programs that provide early technical advice before a facility is operational" (F13-S08, Sec. 3(b)). The order changes agency process obligations; it does not change 21 CFR 210/211, and it does not shorten any statutory review clock. UNCERTAIN: whether any of the Sec. 3 actions have produced a published guidance change that would materially move a pre-approval inspection or a site-transfer supplement is not established here and should be checked against FDA's guidance agenda before any model parameter moves.
- **26 U.S.C. 501(c)(3) and 501(c)(4)** are the tax forms under which every US nonprofit drug company operates (F13-S22). GAO records an unresolved constraint: "Representatives from five companies expressed concerns about maintaining their tax-exempt status when using revenue from their drug sales to support their operations," and IRS "has not taken a position on whether the sale of lower-priced drugs or the sale of drugs to specific patients, for example, serves a charitable purpose" (F13-S22). UNCERTAIN and material: a nonprofit Telo selling sterile injectables at cost-plus could face unrelated-business-income exposure that no source resolves. This needs tax counsel, not an engineering judgment.
- **State authority.** California's SB 852 (Affordable Drug Manufacturing Act of 2020) is the statute behind CalRx and required the feasibility report (F13-S18); the executed CalRx agreement cites it as "the California Affordable Drug Manufacturing Act of 2020 (Cal. Health & Safety Code sections 127690 to 127696)" (F13-S45). California Health and Safety Code sections 123700 to 123709 are cited as the state-law basis for CDPH's BabyBIG program in the secondary literature (snippet, section 9). Texas Government Code chapter 2177 is the TPI authority, with the expiration clause quoted above (F13-S20). None of these creates any exemption from FDA requirements; a state-owned product still needs a BLA or ANDA, which is exactly what CDPH holds for BabyBIG, where FDA lists CDPH as the manufacturer of record on STN 125034 (F13-S46).

## 6. Adjacent-industry analogues

**Regional innovation clusters as capacity builders (CHIPS-style).** The EDA Tech Hubs program, authorized by the CHIPS and Science Act, designated the Richmond and Petersburg region as the Advanced Pharmaceutical Manufacturing Tech Hub in 2023 (F13-S25), after a $52.9 million Build Back Better Regional Challenge award to the same coalition in 2022 (F13-S26). The mechanism is not a grant to a firm; it is a grant to a consortium with a fiscal lead, which then routes work to member organizations. The 2026 $15.97 million End-to-End Commercialization Project routes key starting materials to Occam, API to Phlow, and finished dose plus regulatory approval to Civica (F13-S24). For Telo the lesson is structural: the money in this channel is available to a role inside an existing coalition, not to a new coalition. [inference] Entering means being a supplier or a technology partner to Civica or Phlow, not competing with them.

**Government as the license holder for an unprofitable essential product.** California's Department of Public Health holds the FDA biologics license for BabyBIG, an orphan biologic for infant botulism; FDA's approved-blood-products record names CDPH as the manufacturer for STN 125034 (F13-S46), and CDPH operates the clinical channel as a public service through the IBTPP (F13-S38). The "sole producer and sole distributor" characterization carried in the earlier version rested on an unfetched secondary article and is withdrawn. This is the only US example found in this review of a government entity owning a drug application outright and running its distribution. It works because the product has a single tiny indication, no commercial market, and a captive clinical channel. [inference] It does not generalize to a multi-product generic sterile portfolio, and no source suggests otherwise.

**Defense industrial base practice imported into health.** GAO's DPA testimony frames Title III as an industrial-base tool with 30 years of DOD practice behind it and recommends FEMA collect lessons learned from DOD for other agencies (F13-S07). USAntibiotics' testimony makes the same import explicitly, asking Congress for multi-year contracts, a Buy American requirement for federally purchased antibiotics, and a CHIPS-style fund (F13-S31). The analogue is coherent but as of 2026 unbuilt for generic injectables; the actual Title III health spend was two investments (F13-S07).

## 8. Implications for the model
