## 1. Scope

Family 12 asks whether Telo should replace ad hoc drug choice with an explicit scoring or matching procedure that maps a product presentation onto a manufacturing and network architecture, using demand level and volatility, shortage recurrence and duration, supplier and API concentration, shelf life, batch size, equipment fit, sterilization route, release-time decomposition, substitution difficulty, price and reimbursement proxies, fixed-cost burden, minimum economic scale, regional demand heterogeneity, common component compatibility, and contractability. In the feasibility model this touches the product parameter block (`config/products/*.yaml`), the protocol product screen (`protocol/protocol.yaml` `product_selection`), the already-scaffolded feature layer (`src/telo_feasibility/product_architecture.py`), and downstream capacity, inventory, release and cost mechanisms that read those parameters. The repository has already made two product choices by two different procedures with two different objectives, and one of them failed verification, so the question is not whether to have a procedure but which criteria a defensible procedure must carry and whether the data to populate them exists. This dossier records what already exists in shortage-risk scoring and criticality listing, what has failed, what the regulatory anchors are, and what each source would let the model replace.

## 3. What already exists

**Algorithmic product-level shortage-risk scoring is a shipped commercial product, not a research idea.** USP operates the Medicine Supply Map and publishes a monthly risk score. The 2024 annual report states that in 2024 the map "identified a high shortage risk for 98% of sterile injectable shortages and accurately predicted 100% of new injectable shortages as high risk prior to their occurrence" (F12-S14, this sentence is quoted here from the same fetched document as the table row). USP has used the scores to publish a ranked Vulnerable Medicines List of 100 drugs built by combining an essentiality composite with the predicted risk score over roughly 2,300 drugs (F12-S13). USP states that the score considers "more than 100 vulnerability factors" including manufacturing complexity, geographic concentration of production, shortage history and price (F12-S35). The 2025 edition changed the selection rule to a top-decile cutoff on a supply-chain vulnerability score, added a binary key-starting-material concentration indicator, and states its scope as 2,511 finished dosage form products, so the denominator moved between editions as well as the rule (F12-S31). Vizient, USP and Angels for Change jointly published the underlying feature analysis in 2021, reporting price, geographic concentration, manufacturing complexity including dosage form and lyophilization, and inspection outcomes as the statistically significant drivers (F12-S15). Any criterion Telo proposes on shortage propensity is therefore in an occupied space with a decade of head start and proprietary volume data behind it (F12-S13, F12-S15, F12-S31).

**Group purchasing organizations hold granted US patents on drug-shortage scoring and ranking.** Premier Healthcare Solutions holds US 12,598,146 B2 on predicting resource shortages from member transaction data using machine learning (F12-S24) and US 12,718,960 B2 on generating a drug shortage score per drug identifier and ranking those scores within a route type, that is, ranking injectables against injectables (F12-S25). Trulla, now assigned to SpendMend, holds US 11,983,666 B2 on pharmaceutical procurement including a neural-network shortage risk score per medication (F12-S26). The route-type ranking claim in F12-S25 is close in shape to the "score injectable presentations and rank them" idea at the centre of this family [inference]. Telo would be scoring products for its own internal architecture choice rather than selling scores, so infringement exposure is likely low, but the commercial novelty of a shortage score as a product is gone [inference].

**Government has already produced the lists that gate money.** FDA published the EO 13944 list of essential medicines, medical countermeasures and critical inputs, scoped to acute-care hospital use (F12-S02). ASPR and ARMI downselected that list against clinical criteria to a smaller acute-care set (F12-S12). CMS then attached a payment to a specific 86-medicine list: small independent hospitals with 100 beds or fewer that are not part of a chain organization can receive separate IPPS payment for voluntarily establishing and maintaining a six-month buffer stock of one or more of those 86 essential medicines, effective for cost reporting periods beginning on or after 2024-10-01 (F12-S08). The EU runs a parallel and more explicit criticality algorithm: two criteria, therapeutic indication and availability of appropriate alternatives, each scored on three risk levels, combined through a risk matrix into critical, at risk, or other (F12-S11).

**Non-profit manufacturers already run a committee-based product selector.** Civica states that its member health systems drive drug selection so that it makes the medications most essential to patient care (F12-S27), and its board chair describes the model as bypassing middlemen, long-term purchase and supply contracts, and cost-plus pricing, with sterile injectables singled out for manufacturing complexity and low margins (F12-S28, F12-S29). This is the closest existing analogue to what family 12 proposes, and the selection input is clinician and pharmacist judgement plus member demand, not an index.

**Published academic product-level shortage models exist and are modest.** Liu and colleagues built a hospital-side model from dosage form, therapeutic class, controlled-substance schedule, orphan status, generic versus branded status, and number of manufacturers (F12-S20). Pall and colleagues predicted a four-class shortage label one month ahead at 69% accuracy and kappa 0.44 using pharmacy sales data alone (F12-S21). Neither is a product-architecture matcher. Both predict shortage, which is only one of the seventeen criteria in this family.

**The economics that would justify a price or reimbursement criterion are established.** Yurukoglu, Liebman and Ridley identified the Medicare Part B reimbursement change as a cause of increased shortages for generic injectables, with the effect concentrated in drugs with high Medicare market share and large reimbursement declines (F12-S22). FDA's own task force reported a median per-unit pre-shortage price of $11.05 for injectables (F12-S01). IQVIA reported 56% of molecules in shortage priced under $1.00 per unit (F12-S16). USP reported that generic injectables not in shortage averaged $118 versus $9 for those in shortage (F12-S14). The direction is unambiguous and is the single best-evidenced criterion in the family.

**Data infrastructure that a criteria set could actually read.** The openFDA shortage fields explorer renders 20 typed field names plus an `openfda` object, followed by a trailing bare `type` entry with no type label, so the field count is 20 or 21 depending on how that trailing entry is treated (F12-S05). The frozen FDA CSV snapshot in this repository carries 22 columns and 1,635 rows on 2026-09-02 (F12-R04). CMS publishes quarterly ASP payment limit files (F12-S09) and an annual Part B spending-by-drug dataset at HCPCS level (F12-S10). ASHP maintains the broader of the two shortage lists, with 227 active shortages in the current June 2026 quarterly report (F12-S17), against 223 in the prior March 2026 quarter still displayed on the ASHP statistics web page (F12-S18) and an all-time high of 323 in Q1 2024 (F12-S17). The web page trails the PDF by one quarter, so the PDF is the current-quarter source.

## 4. What was tried and failed or was discontinued

**FDA was told in 2014 to run routine analyses of its own shortage database and had still not fully implemented that recommendation as of December 2024.** GAO records this and records that FDA's Supply Disruption Predictive Model was still in validation with no set completion time frames (F12-S36; these two facts are in the full report, not on the highlights page carried at F12-S06). The relevant lesson for Telo is not that prediction is impossible but that the regulator holding the richest data has not been able to convert it into an operating selector in ten years [inference].

**The federal demand-volume data source that a demand criterion would want does not work.** GAO reports that the CARES Act directed manufacturers to report the total amount of drug manufactured annually, that FDA's guidance was delayed to final in February 2024, and that as of 2024-09-30 only 44 percent of prescription drug manufacturers and 26 percent of over-the-counter manufacturers had submitted reports, so FDA cannot use the data (F12-S36; full report, not the highlights page). Any Telo criterion depending on national unit volume must therefore fall back to a commercial dataset (IQVIA NSP, used by USP and IQVIA in F12-S13, F12-S14, F12-S16) or to CMS Part B, which covers only the outpatient Medicare slice (F12-S10).

**The HHS coordination mechanism that would have owned a national criticality-to-investment pipeline was terminated.** The Supply Chain Resilience and Shortages Coordinator position ended in May 2025 when its funding expired, leaving HHS without a coordinating mechanism, and HHS initially did not concur with GAO's recommendation on that basis (F12-S06, F12-S36). The two programs proposed to link purchasing to resilience, MRAP and HRSP, require new statutory authority and funding and were still policy concepts in the April 2024 white paper (F12-S07).

**The best public criticality index openly reports the criterion it cannot compute.** USP states that a measure for available medicine alternatives does not exist and that future work should refine the list to account for therapeutic alternatives across the roughly 2,300 drugs analysed (F12-S13). Substitution difficulty, one of the seventeen criteria in this family, is therefore not obtainable from any published US dataset and is handled in the USP process by expert committee review after the model runs (F12-S13). The EU solves it by having member states classify every authorised medicine by hand against a three-level alternatives scale (F12-S11).

**The index's methodology is unstable between editions.** The 2024-2025 list filtered on a shortage risk score above 50% then ranked by an essentiality composite (F12-S13). The 2025 edition reports a two-score factor-analysis architecture with a top-tenth-percentile cutoff on a supply-chain vulnerability score, over a different denominator of 2,511 finished dosage form products (F12-S31). A model that hard-codes a threshold from one edition inherits a moving definition [inference].

**The published risk scores are reported without per-product discrimination evidence.** The 2023 USP annual report states that the Medicine Supply Map identified a severe risk of shortage for 91% of generic sterile injectable shortages in 2023 and points readers to its Appendix A for the per-drug risk scores of new shortages (F12-S30). An earlier draft of this dossier quoted specific low appendix values from a search-index excerpt; those values were not located in the text now fetched and have been removed. Separately, USP's 2024 report states the severe-risk threshold used for injectables is 40% (F12-S14) while the 2024-2025 VML filters at 50% (F12-S13), so the same score means different things in different documents. The 2023 annual report does not state a numeric threshold in the text fetched, so it is not cited for the 50% figure. A single high aggregate hit rate on a population where nearly all injectables score high tells you little about per-product discrimination, and no confusion matrix or base rate is published (F12-S13, F12-S14) [inference].

**Telo's own first product selection failed verification.** The repository's beachhead was norepinephrine, chosen on "chronically short" plus "fastest legal path". Both load-bearing clauses failed: the drug was not on either shortage list, its cheapest vial was $1.47 on the Big4/FCP schedule and $4.52 on FSS, roughly 20 companies held active applications, and the 503B route was blocked precisely because the drug was not in shortage (F12-R02). This is the strongest available in-house evidence that an unstructured product choice is unsafe, and it is exactly the argument for family 12.

**Telo's second product selection optimizes for something else and says so.** The 26-candidate scorecard that picked furosemide states in its scope line that it picks the drug for the flagship paper, not the company's commercial molecule, and its eight weighted criteria are dominated by spectroscopic tractability (weight 2.0), degradation behaviour (1.5) and reference-method quality (1.5), with shortage severity at 1.2 and regulatory at 0.8 (F12-R01). None of the seventeen criteria in family 12 that concern economics or contractability appear in it. Carrying furosemide forward into `config/longlist.yaml` with the role "reserve (repository scorecard pick 2026-07-16)" therefore imports a paper-demo objective into a commercial screen (F12-R01, F12-R04).

## 5. Regulatory basis and constraints

- **Shortage definition.** 21 U.S.C. 356c(h)(2), the definitions subsection of FD&C Act 506C, defines a drug shortage as a period of time when demand or projected demand for the drug within the United States exceeds supply (F12-S34, verbatim). FDA's operational reading is national-level and forecast-inclusive (F12-S04). Any "shortage recurrence and duration" criterion inherits this definition and its national scope, which is the wrong grain for a regional-node thesis [inference].
- **Notification duties that generate the data.** Under FDASIA 2012 and as expanded by the CARES Act, applicants and manufacturers of certain finished prescription drugs and biological products must notify FDA of permanent discontinuance or of an interruption likely to lead to a meaningful disruption in supply, for both drug product and API, generally at least six months in advance, and notifications must include reasons (F12-S04). This is the statutory origin of the CSV's "Reason for Shortage" field.
- **Risk management plans.** CARES Act added FD&C Act section 506C(j), effective 2020-09-23, requiring certain manufacturers to develop, maintain and implement a redundancy risk management plan identifying and evaluating risks to the supply of the drug for each establishment where the drug or its API is manufactured. FDA's May 2022 draft guidance frames RMPs on ICH Q9 quality risk management and lists product-specific characteristics such as dosage form, API source and manufacturing and testing methods as risk factors (F12-S03). The docket number is FDA-2022-D-0277; it does not appear in the guidance PDF itself and is taken from the Federal Register notice of availability published 2022-05-20 at 87 FR 30963 (F12-S33). UNCERTAIN: whether a Telo node operating under a partner's approved application would itself be a "stakeholder" with an independent 506C(j) obligation, or whether it would sit inside the application holder's RMP, needs a qualified regulatory reviewer.
- **Essential medicines listing authority.** EO 13944 (85 FR 49929, 2020-08-06) section 3(c) directs FDA to identify the list; the list is scoped to acute-care hospital need (F12-S02). [inference] Listing confers no manufacturing authorisation and no direct manufacturer payment; no source consulted states this, and it is the researcher's reading of the EO and of the FDA list page (F12-S02).
- **Reimbursement hooks.** CMS finalised separate IPPS payment under section 1886(d)(5)(I) of the Social Security Act, non-budget-neutral, for small independent hospitals maintaining a six-month buffer stock of one or more of 86 essential medicines, for cost reporting periods beginning on or after 2024-10-01 (89 FR 68989, the FY2025 IPPS final rule, as cited on the CMS page), codified at 42 CFR 412.113(g); the hospital may hold the stock directly or through contractual arrangements with a manufacturer, distributor or intermediary; Medicare will not pay for a newly established buffer stock of a medicine that is currently in shortage (F12-S08). UNCERTAIN: whether a Telo-supplied buffer-stock arrangement would qualify as such a "contractual arrangement" for a given hospital's cost report is a reimbursement question for a qualified adviser, and the eligibility limit to hospitals with 100 beds or fewer that are not part of a chain organization sharply bounds the addressable buyer set (F12-S08).
- **Part B pricing as a criterion input.** ASP payment limits are published quarterly, and CMS states it may not publish a payment limit or crosswalk for all drugs reported by manufacturers (F12-S09). The repository already established that norepinephrine has no ASP entry at all because it is bundled into the inpatient DRG and packaged into the OPPS APC (F12-R02). A reimbursement-proxy criterion built on ASP therefore has structural holes for exactly the inpatient-only injectables this thesis targets [inference].
- **503B pathway coupling.** FDCA 503B(a)(5) and 503B(d)(2)(A) make shortage-list presence at the time of compounding, distribution and dispensing the operative exception to the essentially-a-copy bar (F12-R02). This makes shortage status a legal gate, not just a demand signal, for any 503B-flavoured architecture, and it flips sign: a product that leaves the shortage list removes the legal basis (F12-R02).
- **EU criticality method as a template.** EMA/432940/2023 sets criterion 1 (therapeutic indication) and criterion 2 (availability of appropriate alternatives), each with low, medium and high levels, combined in a matrix; criterion 1 classifies all authorised medicines irrespective of marketing status while criterion 2 classifies only marketed ones (F12-S11). UNCERTAIN: the EU method has no US legal effect and cannot be used as a US regulatory justification for a product choice.

## 6. Adjacent-industry analogues

**WHO risk-based product selection for start-up manufacturers in low- and middle-income countries.** WHO QAS-16.682 provides a risk assessment strategy for deciding whether a given essential medicine can be manufactured locally by a manufacturer with limited experience, scoring product categories by potential loss of state of control and concluding that product categories scoring 4 or higher are unlikely candidates, which puts biotechnology products, sterile dosage forms, inhaled products, low-API-content forms and transdermals in the relatively unfavourable band, with liquid non-sterile solutions and immediate-release solid orals named the most feasible candidates (F12-S32, verbatim). This is the only sourced framework found that scores manufacturability rather than shortage propensity, and it scores the exact archetype Telo has chosen as unfavourable for an inexperienced manufacturer. Two limits on its weight: the document is a working draft for comment (QAS/16.682, August 2016, comment deadline 30 September 2016) and nothing fetched here shows it was adopted as final WHO guidance; and it is a risk-ranking heuristic rather than an empirical model.

**EU critical-medicines listing as a jurisdictional analogue.** The EU assembled the first version of the Union list from six national lists (Finland, France, Germany, Portugal, Spain, Sweden) by having member states review 600 active substance groups against the two-criterion matrix (F12-S11 for the method, F12-S37 for the six lists and the 600 count). A substance is listed only if it also meets additional criteria including being critical in more than one third of EU/EEA countries (F12-S38). By the end of 2024 the phased review covered around 2,200 active substance groups and combinations, accounting for 75% of authorised medicines for human use in the EU/EEA (F12-S37). The design lesson is that the EU treats substitution difficulty as a mandatory, manually assessed criterion and treats supply-chain vulnerability as a separate later stage owned by a different body (F12-S11).

**Hospital-side shortage KPI systems. [unverified]** Vizient is reported to have convened a workgroup of hospital shortage experts to define shortage key performance indicators across inventory, labour, patient safety and workforce, including hours spent compounding and repackaging alternatives and the percentage of shortages that altered treatment plans. This was seen only as a search excerpt, no page was fetched, and it is carried under no source id. Do not rely on it. The relevance to family 12 is that the buyer already measures shortage burden in labour hours and treatment alterations, not in shortage days, so a Telo product criterion expressed in shortage days will not map onto the metric the customer reports internally [inference].

## 8. Implications for the model

### 8.1 Mechanisms this family would change

- **Product parameter block.** Today `config/products/*.yaml` holds twenty parameters for two provisional presentations, every one of them evidence tier 5 and validation status illustrative (F12-R04). Family 12 replaces the act of choosing which product config exists, so it changes every downstream mechanism indirectly.
- **Protocol screen.** `protocol/protocol.yaml` `product_selection` currently has eight hard gates (PG1 to PG8) and seven weighted criteria summing to 1.0: clinical_criticality 0.20, shortage_recurrence_duration 0.20, supplier_vulnerability 0.15, manufacturing_fit 0.15, public_data_strength 0.10, regional_relevance 0.10, demand_contract_fit 0.10 (F12-R04). Seven of the seventeen family-12 criteria have no home in that weight vector: sterilization route, release-time decomposition, price and reimbursement proxies, fixed-cost burden, minimum economic scale, common component compatibility, and shelf life. Changing the weight vector is a protocol amendment and requires a version bump and a revision-log entry under the protocol's own rule.
- **Capacity and commissioning.** A criterion on batch size and minimum economic scale changes `units_per_batch`, `batches_per_site_year_nominal` and the derived utilization, which is where the fixed-cost failure lives.
- **Common-cause groups.** A criterion on supplier and API concentration is the direct input to the disruption model's common-cause grouping, and the KSM finding in F12-S31 says that finished-dose supplier counts overstate independence.
- **Release components.** A criterion on sterilization route and release-time decomposition changes `release_time_days` and its decomposition, which the Phase A result says is binding through the 14-day sterility component.
- **Inventory policy.** Shelf life and demand volatility drive `shelf_life_months`, `target_safety_stock_days` and `expiry_scrap_fraction`.
- **Contract terms.** Contractability and reimbursement proxies drive whether a take-or-pay or buffer-stock revenue mechanism exists at all, and the CMS rule in F12-S08 bounds who can pay for one.
- **Cost structure.** Price and reimbursement proxies and fixed-cost burden drive the break-even comparison that currently fails.

### 8.3 Findings about the repository that this family exposes

1. **The FDA shortage CSV cannot carry a reason-based criterion.** In the frozen 2026-09-02 snapshot, 63.4% of the 1,176 rows with Status "Current" have an empty `Reason for Shortage`, and across all 1,635 rows the blank rate is 73.6% (F12-R04). Of the populated values, "Other" is the largest category at 145 rows. A statutory seven-value taxonomy does exist: FDA states that under FD&C Act section 506E and its regulations the reasons that may be cited are GMP compliance requirements, regulatory delay, shortage of an active ingredient, shortage of an inactive ingredient component, discontinuation of manufacture, delay in shipping, and demand increase (F12-S04), and the six populated non-"Other" values in the frozen snapshot map onto that list. What is missing is adherence and adjudication, not a taxonomy: coverage is at most one-third of Current rows and the largest populated value is the residual. GAO records that the "other" category is used when the reason cannot be categorised into a statutory reason, and that "other" reasons contributed to 84 percent (92 of 109) of shortages in 2019 and 14 percent (14 of 102) in 2024 (F12-S36). This also matches the AMA J Ethics point that FDA and ASHP define shortage differently (F12-S19).
2. **Row counts are not product counts.** The 1,176 Current rows collapse to 70 distinct generic names, of which about 50 have an injectable indication in the generic name or presentation string (F12-R04). The repository's statement that 51 injectables were in current shortage lives in the drug-selection scorecard and the market research file, not in the beachhead verification (F12-R01, F12-R03), and is close to that. A screen must still state which grain it uses, because presentation-level counts run about seventeen times product-level counts in this snapshot (1,176 / 70 = 16.8).
3. **The 44%-under-$5 statistic is verified and should be kept, with its scope stated.** The USP 2024 Annual Drug Shortages Report states verbatim: "In total, 74% of sterile injectables in shortage have a price point below $15 per unit, with 44% less than $5 per unit." (F12-S14). The denominator is stated in the same report as n=61 injectable drug products in shortage, and the price basis is per unit, not per vial. The 2023 report corroborates the direction with "over half of sterile injectables in shortage were under $5" (F12-S30). So the number is defensible; what is not defensible is `market-research.md`'s current wording. That file carries the claim at line 35 as "44% of injectables in shortage sell for under $5 a vial" tagged [V], contradicts itself at line 37 by recording the same figure as [U] on re-check, and lists it at line 582 as needing the FDA shortage list joined to pricing. That join is unnecessary: USP already published the statistic. The repo-facing correction is in section 10.
4. **The two existing product selections cannot both be right.** The scorecard's objective is a spectroscopy paper, stated explicitly in its own scope line (F12-R01). The commercial screen's objective is network economics. Carrying furosemide into the longlist as the "repository scorecard pick" without re-scoring it against economic criteria is a category error that the design-space work should correct (F12-R01, F12-R04).
5. **The protocol weight vector understates the criteria that bind.** Phase A reports that fixed cost, capacity and the sterility release component are what fail. None of those three has a weight in `product_selection.weights` (F12-R04).
