## 1. Scope

This family asks who signs a contract that makes a sterile-injectable plant or reserve worth building, and on what terms. It covers demand-side instruments only: take-or-pay and minimum-volume commitments, capacity subscriptions and reservation fees, resilience premiums, hospital consortium and GPO-backed committed contracting, government advance purchase and stockpile contracts, availability payments, capacity-option contracts, and outcome-based reliability agreements. In Telo's simulation this family changes contract terms, the demand and revenue side, inventory policy, allocation rights, and cost structure; it does not change release time, sterility assurance, or any regulatory gate. The governing test is the assignment's own: a technically feasible plant without contractable demand is not feasible, so the question here is what has actually been signed, at what price, by whom, and how often the commitment held.

## 3. What already exists

### 3.1 Hospital consortium take-or-pay (Civica)

Civica is the only U.S. instance found of an explicit take-or-pay contract for generic sterile injectables. Members commit, drug by drug, to about 50 percent of expected volume for a minimum of five years (F10-S01). ASPE gives the range as three to 10 years and confirms the 50 percent figure and a six-month buffer stock (F10-S02). The NEJM Catalyst commentary calls it "a specifically designed take-or-pay purchasing agreement referred to as a minimum viable volume (MVV) contract" (F10-S01). Read it with the conflict of interest in view. The authors are Carter Dredge, "Lead Futurist, SSM Health, Saint Louis, Missouri, USA", and Stefan Scholtes of "Judge Business School, Cambridge University, United Kingdom", and the disclosure reads "Carter Dredge serves on the Board of Civica Rx and receives no financial compensation for doing so" (F10-S01, fetched text). Neither author holds a Civica officer role; the account is by a board member, not by management. Pricing is the same for every member regardless of size and is described in 2026 as cost-plus (F10-S03).

Scale as of 2026: 60 members, more than 70 essential medicines, more than 1,400 hospitals (F10-S03). Measured market share is small. ASPE's IQVIA analysis found 64 products, 350.2 million units and $255.9 million of sales over 2020 to 2022 (F10-S02); Duke-Margolis states Civica is "well under 10 percent of generic sterile injectable drug sales" (F10-S15).

Two performance numbers matter for the model. ASPE reports the published finding that Civica fulfilled its contractually guaranteed volume at 96 percent against 86 percent for wholesaler orders, and states the caveat that the samples differ (F10-S02). Separately, ASPE found that "the average price of drugs sold under the non-traditional model was higher than the average price of the same drugs sold by the traditional model," with the relative price up to 2.15 times higher for certain generics (F10-S02, fetched text). A take-or-pay contract for reliability is therefore not, in the measured data, cheaper.

The mechanism Civica's own account credits for its speed is that the MVV let it contract existing CMOs holding dormant ANDAs rather than build (F10-S01). The contract, not the factory, was the product for the first several years.

### 3.2 GPO-backed committed contracting (three programs, all live)

- **Vizient Novaplus Enhanced Supply**. Vizient's own accounts of the launch date disagree by a year: a 2022 news release says "In 2019, Vizient launched its Novaplus Enhanced Supply (NES) Program in which contracted suppliers provide additional inventory of essential medications to mitigate disruptions and demand surge" (F10-S47), while a 2026 Vizient blog says "In January 2020, alongside the distribution of the first Essential Medications List, we launched Novaplus Enhanced Supply (NES)" (F10-S48). Contracted suppliers hold additional inventory of essential products, sized on aggregated member historical purchases and warehoused in the U.S. (F10-S04). The NES Reserve extension generates "up to 6 months of additional dedicated supply of essential medications that would not otherwise exist" (F10-S05), currently more than 180 NDCs and, per Vizient's own FAQ, over 131 million units held and over $538 million of inventory cost avoidance for members (F10-S05, fetched text). Critically for the payer question: "Is there an up-front investment or initial program fee to join NES Reserve? No." (F10-S05, fetched text). Higher rebates are earned at 90 percent compliance, and the program "Elevates manufacturer accountability through expanded failure-to-supply and quality standards" (F10-S05, fetched text).
- **Premier ProvideGx**. Long-term committed buying contracts giving manufacturers "a steady demand signal and guaranteed buyer base in exchange for increasing production of generics in shortage, maintaining safety stocks or diversifying supply sources" (F10-S06), with "minimum requirements on manufacturers to retain an average of three to six months of active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) and finished dose products" (F10-S07). More than 150 shortage or recent-shortage drugs; 15 shortages delisted (F10-S06).
- **HealthTrust SIMS** (launched 2019). Five-year contracts instead of the usual three (F10-S08); dedicated reserves "oftentimes up to 90-120 days' worth" available only to HealthTrust members; a unique member-only NDC so the safety stock can be allocated directly rather than through wholesaler historical-allocation rules (F10-S08). SIMS requires either multiple API sources or vertical integration, and geographically distinct redundant plants (F10-S08). Compliance is the differentiator HealthTrust claims: "Unlike other shortage programs that typically achieve only 30-50% contract compliance, SIMS has demonstrated 80-85% compliance among participating members" (F10-S09). The program grew from five drugs at launch to over 75 as of mid-2025, with 20 more expected in 2025 (F10-S09; the source is dated 2025-06-02, so "over 75" is a mid-2025 count, not an end-2024 one). On price, HealthTrust's own vice president of pharmacy says "There's a premium, sometimes, but that premium is generally minimum and always has a clear value proposition" (F10-S09, fetched text).

### 3.3 Government availability payments and capacity options (defense, not health)

DLA's Warstopper program is the closest working government analogue to a capacity subscription. Its stated design principle is explicit: "The preference is to buy guaranteed access to material rather than to purchase or own material outright" (F10-S24). The vehicles are Prime Vendor War Readiness Materiel, vendor-managed inventory contracts with distributors, and Corporate Exigency Contracts with manufacturers, all described as long-term partnerships providing guaranteed product availability, with the vendor rotating stock (F10-S24). GAO records that DLA officials describe the program as operating "like an insurance policy for the military services" (F10-S25). The acquisition rule (DLAD 17.93) allows the surge requirement to be met "through access to production capability as well as contractor-owned or contractor-managed inventory or safety stocks" (F10-S26, fetched text) - that is, capacity or inventory, at the offeror's option, which is exactly the inventory-capacity trade Telo's model is trying to price.

### 3.4 Government advance purchase (medical countermeasures only)

Project BioShield's procurement authority is codified at 42 U.S.C. 247d-6b(c)(7). The contract terms are specified in statute: no payment until delivery of an acceptable portion, with an advance of at most 10 percent of contract value where the Secretary determines it is necessary, repayable on failure to perform, plus optional 5 percent milestone payments capped at 50 percent of contract value (F10-S29). This authority is scoped to security countermeasures for CBRN and emerging infectious disease threats, not routine generic shortages. Telo's own funding file records that the BARDA BioMaP route is currently a consortium to join rather than a source of money for a pre-revenue company (F10-S41).

### 3.5 Payer payment for buffer inventory (small and live)

CMS finalized a separate IPPS payment in the FY2025 rule (89 FR 68989) for hospitals that voluntarily hold a six-month buffer stock of one or more of 86 essential medicines, effective for cost reporting periods beginning on or after 2024-10-01. Eligibility is narrow: "Small, independent hospitals, that is hospitals with 100 beds or fewer that are not part of a chain organization, are eligible for these payments" (F10-S13). The buffer may be held "directly, or through contractual arrangements with a pharmaceutical manufacturer, distributor or intermediary" (F10-S13, fetched text), which is the only U.S. payer mechanism found that would pay a third party to hold stock on a hospital's behalf. Medicare will not pay for a newly established buffer of a medicine already in shortage (F10-S13).

### 3.6 Delinked availability payment in pharmaceuticals (UK, novel antimicrobials)

NHS England runs the only implemented pharmaceutical subscription contract found. It pays "a fixed annual fee for antimicrobials based primarily on a health technology assessment of their value to the NHS, as opposed to the volumes used" (F10-S19, fetched text), and the commissioning route is for products "where payments are delinked from volumes used and contractual requirements guarantee surety of supply" (F10-S19). Contracts run an initial three years with extensions to a maximum of 15 years, in four value bands (F10-S19, fetched text). The 2024 tender was advertised on Find a Tender with contracts commencing 2026-04-01 (F10-S20, snippet only). The important structural feature for this family is that the fee is decoupled from volume and the model contract "will include key performance indicators to support surety of supply and will be published as part of the procurement documentation" (F10-S19, fetched text). Two corrections to an earlier draft of this dossier. First, it asserted that the paid amount is reduced by the value of product the NHS actually buys; the guidance page contains no netting, deduction or offset mechanism, and that assertion is withdrawn. Second, the page states only that KPIs exist, not that the fee is adjusted against them, so "performance-adjusted" is [inference] and unverified.

### 3.7 Collaborative and resilience-weighted public procurement (EU)

Regulation (EU) 2022/2371 provides a reinforced joint procurement system open to Member States, EFTA states, EU candidate countries and four microstates (F10-S22). The Critical Medicines Act, proposed March 2025, adds "Public procurement to incentivise the resilience of supply chains of critical medicines" and Commission-supported collaborative procurement among Member States (F10-S23).

### 3.8 Standards infrastructure that reliability contracts would reference

HIRC operates a Resiliency Badge, "awarded to suppliers that demonstrate measurable resiliency and transparency in critical supply-chain practices" (F10-S35). Duke-Margolis names candidate programs of this kind in its June 2024 comment on the Senate Finance draft: "Some examples of potential model initiatives that could be considered include the Healthcare Industry Resilience Collaborative's Resiliency Badging Program, the International Society for Pharmaceutical Engineering's Advancing Pharmaceutical Quality Program, the US Pharmacopeia's Medicines Supply Map and vulnerability scores, and others" (F10-S45; the sentence spans a page break in the PDF). An earlier draft of this dossier attributed to F10-S15 and F10-S16 a different grouping (HIRC badge, a USP "Resiliency Benchmark", FDA Quality Management Maturity); neither document contains that grouping and it is withdrawn. Without such a rating there is nothing for an outcome-based contract to pay against.

### 3.9 Proposed but not enacted: statutory reliability contracts

The Senate Finance Committee's May 2024 discussion draft is the most detailed contract design available anywhere. It would create a Medicare Drug Shortage Prevention and Mitigation Program under a new SSA section 1899C, paying providers quarterly lump sums for committed volume. Its core standards are specific and directly usable as model parameters: contracts of at least two years (2027-2028) then at least three years; a prohibition on off-contract purchases that reduce committed volume, with a narrow supply-failure exception (F10-S11); no rebates, discounts, price concessions or fees reducing the agreed price, except bona fide service fees and late-delivery penalties; permitted upward price adjustment on natural disaster or severe disruption not caused by the supplier's own inspection failure; uniform on-time delivery standards with quarterly reporting; and a separately contracted secondary supplier with API and component sources distinct from the primary supplier's (F10-S11, fetched text). Minimum committed volume rises from 40 percent (2027-2028) to 60 percent (2029-2031) to 75 percent (2032 onward) of the provider's total standard inventory (F10-S11, fetched text). Manufacturers must sign "Manufacturer Reliability Agreements" with each program participant (F10-S11; F10-S18). Advanced standards add two percentage points to the incentive for an advanced manufacturing technology designated under FD&C Act section 560L, and two for domestic finished-dose manufacture with OECD-sourced API (F10-S11, fetched text). Buffer standards from 2029 are a six-month enhanced buffer for large providers, held directly or "through procurement and storage by a third-party entity acting on behalf of such provider, with such buffer supply contractually committed to such provider" (F10-S11, fetched text).

HHS's 2024 white paper proposes the same architecture from the payer side: a Manufacturer Resiliency Assessment Program scoring manufacturers, and a Hospital Resilient Supply Program tying Medicare payments or penalties to a hospital scorecard that counts failure-to-supply clauses, minimum purchasing volume requirements and long-term contracts (F10-S10). HHS's own ten-year cost estimate is $3.26 billion to $5.11 billion, of which about $750 million for MRAP and $2.51 to $4.36 billion for HRSP (F10-S10, fetched text).

## 4. What was tried and failed or discontinued

**The buffer-stock payment was proposed broadly and adopted narrowly.** CMS solicited comment in the CY2024 OPPS rule on separate IPPS and possibly OPPS payment for essential-medicine buffer stock (88 FR 82127-30), and HHS records that "CMS did not adopt a policy regarding separate payment under the IPPS or OPPS for establishing and maintaining access to a buffer stock of essential medicines" at that time (F10-S10, fetched text). The version finalized for FY2025 is limited to hospitals of 100 beds or fewer that are not part of a chain (F10-S13). Duke-Margolis states that "Current policy is limited to ~500 small and independent hospitals (representing <10% of hospitals and <5% of hospital pharmaceutical usage)" (F10-S16). The burden the deck attributes to the buffer-stock policy is reporting, not dual purchasing: "The current buffer stock policy requires hospitals to separately track and report costs on a supplemental cost report. This is burdensome, misses the most significant types of costs, and incentivizes higher costs" (F10-S16, fetched text). An earlier draft of this dossier attached to the buffer-stock policy a sentence about an administrative burden that "has significantly limited uptake"; in the deck that sentence belongs to the separate domestic-N95 payment adjustment, and the misattribution is corrected here.

**Legislative payment reform has stalled.** The Senate Finance draft (F10-S11) drew detailed comment from AHA, HDA, USP and Duke-Margolis in mid-2024 and, on the evidence collected here, was not enacted. At the National Academies workshop convened October 22-23, 2025 (proceedings published 2026), Marta E. Wosińska stated that "recent legislative proposals targeting payment and economic issues have stalled or remain too limited in scope to meaningfully change supply dynamics" (F10-S17, fetched text). I could not confirm from congress.gov whether the draft was ever introduced as a numbered bill (section 9).

**The objections that were raised against a mandated committed-contract program are specific and unresolved.** AHA warned that a 340B-eligible provider could not access 340B pricing for units committed under the program, "undermin[ing] the very purpose of the 340B program," and that waiving the Medicaid inflation rebate would raise prices (F10-S18, fetched text). AHA also objected to holding hospitals accountable for manufacturer failures and for advanced-manufacturing adoption they do not control, and warned that a sudden buffer build of controlled substances could collide with DEA manufacturer quotas (F10-S18, fetched text). HDA argued the volume requirements and off-contract purchasing limits "may prevent new manufacturers from entering the market" (F10-S36, snippet only). Duke-Margolis recommended a maximum committed volume percentage, "perhaps 75%", precisely to keep part of the market open to new entrants: "By helping to ensure that a portion of the market remains uncommitted and thus more accessible to non-participating manufacturers and new manufacturer entrants, this cap would help to reduce manufacturer barriers to entry and reduce market exits" (F10-S45). An earlier draft of this dossier sourced this to F10-S16, which does not contain it. Note also that the Duke ceiling and the SFC draft's own 75 percent minimum from 2032 point in opposite directions: one caps what may be committed, the other is a floor on the provider. Conflating them inverts the meaning. [inference] A committed-contract regime designed to reward incumbency reduces, not increases, the contractable demand available to a new entrant like Telo.

**Contract commitments erode in practice.** HHS records that "the erosion of failure-to-supply clauses in GPO contracts may be associated with drug shortages," and that low-price clauses "allow middlemen to unilaterally walk away from a contract if they find a lower price from another manufacturer" (F10-S10, fetched text). HealthTrust's own comparison implies most shortage programs run at 30-50 percent contract compliance (F10-S09). Duke-Margolis reports under 15 percent of HealthTrust members' generic injectable purchases went through SIMS from July 2022 to June 2023, and that "most hospitals do not participate in such programs or only purchase a small amount of volume through them" (F10-S15, fetched text).

**Take-or-pay demand did not, by itself, produce on-time capacity.** Civica received an occupancy permit for its Petersburg plant in late 2022 (F10-S37, snippet only). As of May 2025, "While the Petersburg plant is operational, it hasn't yet begun to manufacture commercial drugs," with market entry slipped from 2025 to 2026 (F10-S34). Civica's own 2025 manufacturing and grant materials describe the site as pending FDA approval, with a facility prior approval inspection expected in 2026 (F10-S43, F10-S44, both snippet only). The associated Chesterfield laboratory was cut by 8,000 square feet after cost projections came in higher than estimated, and the expected federal grant had not been received (F10-S34). This is the most directly relevant negative datum in the family: the strongest take-or-pay book in the industry did not compress the interval between building completion and first commercial product.

**Reserved government capacity that is not exercised decays.** The CIADM precedent is documented in the family 8 dossier and is not re-derived here. No source for it is carried in this dossier's table, so the claim is [unverified] from within this document and should be checked against family 8. If the lesson recorded there holds (readiness is a function of throughput, not of contract), it would apply to every capacity-subscription design in this family [inference].

**Advance market commitments are not regarded as sufficient.** At the National Academies workshop, Hilary Marston "cautioned that incremental fixes are unlikely to succeed where fundamental market failures persist and noted limitations in existing pull incentives such as advance market commitments" (F10-S17, fetched text).

**The antitrust guidance that consortium purchasing relied on was withdrawn.** DOJ rescinded the 1996 Statements of Antitrust Enforcement Policy in Health Care in February 2023 and the FTC withdrew them on 2023-07-14, on the ground that they "no longer serve their intended purpose of providing accurate guidance to market participants" (F10-S30). Statement 7 of that document is the joint purchasing statement: "This statement sets forth an antitrust safety zone that describes joint purchasing arrangements among health care providers that will not be challenged, absent extraordinary circumstances, by the Agencies under the antitrust laws" (F10-S46). An earlier draft of this dossier let that fact ride on the FTC press release (F10-S30), which does not mention Statement 7 or joint purchasing. [inference] Withdrawal removes guidance; it does not change the underlying law. No source in this table states that, so it is unverified and belongs with the counsel question below. UNCERTAIN: whether a new hospital purchasing consortium now faces materially greater antitrust risk requires antitrust counsel, not this dossier.

## 5. Regulatory basis and constraints

Nothing in this family touches an FDA release requirement. A contract cannot shorten a sterility test, so the 14-day sterility-related delay that binds Telo's runs is untouched by every mechanism here [inference, consistent with F10-S40 and the release-constraints file].

- **42 CFR 1001.952(j)** (anti-kickback safe harbor for group purchasing organizations). Protection requires a written agreement with each participant stating either that vendors will pay the GPO a fee of "3 percent or less of the purchase price," or the specified maximum amount, plus annual written disclosure to provider entities of amounts received from each vendor (F10-S28). Relevance: any Telo-adjacent structure that routes manufacturer payments through a purchasing intermediary must fit this safe harbor or be analyzed outside it. UNCERTAIN: whether a hospital consortium paying a manufacturer an above-market resilience premium implicates section 1128B at all (it is consideration for goods, not for referrals). Needs health-care regulatory counsel.
- **FAR 16.504(a)(1) and (a)(2)** (indefinite-quantity contracts). The government's own take-or-pay analogue: the contract "must require the Government to order and the contractor to furnish at least a stated minimum quantity," and "the minimum quantity must be more than a nominal quantity, but it should not exceed the amount that the Government is fairly certain to order" (F10-S27). This is the binding-consideration test any federal capacity subscription must satisfy. UNCERTAIN: whether an availability payment untied to any ordered quantity is procurable under FAR Part 16 at all, or would require a different instrument (other transaction authority, DPA Title III). Needs government-contracts counsel.
- **42 U.S.C. 247d-6b(c)(7)(B)(ii)(I)** (Project BioShield procurement). Payment conditioned on delivery; advance payment capped at 10 percent of contract value and repayable on failure to perform; milestone advances of 5 percent each capped at 50 percent of contract value (F10-S29). Relevance: the federal advance-purchase instrument that exists will not prefund a plant beyond these caps.
- **DLAD 17.93** (surge and sustainment). Surge coverage may be met "through access to production capability as well as contractor-owned or contractor-managed inventory or safety stocks" (F10-S26). Offerors must submit a capability assessment plan and, where government investment is recommended, a refresh plan and an exit strategy (F10-S26, fetched text).
- **Regulation (EU) 2022/2371 Article 12** (joint procurement of medical countermeasures), preceded by the 2014 Joint Procurement Agreement. Participation is open to Member States, EFTA states, candidate countries and four microstates (F10-S22). Recital 19 provides for an exclusivity clause under which participating countries commit not to procure the countermeasure through other channels or run parallel negotiations (search excerpt of the EUR-Lex full text; not fetched verbatim, so treated as snippet only).
- **Medicare payment structure** is the binding economic constraint, not an FDA one. Generic sterile injectables are paid inside IPPS DRG bundles and Part B ASP, so a hospital that pays more for a reliable supplier is not reimbursed more (F10-S12; F10-S15; F10-S17). The FY2025 IPPS buffer payment (89 FR 68989) is the only carve-out and reaches only hospitals of 100 beds or fewer not in a chain (F10-S13).
- **340B and the Medicaid inflation rebate** limit the manufacturer's ability to charge, and the hospital's ability to pay, a resilience premium (F10-S18; F10-S17 workshop statements). UNCERTAIN in detail; needs 340B and MDRP counsel.
- **DEA production quotas** would constrain a coordinated buffer build for controlled substances (F10-S18).
- **Withdrawn FTC/DOJ health care antitrust statements (2023)** remove the stated joint-purchasing safety zone (F10-S30). UNCERTAIN as above.

## 6. Adjacent-industry analogues

**Availability-payment P3 concessions (surface transportation).** FHWA describes availability payments as "regularly scheduled payments" that the concessionaire earns by making the facility available at prescribed levels of service, with compensation "based on performance, as measured by delivery date, incident response, state of good repair" and the sanction that "If standards are not met, payments can be reduced or eliminated" (F10-S21). The public sponsor takes long-term payment obligation and removes revenue risk from the private party. This is the structural template for a sterile-injectable capacity contract in which Telo would be paid for readiness and penalized for unavailability rather than paid per vial. The transferable design elements are the service-level definition, the deduction schedule, and the concession term (typically 30 years or longer), which is far longer than any pharmaceutical supply contract found here.

**Capacity options in capital-intensive B2B markets (power, chemicals, semiconductors).** Wu and Kleindorfer formalize the contract as a two-part price: "Contract pricing involves both a reservation fee per unit of capacity and an execution fee per unit of output if capacity is called" (F10-S32). The buyer holds a portfolio of reserved capacity and spot purchases; sellers bid capacity at or below their true capacity because they face stringent nonperformance penalties, and the contract price is below the expected spot price (F10-S32, fetched text). Barnes-Schuster, Bassok and Anupindi show that options coordinate a two-period channel only if the exercise price can be piecewise linear, and that supplier returns credit does not always restore individual rationality (F10-S31). The transferable point is negative as well as positive: a naively priced capacity option can leave the supplier worse off than not contracting, which is exactly the failure mode a reserved-capacity strategy in Telo's model must be tested against.

**Defense industrial-base surge contracting.** DLA buys guaranteed access rather than ownership, using vendor-managed inventory with distributors and Corporate Exigency Contracts with manufacturers, requiring the vendor to rotate stock so the reserve does not expire (F10-S24). The design assumes a commercial channel large enough to absorb rotated stock, and manufacturers said as much when DLA studied expanding it: "manufacturers interviewed as part of the assessment expressed concern and, in some cases, reluctance to participate in a program that added production and storage requirements" (F10-S25, fetched text). GAO found that "The Defense Logistics Agency (DLA) does not have clearly defined or outcome-oriented goals or effective performance measures for its Warstopper Program" (F10-S25, fetched text), so its return is not demonstrated, three decades after Congress directed the program in the conference report accompanying the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Years 1992 and 1993 following Operation Desert Storm (F10-S25, fetched text).

**Delinked antimicrobial subscription (UK NHS).** A fixed annual fee, banded by an HTA-assessed value score, delinked from volumes used, under a model contract that "will include key performance indicators to support surety of supply" (F10-S19). The guidance page describes no netting of the fee against product purchased and no stated adjustment of the fee against the KPIs; see section 3.6. This is the only pharmaceutical availability payment found anywhere. It exists because the product is patented, single-source and deliberately under-used; the mapping to a multi-source generic injectable is not obvious [inference].

## 8. Implications for the model

This family changes contract terms, the demand and revenue side, inventory policy, allocation policy, cost structure and (weakly) commissioning delay. It does not change release components, sterility assurance, or any regulatory gate.

**The largest structural gap.** Telo's strategy configuration has `reserved_capacity_fraction` per design and a global `reserved_capacity_fee_fraction`, `reserved_capacity_activation_days`, and `safety_stock_days`, but it has **no representation of committed demand at all**: no committed-volume fraction, no contract term, no take-or-pay penalty, no compliance or leakage rate, no allocation priority, no failure-to-supply penalty. Every strategy therefore implicitly assumes demand is fully available to the network. The repository already flags a related defect: `reserved_capacity_fraction` "is charged as a fraction of variable cost in the workbook, not as reserved capacity (WB-21)" (F10-S42). The capacity-option literature gives the correct two-part structure to fix it: a reservation fee per unit of reserved capacity plus an execution fee per unit actually called (F10-S32).

**Answers to the mechanism questions the assignment asks for each instrument.** Payer, purchaser and beneficiary are the same party only in the Civica model (member health systems fund, buy and benefit). In GPO committed contracting the purchaser is the hospital, the beneficiary is the hospital, and there is no separate payer: NES Reserve charges no up-front fee (F10-S05), so the supplier funds the buffer and recovers it in unit price or foregone alternative sales. In CMS buffer payment, Medicare is the payer, the hospital the purchaser, and eligibility is 100 beds or fewer (F10-S13). In the SFC draft, Medicare is the payer, the provider the purchaser, and the manufacturer the indirect beneficiary. In Warstopper, DLA is payer and purchaser and the warfighter the beneficiary. Committed volume, activation condition, allocation rights and default risk are documented above per program; **price required is documented nowhere** except NHS England's four bands (5, 10, 15 and 20 million pounds per year for England), which are for patented antimicrobials.

**The blunt implication for Telo.** Every committed-contract instrument in this family is currently occupied by an incumbent aggregator (Civica, Vizient, Premier, HealthTrust) that contracts with manufacturers who already hold approvals and capacity. None of them contract with a pre-approval manufacturer. The contract is available to Telo only after Telo has an approved product and a plant, which is the opposite of the sequence that would de-risk building one [inference, from F10-S01 (Civica contracted CMOs holding ANDAs), F10-S06 and F10-S08 (supplier vetting requires existing redundancy, fill-rate history and API diversity), and F10-S39].

Vendor reluctance: "manufacturers interviewed as part of the assessment expressed concern and, in some cases, reluctance to participate in a program that added production and storage requirements", and "some vendors expressed concern that if the medications were not ordered on a Warstopper contract, they would not be able to sell the increased volume of medications to other customers before the medications expire" (F10-S25, fetched text). Cost: "DLA determined it would cost approximately $12.7 million to maintain a 6-month supply of the 30 medications for a 5-year period, including initial investment costs" (F10-S25, fetched text). The five-year period and the inclusion of initial investment are load-bearing; an earlier draft of this dossier omitted both, which overstates the annualised cost by a factor of five.
4. **No contract text is public for any of the four private programs.** Civica's MVV, Vizient NES/Reserve, Premier ProvideGx and HealthTrust SIMS terms are described only in the counterparties' own marketing and press materials. The take-or-pay penalty magnitude, the failure-to-supply penalty, and the price premium are all undisclosed. Everything in section 8 derived from these sources is therefore self-reported.
5. **No price anywhere for a resilience premium.** The 5-15 percent figure (F10-S17) is a workshop attribution to an ASHP staff member and is not sourced to a published survey with a method or a sample. It must not enter the model as anything but Tier 5.
6. **No confirmed capacity-option contract in pharmaceuticals.** I found the theory (F10-S31, F10-S32) and government analogues (F10-S24, F10-S26) but no disclosed pharmaceutical contract with a reservation fee and a separate execution fee. If one exists it is most likely inside a BARDA or CIADM award; the award documents were not retrieved.
7. **NES Reserve's economics are not explained.** Vizient states no up-front investment or fee and 131 million units of supplier-held inventory (F10-S05). Who bears that carrying cost, and how it is recovered, is not disclosed. This is the single most important open question for anyone modelling who pays for resilience.

11. **Legal questions that require named professionals, not more searching.** (a) Whether a resilience premium paid by a hospital consortium implicates 42 U.S.C. 1320a-7b or falls outside 42 CFR 1001.952(j). (b) Whether joint purchasing arrangements carry materially different antitrust risk after the 2023 withdrawal of the FTC/DOJ statements. (c) Whether 340B ceiling price and Medicaid inflation-rebate mechanics permit a manufacturer to charge, and a hospital to pay, a stated premium for supply reliability. (d) Whether an availability payment untied to ordered quantity is procurable under FAR Part 16 or needs a different instrument. All four are gating for any Telo business model that depends on being paid for readiness rather than per vial.
