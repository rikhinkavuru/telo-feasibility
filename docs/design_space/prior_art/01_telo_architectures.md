# Prior art, Family 1: Existing Telo architectures

Owned distributed microplants; a centralized Telo plant; distributed nodes with conventional release; distributed nodes with a validated analytical-release component.

Prepared 2026-09-02 for the adversarial design-space review (docs/design_space/ASSIGNMENT.md, Phase B family 1 and Phase C). All web sources were accessed 2026-09-02. Statements marked "[inference]" are the author's reading, not a source's claim. Regulatory readings are preliminary and are marked UNCERTAIN where a qualified reviewer is needed. Nothing here is legal advice.

## 1. Scope

This family covers the four architectures Telo has already modeled as strategies S4 to S6 (and their owned-plant variants): several Telo-owned small aseptic plants placed regionally; one Telo-owned central sterile-injectable plant; regional nodes that release every batch by conventional compendial testing (USP <71> sterility, endotoxin, assay, environmental monitoring, QA review); and the same nodes with a validated spectroscopic or model-based release component that shortens the chemical part of the release path. The question for each is whether someone has already built it, at what scale, under which registration and release regime, and what happened. The family is judged against the governing objective (strongest feasible intervention against sterile-injectable shortage risk), not against Telo's pitch.

## 3. What already exists

### 3.1 Owned distributed microplants for sterile injectables: one pilot network, no cGMP network

On Demand Pharmaceuticals (ODP, Rockville MD) is the only company found that operates the architecture Telo calls a microplant network for sterile products. Its lineage is DARPA's Battlefield Medicine program (F1-S22, F1-S63), which DARPA now marks complete. What exists as of this review:

- A hub: the Centralized Production and Repackaging (CPR) facility in Rockville, which HHS describes as producing APIs (F1-S21).
- One spoke: a Pharmacy on Demand (PoD) at North Mississippi Medical Center, Tupelo, built with Germfree mobile cleanroom technology (F1-S44). HHS states the PoD "is currently deployed" there (F1-S21).
- Output to date: roughly 12,000 prefilled syringes over a six-month 503A pilot starting December 2023, across five shortage-list drugs including lidocaine, rocuronium and potassium chloride (F1-S40, F1-S43, F1-S44). This was compounding under a state board of pharmacy, not cGMP manufacture of an approved product. The Germfree release quotes an NMHS pharmacy leader on compounding "following regulations from the Board of Pharmacy" without naming the state, so the Mississippi attribution is [inference].
- Stated next steps: convert the Tupelo site to a 503B facility (F1-S40, F1-S42); an Office of Naval Research project (mid-2024 to 2026) intended to end in a DMF and an ANDA submission (F1-S40). No 503B registration for ODP was found, and that check is reproducible: the FDA outsourcing-facility table (updated 8/28/2026, 97 rows) contains no ODP or Tupelo entry (F1-S16). No ANDA approval and no DMF number were found either, but the search behind those two negatives was not recorded in reproducible form, so they are [unverified] as exhaustive negatives.
- Public funding found: $20M DoD/HHS CARES Act contract, September 2020, executed with DARPA oversight (F1-S20); a $325,000 NIH SBIR Phase 1 in 2024 (F1-S24). HHS describes ODP funding as "one part of more than $1 billion HHS has invested" without giving ODP's share (F1-S21). The ONR project value was not found.

FDA's Emerging Technology Program lists "Pharmacy on demand (a small manufacturing platform for continuous bioprocesses)" among accepted technologies, under biological molecules (F1-S05). The repository cites this as the closest public analogue to a microplant (LR-S01 §3); the listing is for bioprocesses, not small-molecule sterile injectables, and [inference] acceptance into ETP is not approval. The nearest source wording is the AMT guidance, "AMT designation requests are made independently of application submissions" (F1-S06); the ETP page itself is a list of accepted technologies and makes no statement about approval (F1-S05). See §5 item 6.

### 3.2 Centralized single plant for shortage injectables: Civica, and the commercial CDMO comparators

Civica Rx's Petersburg VA plant is the direct precedent for "one Telo plant". Announced January 2021 at $124.5M and 120,000 sq ft with lines for 90M vials and 50M prefilled syringes per year (F1-S37); reported at $140M and 140,000 sq ft in January 2023 with market entry expected 2024 (F1-S56); described in 2025 as three lines with capacity above 200M units per year (F1-S39). As of May and August 2025 the plant was operational but had not made a commercial batch, the milestone had slipped from 2024 to 2026, and the facility pre-approval inspection was expected in 2026 (F1-S57, F1-S58, F1-S38). Civica's supply to member hospitals still comes through contracted manufacturers (F1-S57). GAO describes one nonprofit as using long-term hospital purchasing contracts plus a back-up supply to provide shortage drugs (F1-S19); [inference] by context that company is Civica. [inference] The elapsed time from announcement to expected PAI is about five years for a well-funded, nonprofit, greenfield plant with experienced leadership; this is the best available calibration for Telo's commissioning delay on an owned central plant.

Commercial datapoints for a single isolator line: Jubilant HollisterStier's third Spokane line, $132M, 55M units per year at 400 vials per minute, revenue-generating October 2025 (F1-S51, F1-S52); Noramco/Halo Pharma, $25M for a Groninger line with a SKAN isolator inside an existing plant, batches of 5,000 to 250,000 units and capacity above 50M units per year, expected operational within about 12 months of March 2025 (F1-S53). Small gloveless workcells are commercial and approved: Cytiva reports commercial approvals by four agencies on its workcells (F1-S49) and 12 to 15 months from purchase order to validated SA25 (F1-S48); Syntegon's Versynta microBatch was co-developed with Vetter after Vetter found no suitable small-batch equipment in 2019 (F1-S50). List prices are not published; Cytiva's product page shows "On request" (F1-S49).

### 3.3 Distributed nodes with conventional release: three existing populations

- 503B outsourcing facilities: 97 rows on FDA's registered list as of 8/28/2026 (F1-S16), each by statute "a facility at one geographic location or address" (F1-S14). Multi-site operators exist as separately registered facilities (QuVa, Leiters, SCA and Fagron each appear twice on the list; F1-S16). Hospitals overwhelmingly buy from these facilities and few register their own pharmacies (F1-S17). Measured against shortages, overlap is small: 18% (50 of 272) of the unique APIs on FDA's shortage list, whose initial postings run from February 2012 to 27 January 2020, were compounded in the same dosage form by an outsourcing facility in the 503B product reports covering July 2018 onward (F1-S65). The paper's methods give the report window as July 2018 through June 2019 and its results section gives July 2018 to December 2019. The largest, Nephron, has repeat FDA findings from 2022 through 2025 (F1-S14, F1-S15, F1-S60).
- PET drug producers under 21 CFR Part 212: a distributed network with its own CGMP part and conditional release before sterility results, with sterility testing started within 30 hours of production (F1-S11).
- Phlow: an API maker, not a fill-finish network. Its Petersburg kilogram-scale plant (250 to 500 kg per year) began operating late 2023 with a metric-ton plant to follow (F1-S55); [inference] the date is derived, the source says "It began operations late last year" in a piece dated 10 April 2024. Phlow states it has completed five API programs and filed four DMFs (F1-S36). Finished-dose epinephrine is to be filled by Fresenius Kabi, with availability in 2027 pending approvals (F1-S35). The contract is cost-plus-fixed-fee, base value $354,256,000 with options, period of performance to 2030 (F1-S25); obligated to date $696.7M, potential $969.8M (F1-S30); Phlow describes it as a $1.2 billion 10-year contract (F1-S34).

## 5. Regulatory basis and constraints

Sections are cited by number. "UNCERTAIN" marks readings that need a qualified regulatory reviewer.

1. Establishment identity. Today an establishment is a place of business under one management at one general physical location (21 CFR 207.1, quoted in F1-S01). The 2026 proposed rule would add "distributed manufacturing establishment", "distributed manufacturing hub" and "distributed manufacturing unit" to § 207.1 and would let a hub plus DMUs register as one establishment (F1-S01). Conditions read from the primary text: DMUs "demonstrated to be and that remain equivalent in design and operation at any location"; the same drug(s); a single quality unit at the hub with a unified pharmaceutical quality system; one management as a single legal entity, so networks of unaffiliated CMOs do not qualify; a preapproval inspection tied to an approved application (NDA, 505(b)(2), ANDA, NADA or BLA) that describes the decentralized strategy, for at least one drug per profile class; either one DMU capable of mobility or at least two DMUs; initial registration within 5 calendar days after the first domestic DMU begins manufacture for commercial distribution; DMU additions, removals and mobile relocations as expedited updates. The text contains no occurrence of "sterile", "aseptic", "503B", "outsourcing facilit", or "real-time release". Status: proposed rule, comments close 2026-09-11 (F1-S01 record). Corrections to the repository: dme-rule.md (LR-S02) says the primary text was not read; it has now been read and the conditions reported from law-firm summaries verify, with two wording differences: the rule says "capable of mobility", not "movable", and the 5-day clock runs from the first domestic DMU's first commercial manufacture. UNCERTAIN: whether a sterile-injectable ANDA describing a decentralized strategy would receive a preapproval inspection covering aseptic validation at each DMU or at a representative DMU.
2. Quality unit and contract sites. 21 CFR 211.22(a) makes one quality control unit responsible for approving or rejecting every batch, including batches made under contract (F1-S08). [inference] This is the US analogue of the hub quality unit and is the reason architecture B in the repository's CMO map (owner's quality unit retains disposition) applies to any Telo node that is not the application holder (LR-S06).
3. Sterility assurance at each node. 21 CFR 211.113(b) requires validation of all aseptic and sterilization processes (F1-S09); 211.167(a) requires sterility testing of each batch purporting to be sterile (F1-S10); 211.165(a) requires laboratory determination of conformance before release, with the only release-before-sterility carve-out being short-lived radiopharmaceuticals (F1-S07). EU Annex 1 §10.5 to §10.7 treat the sterility test as the last of a series of controls, require representative samples including beginning and end of aseptic fills, and allow release before a sterility result only where shelf life is too short to complete the test (F1-S26). Annex 16 §1.7 lets the certifying QP rely on the pharmaceutical quality system with ongoing assurance (F1-S27). [inference] None of these sections gives a distributed node any release path a central plant lacks; the release components in the model (sterility_incubation_days, environmental_monitoring_days, assay_days, endotoxin_days, qa_review_days) apply per node and per batch.
4. PET precedent. 21 CFR 212.70(c) to (e) permit final release of PET drugs before sterility testing is complete, with the test started within 30 hours, and sub-batch controls (F1-S11). This is a product-class rule tied to short half-life, not a template for conventional injectables (consistent with LR-S01 §1a table).
5. 503B nodes. An outsourcing facility is one geographic location (F1-S14; statute F1-S13 §353b(d)(4)); bulk substances must be on the 503B bulks list or the drug must be on the shortage list at compounding, distribution and dispensing (§353b(a)(2), F1-S13, F1-S14); the drug may not be essentially a copy of an approved drug (§353b(a)(5), F1-S13). A multi-site 503B network therefore means one registration, inspection history and product-report stream per site, and no single-establishment identity. FDA has warned Nephron against sharing equipment or personnel between 503B and conventional operations (F1-S15). UNCERTAIN: how a hospital-hosted PoD converts from 503A to 503B while the host pharmacy continues 503A compounding on the same premises.
6. Programs, not approvals. ETP acceptance and AMT designation (section 506L) facilitate assessment; neither substitutes for an application (F1-S05, F1-S06). GAO reports few products made by advanced manufacturing (F1-S18).
7. UK comparator. SI 2025 No. 87 defines POC and modular manufacture, names only a Control Site on the licence, and provides for an MM master file, "a detailed description of the arrangements for the manufacture or assembly of an MM medicinal product" (F1-S29). MHRA's GMP guideline expects the Control Site to onboard, suspend and decommission remote sites and to demonstrate consistency across them; modular-manufacture product release follows the routine approach with QP certification (F1-S28). The UK POC criterion, verbatim, is a product that "for reasons relating to method of manufacture, shelf life, constituents or method or route of administration, can only be manufactured at or near the place where the product is to be used or administered" (F1-S29). [inference] A conventional sterile injectable with a two-year shelf life would not meet the UK POC test; the modular route would apply instead, and it keeps conventional release.
8. Cross-site equivalence evidence. Neither the FDA proposed rule nor any fetched source specifies how DMU equivalence is demonstrated; the repository's gate G14 (no fleet claim) stays as written (LR-S06).

## 6. Adjacent-industry analogues

PET radiopharmacy network (United States). Positron emission tomography drugs are made at many sites close to hospitals under their own CGMP part, with conditional release before sterility results because the product decays in hours (F1-S11). It is the working existence proof that a distributed sterile network can be regulated and inspected; the enabling condition is a physical shelf-life constraint that generic injectables do not have. The repository already draws this distinction (LR-S01 §1a).

UK decentralised manufacture (point of care and modular). In force since July 2025, this is the only fully enacted national framework for hub-and-spoke manufacture found. Its design choices are informative: a single licensed Control Site, remote sites tracked in a master file rather than on the licence, a designation step that restricts POC to products that cannot be made conventionally, and unchanged release standards for modular products (F1-S28, F1-S29). An ISPE iSpeak summary of a December 2025 presentation says regulators "are responding at different speeds", that MHRA "leads the way with updated legislation and detailed guidance", and that FDA "has yet to issue formal guidance" (F1-S33). The piece states that it is an informal summary of one speaker's presentation, not vetted by any regulator.

Decentralized 3D printing of oral medicines. A pharmaceutical company shipped granulate feedstock to a printing site in another country; in-line NIR with a PLS model quantified drug load as PAT (F1-S69). This is the nearest published instance of "central formulation, local finishing with an analytical release component", and it is non-sterile.

Military logistics (DARPA Battlefield Medicine). The original driver for miniaturized manufacturing was forward-deployed supply where stockpiling is impossible (F1-S22, F1-S63). The commercial descendants (ODP, MIT platform) demonstrated non-sterile liquid doses at single-dose to hundreds-of-doses scale (F1-S62).

Nonprofit manufacturing (Civica). GAO records that nonprofit drug companies fund development by donations and price to cover cost, that donors large enough to fund drug development are scarce, and that Civica's shortage effect comes from long-term contracts and back-up supply rather than from its own plant, which has not yet produced commercial product (F1-S19, F1-S57).

Continuous-manufacturing economics (academic). The Novartis-MIT center (2007, $65M over ten years; F1-S61 snippet) produced the end-to-end systems that CONTINUUS commercialized. Peer-reviewed techno-economic work with FDA co-authors found hybrid batch-continuous configurations cheapest for small-scale API and fully continuous operation more costly because of long stabilization times (F1-S67). Srai et al. (2015) set out the decentralized, low-inventory supply-chain vision but noted low adoption (F1-S66, snippet).
