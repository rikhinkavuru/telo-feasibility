## 1. Scope

This family asks whether the shortage-risk reduction Telo wants from owned microplants can instead be bought by combining stock with capacity: finished-goods inventory held regionally or nationally, API and component reserves held upstream, and some form of reserved, prequalified, or expandable production that is activated when the stock is drawn down. In the simulation this is the region of design space spanned by strategies S1 (180-day safety stock), S2 (dual source), S3 (reserved contract capacity), and S4 (added central capacity), and it changes the inventory policy, contract terms, common-cause exposure, and cost structure rather than the release pathway. The question for the model is which of these hybrids is already sold, mandated, or funded by someone else, what price and activation performance they document, and where the documented failures are.

## 3. What already exists

**Federal stock and reserve programs (United States).**

- The Strategic National Stockpile is a medical-countermeasure stockpile for CBRN threats, emerging infectious diseases, and pandemics (F8-S01). Its formulary is antibiotics, antivirals, vaccines, antidotes, antitoxins, radiological agents, IV fluids and ancillaries, ventilators, and PPE (F8-S02). [inference] It is not a shortage stockpile for routine generic sterile injectables; no source states this, it follows from the formulary. CRS records that the establishing statute frames the SNS around "a bioterrorist attack or other public health emergency" (F8-S03). The deployment trigger is not the binding constraint: CRS states that "Statute does not require either the declaration of a public health emergency by the HHS Secretary or a presidential emergency declaration for deployment of the stockpile", and that administrators have deployed it across "multiple hurricane and flooding responses" among other non-CBRN scenarios, at the request of state, local, tribal, and territorial jurisdictions and at the Secretary's discretion (F8-S03). The binding constraints are the formulary (F8-S02) and the obligation share: countermeasures against smallpox and anthrax took approximately 75 percent of funds obligated for non-COVID-19 supplies in FY2015 through FY2021 (F8-S03). UNCERTAIN: whether the Secretary has ever exercised that discretion for a routine drug shortage; no source was found either way.
- SNS vendor-managed inventory (VMI) is the government's own rotating-stock mechanism: the SNS pays a vendor to hold a specified quantity, the vendor rotates it into its commercial channel, and delivery on call is 24 to 36 hours (F8-S03). CRS reports that HHS terminated all VMI contracts in 2017 as not cost-effective, restarted them during COVID-19, and that VMI is about 10% of current SNS contracts (F8-S03). The documented limit is that rotation only works when the commercial market is large enough to sell the reserve before expiry (F8-S03).
- Shelf life. The SNS uses the federal Shelf-Life Extension Program; FDA states that SLEP "remains limited to federal stockpiles at this time" (F8-S20), and initial SLEP extensions are typically 12 to 24 months (F8-S02). For commercial product during shortages, FDA posts lot-specific extended use dates supported by manufacturer stability data (F8-S19). Neither mechanism is available to a private regional reserve as a general right.
- Strategic Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient Reserve (SAPIR). The only funded U.S. instance is a line item in BARDA's 2020 Phlow contract: a four-year, $354 million award extendable to $812 million over ten years (F8-S07); the contract's CLIN 0004 is to establish a SAPIR facility (seen in the contract text via search excerpt; the KEI-hosted copy was not fetched). Phlow's own 2020 white paper describes SAPIR as an API stockpile that would be converted to finished product through capacity contracts with ANDA holders and 503B facilities, and says legislation may be needed for multiple manufacturers to use SAPIR API (F8-S08). The 2021 White House 100-day review recommended a "virtual strategic stockpile" of API, critical materials, and finished doses, held by suppliers under contract together with surge capacity, managed by the SNS (F8-S04). Legislation to create a statutory reserve has been introduced repeatedly: PREPARE Act (S. 2740, 2021; H.R. 7709, 2024) and RAPID Reserve Act (S. 2510 and H.R. 6802, 2023; S. 2062 and H.R. 3955, 2025) (F8-S05, F8-S06). The RAPID Reserve Act would pay application holders to hold a six-month rotating reserve of both API and finished product, produce at HHS direction, transfer API to another manufacturer on HHS determination, and authorizes $500 million for FY2026 (F8-S06, read from the bill text). None of these bills was found enacted; the repository's statement that the Strategic API Reserve is a funded policy tailwind (F8-S42) is true only of the Phlow CLIN, not of any statutory program.
- Hospital-side buffer stock paid by Medicare. In the FY2025 IPPS final rule CMS created a separate payment for small, independent hospitals (100 beds or fewer, not in a chain) that voluntarily hold a six-month buffer stock of one or more of 86 essential medicines, effective for cost-reporting periods beginning on or after 2024-10-01, directly or "through contractual arrangements with a pharmaceutical manufacturer, distributor or intermediary" (F8-S17). Payment is for the additional resource cost of establishing and maintaining the buffer, not the drugs; CMS assumed a carrying cost of 20 percent of the cost of the medicines for its spending estimate (F8-S18). Medicare will not pay for a buffer newly established for a medicine already in shortage (F8-S17). The mechanism is small: it excludes every hospital over 100 beds and every system hospital. No earlier U.S. payer mechanism for a hospital buffer stock was found in this search, and no cited source calls this one the first, so no first-of-its-kind claim is made [inference].

**Private and nonprofit programs (United States).**

- Civica Rx (2018): member health systems commit to about 50 percent of expected volume for three to ten years, receive one price and guaranteed delivery, and Civica holds a six-month buffer stock (F8-S14). Civica entered by contracting CMOs that held spare capacity and ANDAs (F8-S14); GAO describes its model as long-term purchasing contracts plus a back-up supply (F8-S15). ASPE reports a study finding 96 percent fulfilment of contracted volume against 86 percent for wholesaler orders, with the caveat that the samples differ (F8-S14). The repository describes Civica as "one large plant plus a ~6-month stockpile" (F8-S42); ASPE's account is CMO-based supply with the buffer as the working mechanism, so the repo's single-plant framing should be corrected [inference from F8-S14].
- Vizient Novaplus Enhanced Supply (launched 2019): contracted suppliers manufacture additional supply of essential medications and hold it onshore, sized on members' historical usage (F8-S10). Vizient's Pharmacy Reserve adds "up to six months of dedicated inventory, warehoused in the U.S." (F8-S11). NES is supplier-held (F8-S10). Who holds title to Pharmacy Reserve stock is not stated on the Vizient page, which says only that it is "warehoused in the U.S." and that NES and Pharmacy Reserve "work together to secure committed volume and dedicated inventory" (F8-S11) [inference: supplier- or distributor-held]. Both are GPO-contracted regional stock, which is exactly the "regional stock plus centralized expansion" sub-idea. The holder is load-bearing because it decides the DSCSA structure analysed in section 5.
- Premier ProvideGx: long-term committed contracts with manufacturers that require them to hold an average of three to six months of both API and finished dose (F8-S12). This is the "API reserve plus finished-product inventory" sub-idea, already contracted at GPO scale.

**Mandated stock abroad.**

- Germany's ALBVVG (2023) requires a six-month stock for drugs under statutory rebate contracts and requires hospital pharmacies to increase stocks of parenteral products and ICU antibiotics (F8-S32; the quoted sentence translates as "Pharmaceutical companies will in future be required to hold a six-month stock of rebated medicines").
- Finland's Act on Obligatory Storing of Medicines (979/2008, with Government Decree 1114/2008 as amended by Decrees 308/2012 and 1106/2015) obliges "pharmaceutical companies operating in Finland, importers of medicinal products, health care units and the National Institute for Health and Welfare" to hold stock located in Finland (F8-S33). The quantity schedule differs by tier and the two must not be conflated (F8-S34). For pharmaceutical companies and importers, "the quantity to be stocked is the number of products equivalent to the average sales over a period of 10, 6 or 3 months." For health care units, "the quantity to be stocked is the number of products equivalent to the average consumption over a period of 6 months, 3 months or 2 weeks." The hospital-tier floor is therefore two weeks, an order of magnitude below the manufacturer floor. Compensation is paid by the National Emergency Supply Agency to pharmaceutical companies and importers; the Fimea page does not say health care units are compensated (F8-S33). Upstream materials may be held instead of finished product: "The products to be stocked as obligatory storage include medicinal substances and medicinal products as well as the excipients and additives and packaging materials used in the manufacturing of medicinal products" (F8-S33).
- The EU Critical Medicines Act was proposed in March 2025 and centers on strategic manufacturing projects, resilience-weighted public procurement, and collaborative procurement (F8-S35). It is a capacity-and-procurement instrument, not a stockpile.

**Software.** A granted U.S. patent (US11983666B2, Trulla/SpendMend, 2024) covers a hospital pharmaceutical procurement platform with inventory integration and shortage prediction (F8-S40). [inference] Shortage-aware procurement software is an occupied area. This generalises from a single granted patent; no market or landscape source was obtained.

## 4. What was tried and failed or discontinued

- **Reserved idle capacity: CIADM (2012 to 2021).** HHS funded three sites to hold pandemic surge capacity. GAO found the sites were underused before COVID-19 (an internal review found two sites with none of their capacity used and the third at 7 percent), that the lack of regular work prevented them from building commercial-scale capability, that during the pandemic BARDA paid to reserve capacity but the sites "were learning in real time," that one site was terminated in November 2021 after cross-contamination, and that none met the surge goal (F8-S09; the utilization and $750 million reservation figures are from the report body seen in search excerpts, the product page was fetched). Documented cause, as GAO states it: the lack of regular manufacturing work prevented the sites from developing the capability the program intended (F8-S09). [inference] The generalisation that unexercised reserved capacity decays, and that readiness is a function of throughput rather than of contract, is this dossier's, not GAO's.
- **Government rotating stock: SNS VMI (terminated 2017).** HHS ended all VMI contracts as not cost-effective or not meeting preparedness goals, because for some products the SNS requirement exceeded what vendors could sell before expiry (F8-S03). Restarted during COVID-19 at about 10 percent of contracts (F8-S03). Documented cause, as CRS states it qualitatively: rotation requires a commercial market large enough to sell the products before they expire (F8-S03). [inference] The inequality form, that rotation needs commercial throughput at least equal to reserve volume divided by shelf life, is this dossier's formalisation and it carries into the `expiry_scrap_fraction` recommendation in section 8.
- **Universal hospital buffer-stock payment (2023 request for comment).** CMS solicited comment on paying all hospitals for buffer stocks (88 FR 82127-30); HHS records that CMS did not adopt that policy (F8-S16). In the FY2025 rule commenters said a three-month buffer was inadequate, that even six months might not protect against long shortages, and that shortages "can be compounded by panic buying and hoarding behaviors" (F8-S18). A trade-press summary characterizes the 2023 response as stakeholders largely rejecting the idea as potentially exacerbating shortages (F8-S39; news source). The policy survived only as the small-independent-hospital payment (F8-S17).
- **Statutory API reserve.** Reserve bills have been introduced in four Congresses (2021, 2023, 2024, 2025) without enactment (F8-S05, F8-S06). No evidence was found that the 2021 virtual-stockpile recommendation (F8-S04) was implemented beyond the Phlow CLIN.
- **Uncoordinated hospital inventory as a shortage amplifier.** 81.3 percent of 719 pharmacy practice managers reported hoarding as a mitigation strategy (F8-S30). Park et al. show hospital stockpiling in the first two months of the pandemic was "an under-recognized cause of the sourcing challenges," without price inflation (F8-S31). This is not a program failure but it is the documented reason regulators resist mandated hospital buffers.
- No documented failure was found for Civica, Vizient NES, or ProvideGx; their performance claims are company claims except the ASPE fill-rate comparison (F8-S14).

## 5. Regulatory basis and constraints

Citations are to the section numbers read. Interpretation labeled UNCERTAIN needs a qualified regulatory reviewer.

- **Expiry and rotation of finished goods.** 21 CFR 211.137(a) requires an expiration date determined by 211.166 stability testing (F8-S22). 21 CFR 211.150(a) requires a distribution procedure whereby the oldest approved stock is distributed first, with temporary and appropriate deviations permitted (F8-S21). 21 CFR 211.142 requires written warehousing procedures with quarantine before release and controlled storage conditions (read from the same CFR volume; not a separate row). [inference] A manufacturer- or 3PL-held rotating reserve is therefore an ordinary cGMP warehousing operation; what is regulated is the storage condition and the first-in-first-out rotation, not the size of the reserve. This reading of 211.137, 211.142, and 211.150 has not been confirmed by a qualified regulatory reviewer.
- **Extending dating for a reserve.** Manufacturer-initiated extension requires long-term stability data on three batches under an approved protocol (F8-S20). SLEP is federal-only (F8-S20). Shortage extended-use dates are lot-specific and posted by FDA only when the manufacturer supplies data (F8-S19). UNCERTAIN: whether a private reserve could obtain SLEP-like testing under any current authority; FDA's page says no.
- **Moving stock between hospitals (DSCSA).** Under 21 U.S.C. 360eee(19), "specific patient need" transfers between pharmacies are allowed but the term excludes transfers "for the purpose of increasing or replenishing stock in anticipation of a potential need" (F8-S23). Transactions among hospitals under common control are exempt from transaction requirements, but the emergency-medical-reasons exemption states that "a drug shortage not caused by a public health emergency shall not constitute an emergency medical reason" (F8-S24). [inference] A rotating reserve shared across unaffiliated hospitals therefore has to be structured through a licensed wholesale distributor or a 3PL holding title-transferring transactions with full DSCSA data, or kept within one health system. UNCERTAIN: state wholesale-distributor licensing thresholds for hospital-to-hospital sales (21 U.S.C. 353(e) and 21 CFR Part 205 were not fetched).
- **API and component reserves.** [unverified] An API held in a reserve is usable only by application holders whose approved application names that API source, or after a supplement. No source for this rule was fetched (21 CFR 314.70 is listed in section 9 as not obtained), so it stands as an unverified working assumption and not as a finding. The adjacent sourced fact is that Phlow's white paper concedes legislation may be needed for multiple manufacturers to draw on SAPIR API (F8-S08). The RAPID Reserve Act would require reserves stored under 21 CFR Parts 210 and 211 and would let HHS direct API transfer to another manufacturer (F8-S06). UNCERTAIN: the supplement category (21 CFR 314.70) for switching to reserve API for a given ANDA, and the retest-period rules for stored API under ICH Q7 (not fetched).
- **Payer rules for hospital buffer stock.** CMS pays only the IPPS share of the additional resource cost of a six-month buffer of listed essential medicines, only to hospitals of 100 beds or fewer that are not in a chain, and not for a buffer newly established while the medicine is in shortage (F8-S17, F8-S18). The rule codifies the adjustment at 42 CFR 412.113(g) (per the CMS MLN notice seen in search; not fetched).
- **503B product in a reserve.** The repository's 503B map records that inventory compounded under the shortage exception may not be distributed or dispensed under the exception once the drug leaves the shortage list, with transition relief an unresolved question (F8-S44). Combined with the 6-day at-risk BUD economics recorded in the release-constraints memo (F8-S45), 503B product cannot serve as reserve inventory. Any inventory-capacity hybrid must hold approved product.
- **SNS scope.** The establishing statute frames the stockpile around "a bioterrorist attack or other public health emergency" (F8-S03), but the deployment gate is narrower than that language suggests in only one direction: CRS states that "Statute does not require either the declaration of a public health emergency by the HHS Secretary or a presidential emergency declaration for deployment of the stockpile", and records non-CBRN deployments including hurricanes and flooding (F8-S03). Deployment turns on an SLTT request plus the Secretary's discretion. The constraint on using the SNS against a routine generic shortage is therefore what the stockpile holds (F8-S02) and where its obligations go (about 75 percent to smallpox and anthrax, F8-S03), not a statutory scope test. UNCERTAIN: whether that discretion has ever been used for a routine drug shortage.
- **Foreign mandates.** Germany: six-month stock obligation for rebate-contract products under §130a(8) SGB V per the ministry summary, plus hospital-pharmacy stocking duties (F8-S32). Finland: Act 979/2008 and Decree 1114/2008, compensated, supervised by Fimea (F8-S33). These are legal duties on manufacturers and hospitals, not commercial products, and they show the state as the payer for carrying cost.

## 6. Adjacent-industry analogues

**Strategic Petroleum Reserve exchanges (U.S. DOE).** The SPR lends crude to a refiner after a physical disruption and the borrower repays in kind with a premium quantity; exchange agreements can be completed within days of a request and are used for hurricanes and channel closures rather than for price events (F8-S36). [inference] The structural lesson for a drug reserve is the loan-with-in-kind-premium contract, which makes drawdown self-replenishing and prices the reserve's option value in product rather than cash. [inference] The analogue breaks on shelf life: crude does not expire, so the SPR never needs commercial rotation; a drug reserve needs the VMI-style rotation whose feasibility condition is documented in F8-S03.

**Compensated national obligatory storage (Finland).** Finland runs mandatory multi-party stock under Act 979/2008 and Decree 1114/2008 as amended, with obligations on pharmaceutical companies, importers, health care units, and the National Institute for Health and Welfare, and with medicinal substances, excipients, additives, and packaging materials allowed in place of finished product (F8-S33). The obligation is sized differently by tier: 10, 6, or 3 months of average sales for companies and importers, against 6 months, 3 months, or 2 weeks of average consumption for health care units (F8-S34). Compensation is paid to companies and importers (F8-S33). No start year earlier than the 2008 Act is supported by the fetched sources (see section 9). It is the only sourced example of the full family (finished stock plus upstream reserve plus hospital stock) operating as one system, and it is a state program with a state payer, not a company. [inference] The tier split is the load-bearing detail for the rotating-hospital-inventory sub-idea: the state asks manufacturers for months and hospitals for as little as two weeks.

## 8. Implications for the model

Parameter names below are the ids in feasibility/config/global.yaml and the design variables in config/strategies/illustrative_baseline.yaml.

**Inventory policy.**
- `safety_stock_days` (strategy) and `target_safety_stock_days` (product; illustrative base 60, range 30 to 180): the modal externally-set buffer is six months of finished goods (Civica F8-S14; Vizient Pharmacy Reserve F8-S11; CMS F8-S17; Germany F8-S32; RAPID Reserve Act F8-S06), with three to six months at ProvideGx (F8-S12). Finland is not a single anchor and should not be used as one: its manufacturer tier is 10, 6, or 3 months of sales, while its health-care-unit tier is 6 months, 3 months, or 2 weeks of consumption (F8-S34). The only externally mandated *hospital* floor found in this family is therefore two weeks, which sits below the model's 30-day low bound. The optimizer's grid should include 90 and 180 days as policy anchors, and the S1 comparator (180 days) needs to be rankable; the strategies file notes the workbook screen cannot rank S1 (WB-02).
- `inventory_carrying_rate` (illustrative base 0.20): CMS assumed 20 percent of drug cost per year for its buffer-stock spending estimate (F8-S18). This does not validate the parameter but gives it a government-cited anchor for the base case; low and high bounds remain illustrative.
- Split finished-goods days from upstream days: add `api_reserve_days` and `component_reserve_days` (not in the current config). ProvideGx contracts both API and finished dose (F8-S12); Finland allows medicinal substances, excipients, additives, and packaging materials in lieu of finished product (F8-S33). API reserves act on `supplier_disruptions_per_supplier_year` and `supplier_disruption_duration_days` exposure; component reserves act on component lead time in the common-cause groups.
- `expiry_scrap_fraction`: rotation through a commercial channel is feasible only when annual commercial throughput is at least the reserve divided by shelf life (F8-S03, the documented VMI limit). The model should compute scrap from reserve size, shelf life, and contracted throughput rather than take a fixed fraction; SLEP-style extension is unavailable to private reserves (F8-S20) and shortage extended-use dates are lot-specific and discretionary (F8-S19).
- `bud` (503B beyond-use date) and the 503B state machine: 503B product cannot be reserve inventory (F8-S44, F8-S45). Inventory hybrids must be restricted to `pathway: approved_cmo`.

**Demand and hoarding.** The simulation has `demand_shock_multiplier` and `demand_shocks_per_year` but no term for demand amplification caused by the shortage itself. 81 percent of pharmacy managers hoard (F8-S30); hospital stockpiling at onset was a cause of sourcing failure (F8-S31); CMS commenters flagged panic buying (F8-S18). Add a `hoarding_amplification_factor` applied to regional demand when fill drops below a threshold, and test whether a coordinated reserve (single allocation authority) removes it. This is the mechanism by which uncoordinated regional stock can worsen the shortage the model is trying to cure.

**Capacity, commissioning delay, and activation.**
- `reserved_capacity_activation_days` (illustrative 7/21/60) has no sourced value for sterile injectables. The only rich datapoint is negative: CIADM reserved capacity did not deliver reliably despite about $750 million of reservation funding because the sites had not been exercised (F8-S09). The model needs a `reserved_capacity_activation_failure_prob` that increases with idle fraction, and a readiness-exercise cost; a reservation fee alone (`reserved_capacity_fee_fraction`, illustrative 0.1 to 0.6) does not buy readiness.
- Lee et al. find plant-level learning: each prior resolved shortage shortens a plant's time to recovery, learning is confined to internally caused disruptions and does not transfer across sites within a firm (F8-S27). This argues for a site-level recovery-time parameter that decreases with campaign count, and against assuming a rarely-run node recovers as fast as a busy one. It also bears on `site_failure_duration_days`.
- VMI delivery of 24 to 36 hours (F8-S03) is the sourced inventory activation time; `emergency_transport_days` can be bounded by it for held stock.
- `node_commissioning_days` (365 to 1095) is untouched by this family; the hybrid substitutes stock for new nodes during that window, which is the main reason to model it.

**Common-cause groups.** Finished-goods reserves extend coverage by `safety_stock_days` against any event but do not change `common_cause_events_per_year`, `common_cause_duration_days`, or `common_cause_capacity_impact`. API and component reserves reduce exposure to the API and component members of the common-cause group only if the reserve API is usable by the affected application holder (UNCERTAIN, section 5). Tomlin's rule that sourcing beats inventory as disruptions become rarer and longer (F8-S25) means the base-case `common_cause_duration_days` of 60 (range 14 to 240) decides whether inventory or a second source dominates; this is a sensitivity to run explicitly.

**Contract terms.** Civica: about 50 percent of expected volume committed for three to ten years, one price, guaranteed delivery (F8-S14). Vizient NES: supplier-held stock sized on historical usage with no member commitment fee stated (F8-S10). ProvideGx: long-term committed contracts with three to six months of API and finished dose (F8-S12). Jia and Zhao: price increases alone are ineffective; price plus strengthened failure-to-supply clauses reduces shortages 25 to 70 percent in their scenarios (F8-S28). Tucker et al.: moderate failure-to-supply penalties, mandatory redundancy, substantial inventory, or large price increases each reduce shortages, at different societal cost (F8-S29). The model should carry `contract_committed_fraction`, `contract_length_years`, and a `failure_to_supply_penalty` as design variables, and treat the reserved-capacity fee in S3 as a capacity charge rather than a variable-cost fraction (the strategies file records this as workbook anomaly WB-21).

**Cost structure and payer.** The only external payer for hospital-held buffers is Medicare's small-independent-hospital adjustment, which pays carrying cost share and not the drugs (F8-S17, F8-S18). Germany and Finland put the obligation on companies and hospitals, with Finland compensating (F8-S32, F8-S33). For Telo the implication is that a reserve product needs a named payer; GPO programs price it inside the drug price, and the federal reserve remains a bill.

**Release components.** Unchanged by this family. Reserve stock is released product; the 14-day sterility path in the release memo (F8-S45) affects only the replenishment lead time, which is one input to the required `safety_stock_days`.

**Allocation and DSCSA.** A `pooling_scope` variable is needed: within one health system (transfers exempt under common control) versus across systems (requires wholesale-distributor structure; shortage is not an emergency reason) (F8-S23, F8-S24). No allocation or pooling parameter appears in the repository config: a case-insensitive search of `feasibility/config/` for "allocat" returns no match (checked 2026-09-03). A shared reserve without allocation rules is where hoarding re-enters.

**Repository corrections surfaced.**
- sources.yaml S25 (F8-S43): the DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2024.08337 resolves; the paper is Lee, Lee, Noh, Staats, "Learning in Recovery from Disruption: Empirical Evidence from the U.S. Drug Shortages," Management Science, published online 2026-05-28. The workbook's 2026 is the publication year; 2024 in the DOI string is the manuscript number. Its subject is plant-level learning in recovery, not inventory policy, so it should not be cited for recovery parameters without reading the effect sizes (F8-S27).
- market-research.md (F8-S42): "Strategic API Reserve" is not a funded statutory program; it is a Phlow contract line item plus unenacted bills (F8-S05 to F8-S08). Civica is CMO-supplied with a six-month buffer, not "one large plant" as the working mechanism (F8-S14). The claim that Telo "removes the inventory cost" is unsupported; inventory is what the sourced programs actually pay for.
