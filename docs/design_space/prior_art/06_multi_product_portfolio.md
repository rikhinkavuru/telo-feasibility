## 1. Scope

Family 6 asks whether a Telo node (owned or contracted, small or mid-scale) should run several sterile-injectable products on shared lines, shared utilities and a shared quality organization, scheduled in campaigns, so that fixed cost is spread and demand is pooled. In the feasibility model this touches the capacity and cost mechanisms (node scale, fixed QA labor, validation cost), the disruption mechanisms (common-cause groups, site failures), the release mechanisms (per-product release components), and inventory policy (shelf life, safety stock). The model today runs one product per network, so portfolio pooling is absent; this dossier records what the industry and regulators already do on every sub-idea in the family, what has failed, and what each source would let the model replace. For the denominator, ASHP with the University of Utah Drug Information Service reports that "The number of active drug shortages is 223, trending up for the second quarter in a row, but significantly lower than the all-time high of 323 in the first quarter of 2024" (F6-S21).

## 3. What already exists

**Multi-product sterile plants are the industry default, at scales two to three orders of magnitude above a Telo node.** Fresenius Kabi's Melrose Park site states 250 finished products, six aseptic lines, six lyophilizers and one terminal sterilizer producing more than 160 million vials a year, with a further five lines added in a stand-alone expansion building "with separate utilities" that includes one cytotoxic line and one high-potency and hormone line (F6-S26). Hikma reports more than 180 injectable products across its US portfolio (F6-S27). Pfizer Rocky Mount produces "nearly 50" medicines that "make up about 25 percent of Pfizer's sterile injectables used in U.S. hospitals, which equates to approximately 8 percent of the U.S. hospital supply" (F6-S25); the sterile-injectable qualifier attaches to the 25 percent figure, and the 8 percent is stated against US hospital supply generally. Gland Pharma discloses per-site line counts (Dundigal: six vial lines with seven lyophilizers, one ampoule line, two PFS lines, two bag lines, one ophthalmic line) and 28 lines company-wide at roughly 1,000 million units of normalized capacity in FY2021-22 (F6-S28, F6-S29). None of these companies publishes products-per-line; the products-per-site figures above are the only disclosed counts found. [inference] Fresenius does not say which lines the 250-product and 160-million-vial totals cover. Read against the six existing lines they give of the order of 25 million vials and 40 products per line-year; read against all eleven lines including the five-line expansion they give about 15 million vials and 23 products per line. Products are not spread evenly in either case, so treat both as bounds, not measurements.

**Segregated product families inside multi-product sites are standard.** Fresenius separates cytotoxic and high-potency/hormone lines (F6-S26); Gland runs a dedicated penem facility and a separate oncology facility (F6-S28); ASPE recorded in 2011 that "certain drugs (including cytotoxic drugs) may only be produced on certain types of lines and in certain types of facilities, so the extent of substitution is limited" and that "there are costs in shifting from one drug to another on a specific line" (F6-S15).

**Line flexibility is marketed by both manufacturers and equipment vendors.** Gland states its facilities "can switch product lines seamlessly" (F6-S28). Civica's Petersburg plant, three lines (vials, prefilled syringes, pen cartridges), more than 200 million units a year, is described as able to pivot "to produce many different forms of injectables based on greatest need"; the same document marks the site "pending FDA approval" (F6-S31). Vendors sell small-batch isolator cells with quoted batch-to-batch changeover of about two hours (Syntegon Versynta microBatch, up to 450 containers per hour, F6-S41) and tool-free format or process module changes under 30 minutes (SKAN VarioSys, up to about 4,500 containers per hour, F6-S42). Gloveless robotic nested-container filling is patented (Vanrx, F6-S43). These changeover figures are vendor claims for format and module swaps, and the two vendors scope their cells differently: Syntegon targets clinical trials, cell and gene therapy and personalized medicine (F6-S41), while SKAN states VarioSys "is suitable for aseptic(-toxic) filling in early development phases, clinical applications, and small to medium commercial batches" (F6-S42). Both figures exclude product-specific cleaning validation, H2O2 decontamination cycles, line clearance and QA release of the changeover, and no independent commercial-scale changeover data for generic sterile lines was found.

**Campaign scheduling with sequence-dependent changeovers and shelf-life constraints is a solved formulation problem in the process-systems literature**, at least for biopharmaceutical plants (MILP, resource-task-network, continuous time; F6-S39). The search also returned a "rigorous approach to scheduling of sterile drug product manufacturing" paper and several multiproduct batch-plant scheduling papers (listed in section 9; not fetched).

**Demand pooling across hospitals with long-term contracts and a buffer stock exists as a business.** Civica supplies more than 70 medications to over 1,400 hospitals under long-term purchase and supply contracts and targets six months of extra inventory (F6-S30); GAO describes the same model in 2025 (F6-S32). This is pooling of demand across buyers for a fixed product list chosen by member pharmacists, not portfolio pooling at a plant; until Petersburg is approved the products are contract-manufactured.

**Cross-contamination control in shared facilities has a mature methodology, set by regulation in the EU and carried by industry guidance in the US**: EMA's health-based exposure limit guideline (F6-S07), its 2018 Q&A (F6-S08), Annex 15 chapter 10 (F6-S04), EU GMP Chapters 3 and 5 (F6-S05, F6-S06), FDA's 1993 cleaning-validation inspection guide (F6-S09), and ISPE Risk-MaPP second edition, whose product page describes the guide as "Supporting compliance with evolving global regulatory expectations, including EMA and FDA requirements" (F6-S40). That is alignment with agency requirements, not agency review of the guide; no source was found showing that FDA or EMA reviewed Risk-MaPP before publication.

## 4. What was tried and failed or discontinued

**Multi-product sterile sites that failed took every product on the site down with them; the documented reasons are quality-system and economic, not portfolio design.**

- Ben Venue Laboratories (Bedford, Ohio) announced it would cease production by the end of 2013. The company's own reasons: "The magnitude of continued investment and time required to overcome the systemic manufacturing challenges is not viable," after more than $350 million invested and about $700 million of projected losses over five years (F6-S33). No source describing Ben Venue's product count, its customers or its compliance history was fetched, so none of that is asserted here; a DOJ release on the 2013 permanent injunction was found in search but the page could not be retrieved (section 9).
- Akorn filed Chapter 7 on 23 February 2023 and, per the FDA-posted company announcement, "has ceased and shutdown all operations and terminated all its employees of all domestic US Sites"; the trustee recalled all within-expiry products because "The discontinuation of the Quality program means the company will not be able to support or guarantee that the products will meet all intended specifications through the labeled shelf life of the product" (F6-S24). [inference] This is the clearest documented case that a shared quality organization is a single point of failure for an entire portfolio: the products were not defective, the quality system that vouched for them stopped existing.
- PharMEDium (503B outsourcing facilities: Memphis, Dayton NJ, Sugar Land TX, Cleveland MS) "voluntarily suspended production activities in December 2017 at its largest compounding facility located in Memphis, Tennessee pending execution of certain remedial measures" (F6-S44), then entered a consent decree in May 2019 that allowed compounding to continue at only two sites (F6-S34). The parent is reported to have closed the business in early 2020, but no page supporting that was fetched, so the closure is [unverified] (section 9). Multi-site, multi-product compounding under one quality system did not survive one site's sterility findings plus remediation cost.
- Teligent (Buena, New Jersey) built a small versatile vial and ampoule line rated at four to eight million units a year inside an existing topical plant; the 2019 site warning letter blocked the pre-approval inspection for the new injectable line, and the company entered Chapter 11 in October 2021 (F6-S35). [inference] This is the closest documented analogue to a small multi-product node: the line was sized like a Telo node, and site-level compliance, not line capacity, decided whether any product could launch.
- Intas Sanand (Ahmedabad) was inspected "from November 22 to December 02, 2022", and "FDA placed your firm on Import Alert 66-40 on June 1, 2023" after the inspection found destroyed records and data-integrity failures across production and laboratory departments (F6-S22). KFF Health News reported, in its own reportorial voice and without naming a source for the figure, that the plant "provided more than half of the U.S. supply of generic cisplatin and carboplatin" (F6-S23). One site's data-integrity failure removed a whole oncology portfolio at once. Whether FDA exempted any Intas products from the import alert was not established and is not asserted here.
- Hospira Rocky Mount (shutdown from 2010) closed for an upgrade planned at three months that took a year. Of the six shortages GAO attributed to temporary shutdowns of entire facilities, "Four of these six shortages were caused by one manufacturer’s shutdown of a single facility", and "because the manufacturer had not planned for an extended shutdown, 4 of the 15 drug shortages we reviewed, as well as other sterile injectable drugs produced at that facility, went into short supply" (F6-S14; GAO does not name the firm). [inference] The GAO case and the Hospira Rocky Mount shutdown are the same event; the attribution is the researcher's, not GAO's. The 2012 restart date rests only on a snippet-only Reuters row (F6-S36) and is [unverified].
- Pfizer Rocky Mount (July 2023 tornado): production areas were undamaged, the warehouse lost about 40,000 pallets of packaging and finished goods, and "All medicines manufactured in Rocky Mount have been placed on allocation" (F6-S25). A shared non-production asset (warehouse) put all roughly 50 products on allocation simultaneously.

**What the failures have in common.** GAO found in 2016 that all seven establishments linked to widespread shortages "had previous indications of difficulty complying with manufacturing standards" (F6-S17). ASPE wrote in 2011 that "quality problems that affect an entire plant may result in shortages for many drugs" (F6-S15) and FDA's own drug-shortage staff summarized the same report as "Problems typically affect multiple products at once" (F6-S16). Both FDA and HHS locate the root cause in prices too low to fund redundancy and quality investment (F6-S13, F6-S18). Two FDA authors put numbers on the same mechanism: "after one key facility with cytotoxic lines shut down in early 2010, the number of shortages of chemotherapy drugs increased from 4 to 24", and on the absence of backup sites, "Our analysis of sterile-injectable applications approved between 2000 and 2011 identified close to 900 approved abbreviated new drug applications (ANDAs). Of those, only 11, or just over 1%, referenced more than one facility for production of the finished drug." (F6-S37). No source was found describing a multi-product sterile node that failed because of changeover burden or scheduling complexity as such.

## 5. Regulatory basis and constraints

Citations are to section numbers; interpretations that need a qualified reviewer are marked UNCERTAIN.

**Shared equipment and cleaning (US).** 21 CFR 211.67(a)-(c) requires cleaning and maintenance at appropriate intervals, written procedures including removal of previous batch identification and inspection before use, and records (F6-S02). 21 CFR 211.42(c) requires separate or defined areas to prevent contamination and mix-ups, and 211.42(d) requires penicillin to be made in separate facilities (F6-S01). FDA's 1993 inspection guide expects distinct written cleaning processes for between-batch versus between-product changes and states that validation must show the process "consistently meets predetermined specifications" (F6-S09). FDA's 2013 beta-lactam guidance says manufacturers "generally should utilize separate facilities" for non-penicillin beta-lactams (F6-S10). UNCERTAIN: whether FDA would accept HBEL-based (PDE) carryover limits in lieu of the historical 1/1000-dose or 10 ppm criteria for a given product pair; FDA has no counterpart to the EMA HBEL guideline, and Risk-MaPP (F6-S40) is an industry document.

**Shared equipment and cleaning (EU/PIC/S).** Annex 15 chapter 10 (2015): 10.1 cleaning validation for all product-contact equipment; 10.2 visual check alone not acceptable; 10.6 carryover limits based on a toxicological evaluation (footnote to the EMA HBEL guideline); 10.8 dirty and clean hold times; 10.9 campaign length as the basis for cleaning validation; 10.10 worst-case product approach must be re-assessed when new products are introduced; 10.13 number of runs set by risk assessment (no fixed "three"); 10.14 dedicated equipment where cleaning is ineffective (F6-S04). Chapter 3 section 3.6 and Chapter 5 sections 5.17-5.22: quality risk management including a potency and toxicological evaluation decides whether premises and equipment are dedicated "to a particular product or product family", and lists technical measures (dedicated facility; self-contained areas with separate HVAC and possibly isolated utilities; closed systems; isolators; single-use technology) and organisational measures (campaign dedication "by separation in time" followed by validated cleaning; cleaning verification after each campaign for higher-risk products) (F6-S05, F6-S06). The EMA HBEL guideline sets the PDE method and a phase-in of six months for new products in shared facilities (F6-S07); the 2018 Q&A requires HBELs for all products, expects the assessor to have "adequate expertise and experience in toxicology/pharmacology, familiarity with pharmaceuticals as well as experience in the determination of health-based exposure limits such as Occupational Exposure Levels (OEL) or Permitted Daily Exposure (PDE)" rather than a named credential, says contract givers must supply the HBEL data to contract manufacturers, and says products go to dedicated facilities where controls cannot assure the HBEL (F6-S08). Implication for the node: every product added to a shared line needs an HBEL, a cross-contamination QRM study, and possibly re-validation of the worst-case cleaning model. A 2023 Senate majority staff report states the same constraint from the product side: "Other complicating manufacturing factors include that because Vincristine’s active ingredient is cytotoxic and hazardous, it is expensive to manufacture and requires a dedicated facility." (F6-S19).

**Product-specific validation.** Each product on a line needs its own process validation, and each product-line-container combination needs media fills under 21 CFR 211.113(b); FDA's 2004 guidance sets a media-fill starting point of 5,000 to 10,000 units and, for batches under 5,000 units, a media fill "at least equal [to] the maximum batch size made on the processing line" (F6-S11). [inference] For a small node this removes the economy of a small process simulation: a 3,000-vial commercial batch implies a 3,000-unit media fill per line qualification and re-qualification, and every new container-closure or fill configuration adds one. Site transfer of an approved generic needs a supplement under 21 CFR 314.70; the repository's pathway map leaves the reporting category for a new sterile fill-finish site UNCERTAIN (F6-R02, gate G07). Secondary sources seen in search on 2026-09-02 put such transfers at prior-approval supplements with review clocks of four to ten months, but those sources were not recorded and cannot be traced, so the four-to-ten-month range is [unverified]; it was not confirmed against the 2004 FDA guidance "Changes to an Approved NDA or ANDA" and the reporting category stays UNCERTAIN.

**Shared utilities and quality staff.** FDA's draft RMP guidance (2022) asks manufacturers to identify "weaknesses in the infrastructure and utilities; water systems; heating, ventilation, air conditioning, and environmental control; and the proximity of support services such as laboratory testing", and lists "lack of availability of laboratory services to support manufacturing" and "inadequacy of management oversight" among manufacturing vulnerabilities (F6-S12). FDA states that "In March 2020, with the enactment of the Coronavirus Aid, Relief, and Economic Security Act (CARES Act), Congress added section 506C(j) to the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act)", requiring certain manufacturers to develop, maintain and implement a redundancy risk management plan (F6-S12). The required scope is prescription drug products that are "Life-supporting", "Life-sustaining" or "Intended for use in the prevention or treatment of a debilitating disease or condition, including any such drug used in emergency medical care or during surgery" (footnote markers 28 and 29 omitted) or critical to public health during a declared emergency (F6-S12). A Telo node making shortage-prone acute-care injectables would almost certainly be in scope (UNCERTAIN as to which products). The Akorn recall shows the regulatory consequence of a quality program ceasing (F6-S24).

**Shelf life.** 21 CFR 211.137 requires an expiration date supported by 211.166 stability testing (F6-S03). The RMP guidance asks manufacturers to evaluate whether stability data "demonstrate safety and efficacy beyond the labeled expiry date" (F6-S12), and the DSTF report lists lengthened expiration dating among FDA initiatives (F6-S13). [inference] Shelf-life extension is the cheapest lever on inventory-based resilience and is product-specific evidence, not a facility property.

**Correlated shortages as a regulatory finding.** FDA's 2019 analysis found that being made at a single API or finished-dose facility "was not a sufficiently discerning risk factor" because most marketed drugs are single-facility, and that narrowing to single facilities with significant quality issues "may further enhance the ability to predict the risk of shortage" (F6-S13, Appendix C). USP states the risk is "particularly acute when a single facility is responsible for producing the entire U.S. market supply" (F6-S20). [inference] Together these say: the correlation that matters for a portfolio node is conditional on a site-level quality event, and such events are the dominant shortage trigger.

## 6. Adjacent-industry analogues

**Biopharmaceutical campaign planning.** The process-systems engineering literature has formulated multi-product campaign scheduling with sequence-dependent changeovers, shelf-life-limited storage and lot traceability as MILP problems and solved industrial-scale instances (F6-S39). The formulation is directly reusable for a small-molecule fill-finish node; the parameters (changeover matrices, campaign length limits) are not, because sterile fill-finish changeovers are dominated by cleaning validation and decontamination rather than by bioreactor turnaround.

**Clinical-scale contract fill-finish.** Vetter and Syntegon co-developed a gloveless robotic workcell for small aseptic batches because, per the vendor, no equipment met the regulatory and automation demands of small-batch filling in 2019; the resulting product line targets clinical trials and cell and gene therapy with about two-hour batch changeovers (F6-S41). SKAN markets modular isolator cells for "small to medium commercial batches" with sub-30-minute module changes (F6-S42), and Vanrx's nested-container robotic filling is patented (F6-S43). These are the closest existing hardware analogues to a many-products, small-batch node; their customers are high-value biologics, not low-price generics, which is the economic gap the FDA and HHS reports describe (F6-S13, F6-S18).

## 8. Implications for the model

Parameter ids below were read from `feasibility/config/global.yaml`, `feasibility/config/strategies/illustrative_baseline.yaml` and `feasibility/src/telo_feasibility/strategies.py`. Those files are outside this dossier's citable in-repo list and the names were not re-checked in the 2026-09-03 audit, so every parameter id in this section is [unverified] and the orchestrator must confirm it before wiring anything. Four of the web sources used here are already frozen snapshots in the repository's official source registry (S02 DSTF report, S03 ASPE 2024 paper, S05 GAO-25-107153, S07 aseptic guidance; F6-R03), so wiring those needs no new snapshot.

**Mechanisms this family changes.**

1. *Common-cause groups.* The current groups are supplier-, OS-, quality- and geography-keyed (`cc_api_1`, `cc_vial_1`, `cc_os`, `cc_quality`, `cc_geo_R1`), and each strategy runs one product. A portfolio node needs a plant-level group per node whose members are every product on that node, with capacity impact near 1.0 for the event classes documented here (site shutdown for remediation, import alert, consent decree, quality-program loss, warehouse loss): F6-S14, F6-S22, F6-S24, F6-S25, F6-S33, F6-S34. Proposed: `cc_plant_<node>` with `common_cause_capacity_impact` drawn per event class; FDA's finding that single-facility status alone is not discriminating (F6-S13) argues for conditioning the event rate on a site quality-state variable rather than on facility count.

2. *Capacity.* Replace the single-product `node_scale_fraction` and `capacity_factor` with a product-to-line assignment and a per-line time budget: batches per product per campaign, `changeover_hours_format` (vendor claims: about 2 h batch-to-batch for a clinical-scale cell, F6-S41; under 30 min for tool-free module swaps on a cell scoped from early development through small to medium commercial batches, F6-S42), `changeover_hours_product` (must include decontamination cycle, line clearance, cleaning verification for higher-risk products per EU Ch.5 5.21; no commercial data found, elicit), `campaign_max_batches` (cleaning-validation basis per Annex 15 10.9, F6-S04), and a product-family compatibility matrix that forbids co-location of penicillins and beta-lactams and gates cytotoxics/hormones behind dedicated-line cost (F6-S01, F6-S10, F6-S07, F6-S26).

3. *Commissioning delay.* `node_commissioning_days` is per node; portfolio nodes need per-product-per-line validation lead time: media fills sized at the maximum batch for batches under 5,000 units (F6-S11), cleaning validation runs set by risk assessment (F6-S04 10.13), HBEL and cross-contamination QRM per product (F6-S07, F6-S08), and the site-change supplement (F6-R02 gate G07, UNCERTAIN; the four-to-ten-month range from search-only secondary sources is [unverified], see section 5). Proposed `product_qualification_days` and `validation_cost_per_product_line`, replacing the flat `validation_factor`. Teligent shows that a site-level compliance state can block every product's PAI (F6-S35), so product qualification should be conditional on site quality state.

4. *Release components.* Unchanged per product (F6-R01). A shared QC laboratory adds a queue: `qa_review_days` and `assay_days` become shared-resource waits when several products release in the same week; the RMP guidance names laboratory availability as a vulnerability (F6-S12).

5. *Inventory policy.* `shelf_life_months` and `expiry_scrap_fraction` already exist per product. Portfolio nodes change the trade-off: longer campaigns raise stock age; the Civica target of six months of extra inventory (F6-S30) is an observed operating point for `safety_stock_days` (about 180 d) that the S1 comparator already uses.

6. *Contract terms.* Demand pooling across buyers with long-term purchase commitments is the Civica mechanism (F6-S30, F6-S32); it maps to the missing take-or-pay volume term the handoff notes describe for reserved capacity, not to a plant parameter.

7. *Cost structure.* `node_fixed_cost_scale_exponent` and the fixed QA labor input should be shared across products at a node (fixed QA cost per node divided by product count), which is exactly what the failure cases warn about: the cost saving and the common cause are the same object (F6-S24, F6-S15). Model both together or neither.

**What each source lets the model replace (summary).** F6-S26/S27/S28/S29: plausible products-per-site and units-per-line ranges for a scale sanity check. F6-S41/S42: lower bounds on format changeover. F6-S11: media-fill minimum for small batches. F6-S04/S06: campaign-length and cleaning-verification structure. F6-S14/S25/S37: event shapes for plant-level common cause (three-month plan becoming a one-year outage; all products on allocation after a non-production asset loss; chemotherapy shortages rising "from 4 to 24" after one cytotoxic facility shut, F6-S37). F6-S13: the conditioning rule for correlation. F6-S30/S32: buffer-stock and contract-term operating points.
