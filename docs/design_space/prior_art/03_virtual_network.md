## 1. Scope

Family 3 is the architecture in which Telo owns no plant. Telo prequalifies existing sterile-injectable manufacturers and CDMOs, holds a product-specific technical-transfer package for each covered product, pays for or contracts reserved campaign capacity, decides when a covered product is activated and how output is allocated, and runs the common operating and data layer across sites. In the feasibility model's terms this is a strategy that replaces owned-node capacity, commissioning delay, and replicated fixed quality cost with contract capacity, activation lead time, reservation fees, and technical-transfer lead time, while leaving the release components (sterility, environmental monitoring, chemical CQAs, QA disposition) with whichever quality unit legally owns the application. The question this dossier answers is whether each piece already exists, what happened when it was tried, and which regulation constrains a coordinator that holds neither the application nor the facility.

## 3. What already exists

### 3.1 Government-paid reserved capacity across prequalified private sites (the closest structural precedent)

**EU FAB (HERA, 2022 to present).** A framework contract signed 2023-06-30 reserves vaccine capacity and a priority right at four contractors (Pfizer Ireland and Belgium for mRNA; Bilthoven Biologicals for vector; HIPRA, CZ Vaccines and Reig Jofre for protein) [F3-S1, F3-S5] for 325 million doses per year, with the tender specifying a minimum of 450 million doses under all lots, an estimated total value of EUR 160 million and a 48-month duration [F3-S1, F3-S2]. The Commission's 2025 review of HERA states the same figure as an annual one: "Annually, EUR 160 million is devoted to ensuring sufficient and agile manufacturing capacities for three different vaccine platforms in six production facilities located across Europe" [F3-S3]. The two readings of EUR 160 million conflict by about four times. F3-S2 is a pre-award tender estimate of total contract value over 48 months; F3-S3 is later and describes the programme in operation. Where a single figure is needed this dossier prefers F3-S3, but every use below carries the comparator as a range of roughly EUR 40 million to EUR 160 million per year and repeats the conflict. No public contract value settles it; the framework contract is not published (section 9). Two phases are defined: a preparedness phase in which facilities keep equipment current, staff trained and supply chains monitored, and a crisis phase in which the Commission decides to purchase and activates the network [F3-S1]. The contracting entity buys and pays for unused capacity as insurance [F3-S6]. One member (Bilthoven Biologicals) built new fill/finish isolator capacity under the program and inaugurated it in January 2025 [F3-S4]. Scale: national-bloc, vaccines only. Whether the network has ever been activated is [unverified]: no source found states that it was or was not, and the 2025 review describes readiness rather than an activation [F3-S3].

**BARDA Fill Finish Manufacturing Network (2013 to 2023).** Four IDIQ contracts awarded 2013-09-27 to Cook Pharmica (Bloomington, IN), Patheon Pharmaceuticals (Greenville, NC), Par Pharmaceuticals (Rochester, MI) and Nanotherapeutics (Alachua, FL), plus two 2016 awards (ABL and IDT Biologika, Rockville, MD); the stated purpose was an additional 117 million influenza vaccine doses in a pandemic and the network's listed services include technology transfer, regulatory support, formulation and fill-finish of vials and syringes [F3-S11]. The vehicle ceiling was $39.8 million with about $62.6 million obligated over its ten-year ordering period [F3-S13]. F3-S11 and F3-S13 name no successor owners; the ownership history is in section 4 and is sourced to the parties' own releases. Scale: small. It is the nearest US analogue to "prequalified sterile fill-finish capacity held on retainer".

**BARDA CIADM capacity reservation task orders (2020 to 2021).** Under the 2012 CIADM IDIQs, BARDA issued a $628.25 million task order to Emergent on 2020-05-30 of which $542.75 million was for CDMO capacity reservation at Bayview, Camden and Rockville and $85.5 million for fill/finish expansion [F3-S9], and a task order to Texas A&M/Fujifilm Diosynth Texas that reserved the Flexible Biomanufacturing Facility exclusively for BARDA-designated developers, required the subcontractor to delay or transfer other customers' programs, and gave BARDA "the exclusive right to make the final determination and designation of the use of the capacity" [F3-S10]. The reservation was justified as securing capacity "before other market forces capture it" [F3-S15]. GAO's account of the outcome is in section 4.

**BARDA and DoD fill-finish expansions at commercial CDMOs (2020 to 2022).** GRAM (Grand Rapids, MI) had fill/finish capacity on a commercial line reserved for federal partners in August 2020 [F3-S14]; Jubilant HollisterStier (Spokane, WA) received a $149.6 million cooperative agreement in 2022 to double injectable filling capacity at a total cost of $193 million by 2025 [F3-S16]; Curia (Albuquerque, NM) received a cooperative agreement in 2022 for an isolated high-speed BSL-2 vial line [F3-S17]. BARDA states that it has invested more than $2 billion in surge-capacity expansion including aseptic fill/finish [F3-S12]. These are capital grants for capacity that the recipient then operates commercially, with government priority access, not standing reservations. [inference: the Texas A&M task order shows the priority-access form; F3-S10 states that after expansion "the Contractor and FDBT agree to provide the Government priority access to the equipment funded by the government ... upon reasonable advance notice and subject to payment of reasonable fees".]

**BARDA "CDMO network" (2021).** AMRI/Curia (Albuquerque) and Phlow were "approved for inclusion" in a BARDA CDMO network whose sites "must be vetted and prequalified for the immediate production of medical countermeasures" [F3-S18, F3-S34]. BARDA's 2018 RFI that preceded it lists the three structural problems of such networks: government visibility into CDMO capacity when the sponsor, not the government, holds the CDMO contract; prime/sponsor contractual friction when the CDMO is prime; and the fact that preparedness work has little annualized revenue, which "has been a deterrent to USG partnering in the past" [F3-S19]. [inference: this RFI is the most direct public statement of why a third-party capacity coordinator struggles to get CDMOs to hold capacity for intermittent work.]

**BioMaP-Consortium (2023 to present).** BARDA's successor to CIADM is a 10-year OTA consortium managed by ATI with three domains, one of which is "Biomanufacturing Capacity Expansion and Reservation" [F3-S12, F3-S20]. The only awards named in the consortium manager's own announcement are two key-starting-material and API projects, not fill-finish reservations: "Antheia, Inc. received $11 million in federal funding for a two-year agreement" and "Capra Biosciences, Inc. received $7.5 million for a two-year agreement" [F3-S20]. An earlier version of this dossier listed four further recipients taken from a page that was never fetched and never entered the sources table; those names are removed rather than asserted. The repository already records that the large-volume-parenteral RPP closed 2026-03-16 and that RPPs demand MRL 6+ [F3-R7].

**CEPI Vaccine Manufacturing Facility Network.** CEPI reserved drug-product capacity at Biofabri and GC Pharma for defined windows in 2020 to 2022 with options to extend [F3-S22], and in February 2026 added Samsung Biologics with guaranteed access to up to 50 million doses and one billion doses of drug substance, an up to $20 million budget to "pre-position a ready-to-activate process", and a simulated outbreak exercise intended to help prepare "a generic regulatory submission dossier" [F3-S21]. This is the closest public precedent for maintaining a product-agnostic, pre-positioned technical package at a third-party site.

**UK (2020 to 2022).** The UK government contracted Wockhardt (Wrexham) for 18 months of fill-finish [F3-S58] and funded the Vaccine Manufacturing and Innovation Centre, which was sold to Catalent before opening [F3-S59, F3-S60].

### 3.2 Private demand-aggregator with a contracted manufacturing network (the closest business-model precedent)

**Civica Rx (2018 to present).** Civica launched with take-or-pay "minimum viable volume" contracts under which members commit, drug by drug, to about 50% of expected volume for a minimum of five years, and used those commitments to sign supply contracts with CMOs that already held ANDAs, many of them "underutilized or even dormant" [F3-S23]. Products are made under the CMO's ANDA with Civica labeling and NDC (Xellia for vancomycin and daptomycin; Exela for sodium bicarbonate; Hikma for 14 sterile injectables [F3-S75]) [F3-S26, F3-S27]. GAO reports 14 manufacturer partners making 77 sterile generic injectables under the Civica label, back-up sources for certain drugs, multi-year contracts with manufacturers, a 6-month reserve target, and that from 2024 Civica no longer uses 5-year member terms though it keeps multi-year contracts [F3-S25]. ASPE's case study counts 64 generic sterile injectables sold 2020 to 2022 (350.2 million units, $255.9 million) [F3-S24]. Civica quotes about 1,400 hospitals, over 30% of licensed beds, roughly 80 drugs, cost-plus uniform pricing and a 6-month buffer target [F3-S30]. Civica's Senate testimony describes intensive supplier quality audits and asks Congress for grant or forgivable-loan programs to create reserve US finished-dosage-form capacity [F3-S28]. Civica is now building its own plant in Petersburg, Virginia: three production lines and more than 200 million units per year [F3-S64], about three dozen ANDAs in development [F3-S29], $140 million and 140,000 square feet with an occupancy permit in late 2022 [F3-S31]. Both news sources say the plant is operating but not yet making commercial product, with 2026 as the target [F3-S31, F3-S64]; both were fetched on 2026-09-03. [inference: Civica is the existence proof that the demand side of Family 3 can be assembled without owning a plant; it is also the evidence that the operator chose to stop being virtual.]

**Phlow SAPIR (claimed).** Phlow describes SAPIR as an API reserve plus "a national network of ready-to-activate partners, including 503B compounding pharmacies and commercial manufacturing firms, equipped to rapidly convert APIs into finished drug products" and a "Digital Operations Hub" for inventory, forecasting and deployment [F3-S32]. ASPR states Phlow is establishing KSM, API and finished-dose capability for 15 essential medicines [F3-S33]. Executive Order 14336 (2025-08-13) directs ASPR to fill SAPIR with a 6-month API supply for about 26 critical drugs and states that the reserve was "nearly empty" [F3-S35]. No public document lists which conversion partners are contracted, on what terms, or whether any activation has occurred; the conversion network is a company claim, not a verified operating program.

### 3.3 Commercial capacity-reservation contracting (the mechanism Telo would have to buy)

Public SEC exhibits show the standard forms: a mutually binding suite reservation used "fully and exclusively" for the customer (Lonza/Vaxcyte, 2026) [F3-S53]; a CDMO undertaking to "maintain capacity" contingent on the customer meeting annual minimum purchases, with shortfall payments (Lonza Tampa/Liquidia, 2023) [F3-S54]; and a nonrefundable deposit against the binding forecast, creditable to batch payments (WuXi Biologics/Invivyd, 2023) [F3-S55]. The BARDA/Texas A&M task order uses the same crediting logic for reservation fees [F3-S10]. Sterile-injectable CDMOs with published US aseptic capacity include Pfizer CentreOne McPherson (125 million plus units per year) [F3-S62], Simtra BioPharma Solutions, Bloomington IN (Baxter announced the $4.25 billion divestiture of BioPharma Solutions in 2023 [F3-S61] and the buyers named the standalone company Simtra on closing [F3-S71]), Jubilant HollisterStier Spokane (110 million vials a year, rising to 220 million with the expansion [F3-S72], which a 2022 federal cooperative agreement funded as a doubling of injectable filling capacity [F3-S16], alongside a separate $92 million 400-vials-per-minute isolator line that adds 50% [F3-S73]; the Fierce Pharma article that also carries the 110-to-220 figure is Cloudflare-blocked and cannot be fetched [F3-S66]), GRAM [F3-S14] and Curia Albuquerque [F3-S17]. No published price list for a reservation fee or a shortage-generic campaign slot was found; reservation economics in the SEC exhibits are redacted.

### 3.4 Technical-transfer package standards

WHO TRS 961 Annex 7 defines the sending unit, receiving unit and success criterion (documented routine reproduction against agreed specifications) and calls for particularly close control for sterile products [F3-S50]. ISPE's Good Practice Guide (3rd edition, 2018) is the industry standard for two-party transfers of drug product processes and analytical methods and aligns with ICH Q8 to Q11 control-strategy language [F3-S51]. ICH Q12's Post-Approval Change Management Protocol and FDA's Comparability Protocol are the regulatory instruments for pre-agreeing the evidence and reporting category for a planned change, including a site change [F3-S46, F3-S47]. These are mature; the package itself is not novel.

## 4. What was tried and failed or discontinued, and the documented reasons

**CIADM (2012 to 2021), reserved surge capacity at three sites.** GAO: the program "did not meet its goals"; before the pandemic HHS funded only small batches; when capacity was reserved at scale during COVID-19 the sites "faced challenges reliably producing products at a larger scale, such as poor quality control, that led to the eventual shutdown of one site due to cross-contamination" (Emergent Bayview, April to July 2021, contract terminated by mutual agreement November 2021); an internal HHS review attributed the failure to "a lack of regular manufacturing work"; CSL Seqirus left in 2018 [F3-S7]. The cost side: pre-pandemic task orders averaged about $4 million per year while a ready state "requires consistent funding of $30 to $60 million per year" [F3-S8]. GAO's summary line is that "building initial capacity is not enough" [F3-S8]. [inference: the failure mode is not the contract form; it is that a facility that does not run product continuously cannot keep a validated quality system and workforce, and nobody paid the ready-state cost.]

**BARDA's own diagnosis of CDMO partnering (2018).** Preparedness work is intermittent and low-revenue, so CDMOs decline to hold processes and capacity for it; the government lacks visibility when the sponsor holds the CDMO contract [F3-S19]. This predates CIADM's collapse and was not solved by it.

**FFMN (2013 to 2023).** Obligations of about $62.6 million over ten years against a 117-million-dose target [F3-S11, F3-S13]. Three of the four original awardees changed owner. Cook Group sold Cook Pharmica to Catalent in 2017, and it became Catalent Bloomington [F3-S67]. Thermo Fisher completed its acquisition of Patheon in August 2017 [F3-S68]. Nanotherapeutics renamed itself Ology Bioservices in October 2017 [F3-S69], which was a rebrand and not a sale, and Ology was then acquired by National Resilience in April 2021 [F3-S70]. Neither F3-S11 nor F3-S13 contains any of these names; an earlier version of this sentence cited them for it. No public record of an activation for a US shortage was found. [inference: a retainer this small buys inspection-ready status, not standby labour or slot priority.]

**UK VMIC (2018 to 2022).** A GBP 200 million publicly funded not-for-profit facility was sold to Catalent before completion because "the initial 2020 rationale for the expansion of VMIC into a factory no longer held" once commercial producers scaled [F3-S59, F3-S60]. The Wockhardt fill-finish reservation was an 18-month wartime contract, not a standing network [F3-S58].

**EU FAB.** Not failed, and paid on a standing basis for capacity that has produced nothing under the contract [F3-S6]. The price is EUR 160 million per year on the Commission's reading [F3-S3] or EUR 160 million over 48 months, about EUR 40 million per year, on the tender's [F3-S2]; the conflict is set out in section 3.1 and no source found resolves it. Whether the network has been activated is [unverified]. [inference: this is the price of a warm network for a product class with a sovereign buyer; no comparable buyer exists for US shortage generics.]

**Civica's retreat from pure virtual operation.** The company that proved the contracted-network model has built a $140 million, 140,000-square-foot plant in Petersburg, Virginia [F3-S31], is developing about three dozen ANDAs [F3-S29], cites contamination control as a reason to own the line [F3-S25], and its policy lead told the Senate that reserve finished-dose capacity would need public grants or forgivable loans [F3-S28]. Correction: an earlier version gave the plant cost as $124.5 million citing F3-S25 and F3-S29. That figure is in neither source. It comes from the repository's own market research, which records "One large plant (~$124.5M for ~140M units/yr)" with no citation [F3-R6], and F3-S31 contradicts it. GAO also records the shift from 5-year to shorter member terms in 2024 [F3-S25]. [inference: demand aggregation was sufficient to start a network; it was not sufficient to keep the operator content with third-party quality, and the members' willingness to lock in for five years eroded.]

**USAntibiotics (2021 to present).** A revived domestic plant with capacity for 100% of US amoxicillin demand serves about 5% of the market [F3-S36]; unit production cost of about $4 against a $5 buyer price does not cover overhead [F3-S37]; survival to 2025 required a Walmart/McKesson channel deal and government purchase advocacy [F3-S38]. It is oral solid, not sterile, and is included only because it is the cleanest public statement of the economics of a domestic plant that competes on the commodity price of a generic. [inference: a virtual network that pays reservation fees on top of a CDMO's normal margin will sit above this cost curve, not below it.]

The white paper presents both as needing congressional action: "One way in which this goal could be accomplished is through work with Congress to create new authorities and provide additional funding to develop and implement a Manufacturer Resiliency Assessment Program (MRAP) and a new Hospital Resilient Supply Program (HRSP)" [F3-S40]. ## 5. Regulatory basis and constraints

Legal-status labels follow the protocol (law_or_regulation, final_guidance, proposed_rule, expert_interpretation, unresolved_question).

**5.1 Who releases the batch (law_or_regulation).** 21 CFR 211.22(a): the quality control unit "shall be responsible for approving or rejecting drug products manufactured, processed, packed, or held under contract by another company" [F3-S42]. 21 CFR 200.10(b): FDA "regards extramural facilities as an extension of the manufacturer's own facility" [F3-S43]. FDA's 2016 quality-agreements guidance: the owner's quality unit "is legally responsible for approving or rejecting drug products manufactured by the contract facility, including for final release", and "quality agreements cannot be used to delegate statutory or regulatory responsibilities to comply with CGMP" [F3-S41]. ICH Q10 section 2.7 and EU GMP Chapter 7 place the same non-delegable responsibility on the pharmaceutical company / Contract Giver [F3-S48, F3-S49]. The River's Edge warning letter shows FDA enforcing this against an own-label distributor with no plant [F3-S52]. Consequence for Family 3: a coordinator that holds neither the application nor a manufacturing registration has no legal role in disposition. It can be (a) the application holder that contracts manufacturing (Civica's second strategy, or the repository's Architecture A/C in F3-R1), (b) a labeler under a partner's ANDA (Civica's first strategy, F3-S26, F3-S27), or (c) a service provider to the holder. Which of these Telo is determines whether its OS touches release at all. UNCERTAIN: whether a labeler operating under a partner's ANDA and NDC has any disposition authority beyond acceptance of the holder's release; a generic-drug regulatory reviewer must answer this for the specific quality-agreement structure.

**5.2 Adding or switching a sterile fill-finish site (law_or_regulation plus final_guidance with binding reporting categories).** 21 CFR 314.70(b) requires a prior approval supplement for a change to facilities with substantial potential to affect quality, and (b)(2)(iii) names changes that may affect sterility assurance including changes to aseptic processing steps [F3-S44]. FDA's 2004 guidance (section VI, binding as to reporting categories) classifies transfer of an aseptically processed product "to (1) a newly constructed or refurbished aseptic processing facility or area or (2) an existing aseptic processing facility or area that does not manufacture similar (including container types and sizes) approved drug products" as a prior approval supplement (VI.B.4), a move to an aseptic area that already makes similar approved products as CBE-30 (VI.C.1.b), and any move to a site without a satisfactory CGMP inspection for that operation as prior approval (VI.A, VI.B.1 and 2) [F3-S45]. Testing-site moves are CBE-30 under stated conditions and labeling and secondary packaging moves are annual-report items [F3-S45]. Consequence: a network of pre-existing sterile sites that already fill similar presentations is in CBE-30 territory for each product-site pair only if the receiving site already makes similar approved products in the same container type and has a satisfactory inspection; every other pairing is a PAS with a possible pre-approval inspection. UNCERTAIN: the CBE-30 route still requires the applicant to have validated the process at the receiving site (media fills, PPQ, method transfer) before filing; the guidance sets the filing category, not the validation burden, and a CMC reviewer must scope the package per product.

**5.3 Pre-agreeing the transfer (final_guidance).** ICH Q12's PACMP and FDA's 2022 Comparability Protocol let an applicant pre-agree the studies and a reduced reporting category for a planned change, and a change that would ordinarily be a PAS "would typically be reduced to either a CBE-30 or CBE-0 supplement" [F3-S46, F3-S47]. FDA's stated exception is directly on point: "for certain changes, a reduced reporting category may not be justified (e.g., certain manufacturing site or other manufacturing changes that require a facility evaluation and preapproval inspection ...)" and the facility evaluation "cannot be effectively conducted at the time of the CP submission" [F3-S46]. Consequence: a standing library of pre-approved alternate sterile sites is only partly achievable; the site's inspection status at activation time cannot be pre-cleared. UNCERTAIN: whether FDA would accept a CP that names two or more pre-qualified aseptic sites for a shortage generic with the activation choice deferred; no public precedent found.

**5.4 Distributed manufacturing establishment rule (proposed_rule).** The repository's read of FR 2026-14073 is that a DME must be under one management with a single quality unit and that "unaffiliated contract manufacturers and third-party relabelers/repackers under a different quality system are excluded" [F3-R4]. Consequence: Family 3 cannot register as a DME; the DME pathway is for owned or affiliated units. The primary Federal Register text is frozen in the package (S11 in F3-R8) but was not read line by line for this dossier, so the exclusion is [unverified] against the primary text and rests on the repository's secondary read; UNCERTAIN until counsel reads the affiliation language.

**5.5 503B (law_or_regulation).** The repository establishes that 503B copy-restriction eligibility is a product-by-product, time-varying state that lapses when a drug leaves the shortage list [F3-R2, F3-R5]. Phlow's SAPIR conversion network names 503B facilities as activation partners [F3-S32]. Consequence: a 503B node in a Family 3 network can be modelled only as the S7 state machine already in the package, not as durable capacity.

**5.6 Release timing (in_repo, verified).** None of the sources above change the release-component finding in F3-R3: sterility (14 days unless a validated rapid method is accepted), environmental monitoring (5 to 7 days) and QA disposition remain with the holder's quality unit at the contract site; a coordinator's data layer does not shorten them [F3-R3].

**5.7 EU (proposed_rule and government programme).** EU FAB is a HERA procurement, not a regulation; the Critical Medicines Act proposal (COM(2025) 102) would add procurement criteria beyond price, national security-of-supply programmes, contingency-stock rules and collaborative procurement, and it diagnoses fragmented procurement as an investment barrier [F3-S63]. It does not create a private capacity-network role; it would change who the buyer of reserved capacity could be. Status on 2026-09-03: the European Parliament's legislative train records that "A provisional agreement was reached on 12 May 2026" [F3-S74]. That is a trilogue agreement. Final adoption and entry into force are [unverified].

## 6. Adjacent-industry analogues

**Electricity capacity markets (PJM Reliability Pricing Model).** PJM pays generators for the promise to produce when called, procured three years forward through auctions with a reserve requirement, locational pricing, accreditation of each resource's expected availability under stress, and must-offer and offer-cap rules against market power [F3-S57]. The mapping to Family 3 is exact in form: a reservation payment separate from the energy (batch) payment, a forward commitment, and an accreditation step that discounts nominal capacity by expected failure. The difference is that PJM has a regulated buyer with a legal resource-adequacy obligation; US hospital purchasing has no equivalent obligation, which is what HRSP in the HHS white paper would have created [F3-S40]. [inference: without an obligated buyer, a capacity payment for shortage generics has no payer.]

**Semiconductor foundry capacity reservation.** Wu, Kouvelis and Matsuo model the two contract forms used between fabless firms or IDMs and foundries: an ex ante reservation fee paid to induce capacity, and a commitment to route a fixed fraction of realised demand, and show why both coexist and how a dual-sourcing preference changes the choice [F3-S56]. The pharma exhibits in section 3.3 are instances of both forms (deposit creditable to batches; annual minimum with shortfall payment). The foundry model also shows the precondition: a fabless customer with a differentiated, high-margin product that can pay the fee. Commodity generics lack that margin [F3-S37].

**Pandemic vaccine warm networks (EU FAB, CEPI VMFN).** Covered in section 3.1; the relevant lesson is that the buyer is a sovereign or philanthropic body paying an annual insurance premium for a product class with episodic, catastrophic demand [F3-S3, F3-S21]. Shortage generics have chronic, moderate demand gaps, which is a different insurance problem [inference].
