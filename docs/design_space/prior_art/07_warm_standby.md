# Family 7 prior art: warm standby and prequalified capacity

**Prepared 2026-09-02 for the adversarial design-space review (ASSIGNMENT.md Phase B family 7, Phase C novelty review). PRELIMINARY; NOT LEGAL ADVICE. No statement here has been reviewed by a qualified sterile-manufacturing or regulatory professional. Regulatory readings are labelled UNCERTAIN where a qualified reviewer would be needed.**

Absence from Telo's repository is not novelty. Almost every sub-idea in this family has a named implementer, a dated contract, and in several cases a published post-mortem.

---

## 1. Scope

This family covers every design in which manufacturing capacity is paid to exist without producing, so that it can be called on when a shortage or disruption arrives. In Telo's model that is the strategy design variable `reserved_capacity_fraction` plus the two parameters that price and time it, `reserved_capacity_fee_fraction` and `reserved_capacity_activation_days`. The family spans capacity Telo would own and hold idle, capacity Telo would reserve at a third party, backup sites kept on an approved application, technical-transfer packages kept current so a transfer does not start from zero, and campaign rotation that keeps a backup line in a validated state by running it periodically. The economic question is whether readiness cost plus activation latency plus activation failure risk is cheaper per shortage-day avoided than inventory or than a second permanently running site.

## 3. What already exists

**BARDA warm-base contracting for influenza vaccine, 2007 to present.** HHS awarded $77.4M to Sanofi Pasteur and $55.1M to MedImmune in June 2007 to retrofit facilities and hold them ready for two years, with three-year options [F7-S20]. The agency's own program page still describes "warm-basing domestic manufacturing facilities" as a named investment area, with a stated performance requirement of at least 150 million doses of cell-based pandemic influenza vaccine within six months of a pandemic declaration [F7-S11]. This is Family 7 as a standing federal program, nineteen years old and current. A new solicitation for the same posture was posted on 2026-08-17 with responses due 2026-10-30 [F7-S26, F7-S27]. Its described structure, multiple five-year IDIQ vehicles, rests entirely on an AI-assisted trade summary [F7-S26] and is **[unverified]** against the primary solicitation; see section 9.

**CIADM, 2012 to 2021.** BARDA awarded roughly $400M in FY2012 to three sites, Emergent (Baltimore), Novartis then CSL Seqirus (Holly Springs NC), and Texas A&M (College Station), each contractually required to produce 50 million doses of pandemic influenza vaccine within four months of receiving the virus strain, with first doses to HHS within 12 weeks [F7-S01, F7-S02]. GAO-14-329 names Novartis and places it in Holly Springs, North Carolina; the CSL Seqirus successor name is from [F7-S01]. The base contract carried an explicit line item for warm base production and facility readiness reimbursement, funded 100% by the government [F7-S03]. If HHS did not issue a core-services task order, it owed the site a facility readiness reimbursement for up to six months of that facility's capacity per option period [F7-S02]. This is the readiness-fee mechanism the model calls `reserved_capacity_fee_fraction`, implemented and priced.

**Fill Finish Manufacturing Network (FFMN), established fiscal year 2013.** "In fiscal year 2013, BARDA also awarded approximately $40 million to four companies to establish a network of facilities to provide packaging support to ready the product for distribution, known as Fill Finish Manufacturing Network" [F7-S02], to hold aseptic fill-finish capacity ready to package up to 117 million doses of pandemic influenza vaccine in 12 weeks [F7-S02]. GAO-14-329 states no contract ceiling and does not use the term IDIQ; the $39.8M ceiling, the IDIQ vehicle type and the September 2023 ordering-period end date come from the HigherGov aggregator [F7-S34] and have no primary source in this dossier. GAO describes the contracts as funding "the necessary up front activities (e.g., formulation and technology transfer) to establish warm base facilities," and says the network's facilities "will become alternate locations on the vaccine manufacturers' licenses for fill finish activities" [F7-S02]. That is the maintained-technical-transfer-package and prequalified-backup-site sub-ideas, built into a government contract in 2013. GAO also records that the network "may provide assistance in the fill finish manufacturing of other aseptic drugs and medicines during drug shortages" [F7-S02], and BARDA in fact issued FFMN task orders in 2015 for two products drawn from the FDA Drug Shortage Index, each 18 months and including process validation lots and an FDA filing [F7-S25]. **This is the closest existing precedent to Telo's entire thesis, and BARDA established it in fiscal year 2013 [F7-S02], thirteen years before this dossier's access date.**

**Operation Warp Speed capacity reservation, 2020 to 2021.** BARDA obligated over $750M across two task orders to reserve capacity at the two remaining CIADM sites [F7-S01]. The Emergent task order is public: $628,250,000 total, of which $542,750,000 was Task 1 Capacity Reservation and $85,500,000 Task 2 capacity expansion [F7-S04]. The contract text defines what the reservation fee buys and what it does not: it covers "the space and manufacturing activities from batch record preparation through manufacturing execution, including batch readiness activities, labor, and available equipment to produce one batch," and it "does not include tech transfer, process and analytical development, process development, raw materials and lot release testing of a batch" [F7-S04]. Unused monthly capacity lapses [F7-S04]. Separately, the government reserved fill/finish capacity on a commercial filling line at Grand River Aseptic Manufacturing [F7-S21]. The GRAM release states no contract duration, dollar value or period of performance, and no primary award record was retrieved for this dossier, so the term of that reservation is unknown.

**EU FAB, 2022 to present.** HaDEA ran a €160M, 48-month framework contract to reserve capacity and a priority right for at least 450 million doses [F7-S08], signed with four contractors in June 2023 for 325 million doses per year across mRNA, vector and protein platforms [F7-S09]. The 2024 specific contracts are published individually: €107,142,000 (Pfizer Ireland, mRNA), €26,250,000 (Bilthoven Biologicals, vector), €8,983,200 (Laboratorios Hipra, protein) and €17,600,000 (CZ Vaccines / Reig Jofre, protein), each for 12 months [F7-S10]. That totals €159,975,200 for one year of reserved capacity. The preparedness-phase obligation is explicit: keep facilities up to date, keep staff trained, monitor supply chains [F7-S09]. **EU FAB is the single best public price tag for annual reservation of sterile biologic capacity.**

**DLA Warstopper, 1994 to present.** The Warstopper Program "makes investments in industrial production capacity" for items in high wartime and low peacetime demand, including medications [F7-S07]. Its stated procurement philosophy is the opposite of stockpiling: "The preference is to buy guaranteed access to material rather than to purchase or own material outright" [F7-S05]. It publishes the only concrete activation-latency schedule found in this search: for ventilators, "Zoll agreed to make 40 units available within five days of the initial order, 360 by 15 days, 400 in 30 days and so forth" [F7-S06].

**CEPI Vaccine Manufacturing Facility Network (VMFN), 2022 to present.** CEPI funds manufacturers in exchange for reserved capacity: "The partners have agreed to reserve a proportion of Bio Farma's overall manufacturing capacity for supply to countries in the Global South" [F7-S22]. That September 2023 release names only Aspen, Institut Pasteur de Dakar and Bio Farma [F7-S22]. Serum Institute of India joined on 2024-01-23 as "the fourth member of CEPI's global manufacturing network" [F7-S32]. Samsung Biologics joined on 2026-02-03; that release is the first cited source to use the network name "Vaccine Manufacturing Facility Network" and the first to state a reservation quantum, "guaranteed access to up to potentially 50 million vaccine doses, and an additional one billion doses of drug substance ready to be converted into finished vaccines" [F7-S33]. This is Family 7 combined with Phase B family 3 (virtual manufacturing network), operated by a non-profit with no owned plants.

**Commercial capacity reservation between pharma companies and CMOs** is routine enough to be the subject of an MIT Leaders for Global Operations thesis sponsored by AstraZeneca. On one proof-of-concept asset it reports that "reserving a baseline capacity equivalent to 80% of the expected demand and agreeing with the CMO to access an additional 40% flex capacity up to 12 months prior to product delivery upon an option fee, yields to a win-win dynamic for both parties," and that "such contractual terms can lead to a 50% increase in the 10-year return for the CMO as long as the normalized incurrable costs are kept under 17.5% of the total cost of manufacturing" [F7-S23]. The abstract adds that cross-validating all products and assets "reduces the supply shortfall by 6%" against a "typical 24-month window between ordering and delivery" [F7-S23]. **Do not lift these percentages into a parameter register.** The thesis carries an explicit disclaimer: "The data within this thesis has been redacted, obscured, or otherwise altered to protect proprietary information of the company and its commercial partners, i.e. contract manufacturing organizations. The analyzed data and the results thereof are not intended to imply verisimilitude to real data" [F7-S23]. **[inference]** They describe the shape of a real contract structure, not measured values.

## 4. What was tried and failed or was discontinued

**CIADM failed at exactly the failure mode this family must model, and the reasons are documented.** A joint BARDA/DoD "Tiger Team" reviewed the sites in 2018 and "found significant underuse of all of the sites. Specifically, it found that two of the CIADM sites had none of their available capacity used, and the remaining site had 7 percent used" [F7-S01]. Zero, zero, and seven percent. The DoD advanced-manufacturing site, at an estimated 38%, was also considered significantly underused [F7-S01]. The consequences GAO records:

- Readiness was not sustained because it was not funded. BARDA spent about $4M per year on task orders before the pandemic against a stated ready-state requirement of $30M to $60M per year [F7-S01]. BARDA "avoided officially designating sites as ready to provide core services, as that would have required the agency to pay sustainment fees, for which it did not have funds available" [F7-S01]. BARDA's sustainment funding requests were not included in HHS's budgets for fiscal years 2018 through 2020 [F7-S01]. That is an executive-branch budget-request step, not a congressional appropriations decision.
- Capability decayed. "BARDA officials said that without regular use the sites were not able to build the capabilities needed to be prepared, such as hiring a sufficient number of experienced workers and having enough time to ensure that the equipment could be properly tested and validated for quality control purposes" [F7-S01].
- Activation exposed the decay. "BARDA officials told us that the sites were not ready to produce high-quality countermeasures reliably at a commercial scale during the pandemic, and were learning in real time" [F7-S01]. Emergent's Bayview site stopped production April to July 2021 after reporting cross-contamination; an FDA inspection reported CGMP non-compliance observations; BARDA and Emergent terminated the CIADM contract in November 2021 [F7-S01].
- The program did not deliver its contracted output. "Partially as a result of these problems, none of the CIADM sites were able to manufacture vaccines reliably for the national population in order to meet the program's surge production capacity goal" [F7-S01].
- One site left before the pandemic. CSL Seqirus reached a mutual agreement with BARDA to end participation in 2018 and did not take part in the COVID-19 response [F7-S01].
- CIADM was ended and replaced by BioMaP [F7-S01].

**Commercial co-tenancy did not solve the utilization problem, and reservation itself was the reason.** BARDA intended external manufacturers to keep the sites busy. GAO records why they would not come: "officials from two sites said that industry partners were concerned about the risk of being forced out of the sites when the government later needed the capacity for other reasons" [F7-S01]. This is a structural conflict, not a management failure. The same priority right that makes reserved capacity valuable to the reserving party makes the site unattractive to the commercial customers whose work is supposed to keep it warm. **Any Telo design that funds warm standby out of commercial utilization must confront this directly.**

**VMIC (UK) was built for surge and sold before it opened.** The Vaccine Manufacturing and Innovation Centre at Harwell, roughly £200M of public money, "had been expected to offer up to 70 million doses of pandemic vaccine, deliverable within a six-month response time from 2022, but the facility never opened its doors" [F7-S24]. Catalent acquired it in April 2022 and stated it would invest up to $160M to complete and equip it; a Catalent spokesperson said "The site presently has no manufacturing equipment installed" [F7-S24]. The cited source records no government statement that the surge requirement had lapsed. What it reports is a UK Government spokesperson saying "VMIC Limited is a private company" and that "This investment by Catalent is necessary to keep the facility open into the long-term," and it attributes the offload only to "speculation" [F7-S24]. The defundability hazard is real but has to be sourced elsewhere. The CIADM record carries it directly: BARDA "avoided officially designating sites as ready to provide core services, as that would have required the agency to pay sustainment fees, for which it did not have funds available" [F7-S01]. A dedicated standby asset is defundable the moment the last crisis ends.

**Munitions layaway was abandoned as too expensive relative to substitution.** DoD "procures services for the layaway of production facilities, the maintenance of inactive facilities, and the demilitarization of ammunition" [F7-S19]. GAO records that for the 155-mm Copperhead round, planners "decided not to maintain a production capacity for the Copperhead because the round is expensive, requirements are low, the cost of maintaining a production line in layaway status would be prohibitive, and there are substitute items being developed" [F7-S19]. Low volume plus high readiness cost plus an available substitute is precisely the profile of many shortage-prone generic injectables.

**Vendors decline readiness contracts when the readiness volume cannot be sold.** DLA assessed expanding Warstopper to cover 30 shortage-risk medications for all Military Health System users and found manufacturers reluctant: "some vendors expressed concern that if the medications were not ordered on a Warstopper contract, they would not be able to sell the increased volume of medications to other customers before the medications expire. The officials added that at least two vendors stated they would not be inclined to participate in an expansion" [F7-S07].

**The structural reason warm standby is absent from generic sterile injectables.** FDA's own root-cause analysis states it plainly: "Most generic manufacturers cannot afford to support redundant capacity and must make decisions about how to make the best use of their available capacity" [F7-S16]. The nonprofit that most plausibly could have built warm standby chose inventory instead: Civica's stated target is a six-month reserve of drugs, not reserved lines [F7-S17].

## 5. Regulatory basis and constraints

**Keeping a line "warm" is not a management choice, it is a regulated activity with a mandatory minimum cadence.** The floor cost of warm is set by these provisions.

- **EU GMP Annex 1 (2022), paragraph 9.38.** "Normally, APS (periodic revalidation) should be repeated twice a year (approximately every six months) for each aseptic process, each filling line and each shift" [F7-S13]. The same paragraph requires "at least three consecutive satisfactory simulation tests" for initial validation and again "after any significant modification to operational practices, facilities, services or equipment which are assessed to have an impact on the sterility assurance of the product," naming "major facility shut down" as such a modification. It adds: "Consideration should be given to performing an APS after the last batch prior to shut down, before long periods of inactivity or before decommissioning or relocation of a line" [F7-S13]. **A line that goes cold and comes back is on a three-run re-entry path, not a two-per-year maintenance path.**
- **Annex 1 paragraph 9.39.** Manual aseptic operations require each operator to be initially validated on each container, closure and equipment train with at least three consecutive successful APS, then revalidated with one APS about every six months per operator [F7-S13]. Warm standby therefore carries a per-operator cost, not only a per-line cost.
- **Annex 1 paragraph 9.40.** "Typically, a minimum of 5000 to 10000 units are filled" per APS [F7-S13]. Media, containers, closures, incubation and inspection for 5,000 to 10,000 units, twice a year per line per shift, is a hard consumable cost of readiness.
- **Annex 1 paragraph 7.4.** Aseptic gowning compliance must be "confirmed by assessment and periodic reassessment at least annually," and unsupervised Grade A/B access is restricted to personnel "who have passed the gowning assessment and have participated in a successful APS" [F7-S13]. A backup site with no qualified gowned personnel is not a backup site.
- **FDA 2004 aseptic processing guidance, section IX.B.2.** "Subsequently, routine semi-annual qualification conducted for each processing line will evaluate the state of control of the aseptic process" [F7-S12]. The same section requires all personnel authorized to enter the aseptic processing room to participate in a media fill at least once a year, and lists "extended shutdowns" among the events that "may be cause for revalidation of the system" [F7-S12]. Section IV also expects HEPA filter leak testing twice a year for the aseptic processing room [F7-S12].
- **21 CFR 211.113(b).** "Appropriate written procedures, designed to prevent microbiological contamination of drug products purporting to be sterile, shall be established and followed. Such procedures shall include validation of all aseptic and sterilization processes" [F7-S15]. This is the statutory hook that makes the Annex 1 and FDA guidance cadences enforceable expectations rather than advice.
- **21 CFR 211.25(a) and 211.68(a).** Training "shall be conducted by qualified individuals on a continuing basis and with sufficient frequency to assure that employees remain familiar with CGMP requirements applicable to them"; equipment "shall be routinely calibrated, inspected, or checked according to a written program designed to assure proper performance" [F7-S15, same frozen CFR snapshot]. Readiness cost includes a full training and calibration program on a line producing nothing.
- **FDA 2011 Process Validation guidance, Stage 3.** "Stage 3 – Continued Process Verification: Ongoing assurance is gained during routine production that the process remains in a state of control" [F7-S18]. **UNCERTAIN:** the guidance frames Stage 3 around routine production. What "continued process verification" means for a line with no routine production, and whether periodic APS plus periodic engineering runs is an acceptable substitute, is exactly the kind of question a qualified reviewer must answer. Do not assume it.
- **21 CFR 314.70(b)(2)(iii).** Changes "that may affect drug substance or drug product sterility assurance, such as changes in drug substance, drug product, or component sterilization method(s) or an addition, deletion, or substitution of steps in an aseptic processing operation" are major changes requiring a supplement submitted and approved before distribution [F7-S14]. **UNCERTAIN:** whether activating a pre-qualified backup aseptic site under an existing application is a prior approval supplement, a CBE-30, or already covered by a site listed in the approved application depends entirely on how the site was filed. The repo already flags this correctly as gate G07 with a lead time to be elicited, not assumed [F7-S31]. The decisive design question for Family 7 is whether the backup site is *already named in the approved application* (in which case activation is a scheduling event) or *added at activation* (in which case activation includes a supplement review and possibly a preapproval inspection, and the reserved capacity does not shorten the response at all).
- **BARDA's own reading of licence maintenance.** GAO reports that "based on FDA requirements to maintain the license for the pandemic influenza vaccine, the CIADMs may need to produce one annual lot of the vaccine" [F7-S02]. **UNCERTAIN and product-specific**, and stated by BARDA rather than by FDA, but it is the only public statement found that puts a number on the campaign-rotation sub-idea: one lot per year per product per site.
- **The DME proposed rule does not rescue this.** The repository's analysis of FR doc 2026-14073 records a "prior preapproval inspection for an approved application describing the decentralized strategy" and a minimum topology of "at least one movable DMU, or a minimum of two DMUs" [F7-S36]. Nothing in the reported conditions creates a shortcut for activating a dormant unit. That reading is second-hand: the primary Federal Register text was not read for this dossier. Treat the rule as neutral to Family 7 until it is.

## 6. Adjacent-industry analogues

**Electricity capacity markets.** PJM's "capacity market, called the Reliability Pricing Model, ensures long-term grid reliability by securing the appropriate amount of power supply resources needed to meet predicted energy demand in the future" [F7-S35]. It pays for availability rather than for energy delivered. **[inference]** That is structurally analogous to a manufacturing reservation fee. PJM found that availability payments alone were not enough and added Capacity Performance, described as "designed to increase the reliability of individual resources by offering incentives for investment and penalties for non-performance during stressed conditions" [F7-S28]. The design lesson transfers directly and is uncomfortable for this family: PJM concluded that "Resources assume virtually all financial risks if they do not meet their power supply obligations" [F7-S28], because paying for nominal availability without testing and penalising performance produced non-delivery when it mattered. The CIADM outcome is the pharmaceutical version of the same failure, with no penalty regime attached. Any Telo reservation contract that does not price non-performance is repeating an error that a different industry has already diagnosed and fixed.

**Defense industrial base layaway.** DoD has maintained inactive production lines for decades and buys "services for the layaway of production facilities, the maintenance of inactive facilities" as a distinct procurement category [F7-S19]. The relevant transferable finding is a negative one: for the Copperhead round, planners concluded "the cost of maintaining a production line in layaway status would be prohibitive" given low requirements, high unit cost, and available substitutes [F7-S19]. Substitution economics beat readiness economics for low-volume items. Many shortage-prone injectables have clinical substitutes, which is a direct argument for evaluating warm standby only on products where substitution is genuinely hard.

**DLA guaranteed-access contracting.** Warstopper is a mature, funded, multi-decade version of "buy the option, not the asset": "The preference is to buy guaranteed access to material rather than to purchase or own material outright" [F7-S05]. It uses vendor-managed inventory and corporate exigency contract vehicles [F7-S05], requires vendors to rotate stock so material does not expire [F7-S06], and delivers graded activation commitments rather than a single lead time [F7-S06]. It is also the only source found that documents the supply-side refusal condition: vendors decline when the readiness volume cannot be sold into the commercial market before expiry [F7-S07]. That refusal condition is the single most transferable operational constraint in this section, because generic injectables are short-dated and low-margin.

One further structural implication that is not a parameter. **Reserved capacity creates a channel conflict that the model does not currently represent.** GAO records that industry partners would not use CIADM space because they "were concerned about the risk of being forced out of the sites when the government later needed the capacity for other reasons" [F7-S01]. If Telo's business model is to fund standby capacity from commercial work on the same line, the priority right that gives the standby its value suppresses the commercial demand that pays for it. That coupling belongs in the strategy configuration as an explicit assumption, and it argues against any design where the reserving party and the commercial customer are different entities on the same asset.
