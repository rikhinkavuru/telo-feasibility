## 1. Scope

This family covers the choice of sterilization route (terminal sterilization in the final container versus sterilizing filtration followed by aseptic processing) and everything that choice determines about how a batch is released. In Telo's model that is the release-component vector `sterility_incubation_days`, `environmental_monitoring_days`, `assay_days`, `endotoxin_days` and `qa_review_days`, plus the hard gate `G04` (aseptic processing or terminal sterilization validated) and the release-assurance gate `G15`. It spans parametric release of terminally sterilized product, real-time release testing (RTRT) of chemical attributes, process analytical technology (PAT), rapid microbiological methods (RMMs) as validated alternatives to USP `<71>`, container-closure integrity testing (CCIT), and the question the model actually turns on: for each product archetype, which release components are legally mandatory before shipment and which can be compressed or removed with evidence. The finding that matters most is that the only mechanism that removes the sterility test entirely is a property of the *process route*, not of any analytical or statistical layer.

## 3. What already exists

**Parametric release of terminally sterilized parenterals, United States, since 1985.** FDA's compliance policy guide records the origin date directly: "FDA first approved supplemental new drug applications in 1985 for certain large volume parenteral drug products that substituted parametric release for routine lot-by-lot sterility testing of the finished product" [F11-S02]. The mechanism is a full substitution: "A firm may rely on a parametric release strategy and need not perform end-product sterility testing when the firm meets and documents assurances for both of the following conditions" [F11-S02]. The submission content was formalised in a 2010 CMC guidance which defines what has to be filed and how [F11-S03]. This is forty-one years old and it is the only mechanism found in this search that removes the sterility test itself from the release path of a drug product.

**The four systems a parametric release program requires** are enumerated by FDA: "(1) sterilization process validation and control, (2) verification by load monitor(s), (3) a validated container/closure system, and (4) an effective Quality System" [F11-S02]. The load monitor is what stands in for the laboratory test: "If a firm's sterility assurance program is in a demonstrated state of control, as defined above, an appropriate load monitor, considered a critical process parameter under a parametric release program, would satisfy the requirements for a laboratory test (21 CFR 211.167(a))" [F11-S02]. FDA's own guidance says the program can be *better* than the test: "Meeting the requirements of the parametric release process can provide greater assurance that a batch meets the sterility requirement than can be achieved with a sterility test of finished units drawn from the batch" [F11-S03].

**Parametric release in the EU, with a wider modality scope than the US.** Annex 17 permits it for "moist heat, dry heat or ionising radiation (dosimetric release)" [F11-S05]. FDA's CPG covers moist heat only and explicitly excludes radiation and dry heat [F11-S02], although FDA's 2010 guidance leaves a door open: "The principles in the guidance may also be applicable to products sterilized by other terminal sterilization processes, such as radiation sterilization, which may be suitable for parametric release" [F11-S03]. The EU also excludes gas: "Parametric release is not acceptable for gas sterilisation (according to Ph. Eur. chapter 5.1.1)" [F11-S06].

**Dosimetric release under ISO 11137 is older still.** USP states that "The radiation sterilization of articles described in Radiation Sterilization `<1229.10>` performed in accordance with International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 11137 was the first sterilization process to use in-process controls and monitoring to replace sterility testing" [F11-S25].

**Terminal sterilization is the regulatory default everywhere.** FDA 2004: "It is a well-accepted principle that sterile drugs should be manufactured using aseptic processing only when terminal sterilization is not feasible" [F11-S01]. FDA's inspection compliance program: "Terminal sterilization should be utilized when the product and container/closure system are able to withstand the terminal sterilization process" [F11-S15]. Annex 1 paragraph 8.34 [F11-S04]. EMA's sterilisation guideline sets a decision tree and a preference order, and states that "Finished products intended to be sterile should be terminally sterilised in their final container whenever possible, as clearly stated in the Ph. Eur., general chapter 5.1.1" [F11-S06]. The same guideline fixes the numeric envelope: "All steam sterilisation processes require a minimum lethality of F0 ≥ 8 minutes and a minimum process hold temperature of 110 °C", with a routine bioburden limit of 100 CFU/100 ml for parenteral formulations [F11-S06].

**A named, in-market example of the terminally sterilized archetype.** Baxter markets CLINIMIX parenteral nutrition with the manufacturing route stated in its own literature: "Terminally sterilized; terminally sterilized products represent the lowest risk of microbial contamination for sterile pharmaceutical products (USP<1022>)" (the chapter number in Baxter's brochure appears to be an error for `<1222>`; the brochure's own reference list points to `<1222>`) [F11-S33 companion; brochure at https://ushospitalproducts.baxter.com/sites/g/files/ebysai2186/files/2020-06/Clinimix%20and%20Clinimix%20E%20Portfolio%20Brochure.pdf, fetched 2026-09-02].

**Rapid microbiological methods, validated and reported in routine use at one company by 2010.** Novartis validated a growth-based ATP-bioluminescence method in which "micro-colonies are detected on a membrane filter incubated on a solid nutrient medium", following "Ph.Eur. 5.1.6, USP <1223> and PDA Technical report 33 on how to validate alternative microbiological methods", and reports regulatory acceptance: "now approved by FDA CDER, FDA CBER, MHRA and by the European Medicines Agency for a number of Novartis sterile products" [F11-S28]. The article names no instrument vendor and no product. It records a first FDA CDER comparability-protocol approval in June 2008, ten further Pharma approvals in April 2009, EMA CHMP approval in February 2010, and FDA CBER and MHRA acceptance in May 2010 [F11-S28]. This is one company's account of its own programme, not evidence of industry-wide routine use. The published cycle-time result is the single most useful number found in this search, and it is quoted in section 5 below.

**Rapid sterility instruments are commercial and specified.** Growth Direct: "detecting microbial growth in as little as 12 hours and delivering final results in just 1–3 days" [F11-S34]. bioMerieux BACT/ALERT 3D: "with optimized incubation, a respiration-based method like BACT/ALERT® 3D can detect contamination in as little as 4 days" [F11-S37]. ScanRDI solid-phase cytometry, marketed through Eagle Analytical for compounded sterile preparations: "results in 1 or 2 business days" [F11-S36], with the vendor's own regulatory claim being indirect: "the FDA has indicated that there are approved drug applications using the SCANRDI® for sterility tests for product release" [F11-S35].

**Rapid methods as the de facto release test for cell therapy.** All commercialised CAR-T products are described by the instrument vendor as released using BACT/ALERT [F11-S37]. An industry roundtable with FDA-facing participants records the position more carefully: "Several cell therapies have been approved using BacT/ALERT-based sterility assays. In these cases, sponsors were required to validate the non-compendial assay and demonstrate comparability to compendial methods" [F11-S38]. PROVENGE has been released on a 2-day in-process sterility result since 2010, with the 7-day result unavailable at infusion and a notification protocol for late positives [F11-S21].

**Real-time release testing exists and is confined to oral solid dose.** The 2026 AAPS Open review states it plainly [F11-S30]. ICH Q13 decouples RTRT from continuous manufacturing: "RTRT is not a regulatory requirement for CM implementation" [F11-S08]. PAT as a framework has existed since 2004 and is explicit that real time release is one of the expected gains [F11-S07].

**Container-closure integrity as a 100% in-line operation.** Annex 1 requires it for fusion-sealed containers: "Glass ampoules, BFS units and small volume containers (≤100 ml) closed by fusion should be subject to 100% integrity testing using validated methods. ... It should be noted that visual inspection is not considered as an acceptable integrity test method" [F11-S04]. Deterministic headspace CCIT platforms with 100% inspection throughput are commercial and are marketed as aligned to USP `<1207>` and Annex 1 [F11-S39].

**FDA already prices the process route in days of shelf life, in the one policy that governs the company's current pathway.** The 503B CGMP guidance's Appendix B Table D sets default beyond-use dates for batches of 1,000 units or fewer by processing condition. A finished drug product that "is aseptically processed" and for which "A sterility test has not been completed before release" gets **6 days** at controlled room temperature unpreserved, 9 days refrigerated, 45 days frozen. A finished drug product that "is terminally sterilized", where "A validated sterilization cycle that uses physical, chemical, or biological indicators is employed", and for which a sterility test has likewise not been completed before release, gets **14 days** room temperature unpreserved, 28 days refrigerated, 45 days frozen. Either route with "a completed, passing sterility test before release" gets **28 days** room temperature unpreserved [F11-S45]. This is a fully specified, already-in-force schedule in which the sterilization route more than doubles the at-risk dating with no test, and the test roughly doubles it again. It is the cleanest quantitative statement found anywhere in this family of what the route is worth, and it is worth dating, not cycle time.

**Release before a sterility result, with named compensating controls, is written into FDA's own aseptic processing guidance.** Appendix 3 section B records the practice for cellular therapy products: these "are frequently released from the manufacturing facility and administered to patients before final product sterility testing results are available", and where that happens "additional controls and testing should be considered. For example, additional sterility tests can be performed at intermediate stages of manufacture, such as after the last manipulation of the product prior to harvest. Other tests that may indicate microbial contamination, such as microscopic examination, Gram stain (or other bacterial and fungal stain), and endotoxin testing should be performed and meet acceptance criteria prior to product release" [F11-S01]. The repository has been citing PROVENGE labelling for this; the guidance itself is the stronger citation, and it names the substitute test panel rather than one product's version of it.

## 4. What was tried and failed or was discontinued

**FDA's only attempt at RMM sterility guidance was withdrawn unfinalised.** The February 2008 draft "Validation of Growth-Based Rapid Microbiological Methods for Sterility Testing of Cellular and Gene Therapy Products" (docket FDA-2008-D-0055) was withdrawn on 2015-05-08 as recorded on CBER's withdrawn-guidances list [F11-S18], as one of 47 drafts in a bulk withdrawal whose stated reason was administrative, not scientific: the notice says the drafts "have never been finalized" and that "Many of these draft guidances were not finalized most often because of higher priorities and resource issues" [F11-S17]. It has not been replaced. The Federal Register notice announcing the withdrawal published 2015-05-06 [F11-S17], which is the date the repository memo uses; the two-day difference is between the FR publication date and the date CBER's list records, and neither is wrong. The repository memo's statement that there is no finalised FDA guidance on RMM for sterility testing is confirmed [F11-S41 section 4; F11-S17; F11-S18].

**Container-closure integrity testing was tried as a sterility-test substitute and the substitution was bounded to stability, not release.** FDA finalised CCIT in lieu of sterility testing in 2008 and drew the line inside the guidance itself: "The guidance document does not apply to sterility testing methods for product sterility testing prior to release, as container and closure system integrity tests cannot demonstrate a product's initial sterility" [F11-S14]. Anyone modelling CCIT as a way to shorten release is modelling something FDA has explicitly refused.

**Terminal sterilization has documented product-specific failures, and they are chemical, not microbiological.** For sodium bicarbonate injection, autoclaving at 121 degrees C produced needle-like crystals identified as sodium dawsonite formed from aluminium leached out of the glass vial under alkaline high-temperature conditions [F11-S31]. For norepinephrine, the Sun Pharma patent family documents that dilute solutions autoclaved without stabilisers generate 3,4-dihydroxybenzaldehyde and related degradants, and the invention is the antioxidant plus chelator combination that suppresses them [F11-S32]. This is the actual mechanism by which products end up aseptic: not that heat cannot sterilise them, but that heat degrades them or their container in ways that require reformulation work to fix.

**Aseptic filling is chosen commercially even where terminal sterilization is technically on the table.** Baxter's premix platform is marketed on exactly that basis: aseptic filling "increases the number of premix drug options, including those that would otherwise not be available due to the heat associated with terminal sterilization cycles" [F11-S33]. FDA's approved-product record for Baxter's premix norepinephrine confirms that the release testing includes USP `<71>` sterility [F11-S20]. So for that product the sterility test is in the approved application and the 14-day component is real.

**RTRT submissions fail on model quality, not on regulatory appetite.** The 2026 review of global RTRT frameworks records slow industry uptake and attributes it to "the challenging regulatory issues managing out of specification results, analytical technology limitations, the high data and modelling required to support regulatory approval, and the regulatory burden associated with maintaining the control strategy post-approval" [F11-S30]. This is the closest thing found to a documented failure mode for the class of technology Telo has built.

**The one-way ratchet on RTRT and parametric release is real and is in both instruments.** Annex 17 paragraph 3.10: "In the event that the results from RTRT fail or are trending toward failure, a RTRT approach may not be substituted by end-product testing" [F11-S05]. Paragraph 4.18: "Non-compliance with the specification for parametric release cannot be overruled by a finished product passing the test for sterility" [F11-S05]. FDA's parametric guidance requires an equivalent written acknowledgement in the application, "that sterility test results from the finished product will not be used to overrule any failure to meet the acceptance criteria of the parametric release program" [F11-S03]. Any design that treats laboratory testing as a fallback after a model or a cycle declines is designing the prohibited move.

## 5. Regulatory basis and constraints

All readings in this section are **UNCERTAIN** pending review by a qualified sterility-assurance professional and regulatory counsel. Section and paragraph numbers are given so a reviewer can check each one directly.

**The statutory requirement to test.** 21 CFR 211.167(a) requires laboratory testing for conformance for each batch purporting to be sterile [F11-S10]. 21 CFR 211.165(a) requires laboratory determination of conformance to final specifications prior to release, and carries the only release-before-sterility carve-out in the drug CGMPs, for short-lived radiopharmaceuticals [F11-S09]. 21 CFR 211.113(b) requires validation of all aseptic and sterilization processes [F11-S13]. For biologics, 21 CFR 610.12 governs. The section's source note on the eCFR reads "[77 FR 26174, May 3, 2012]" (fetched 2026-09-03), which dates the current text to the 2012 revision, and that text is method-agnostic: it sets validation and verification requirements and contains explicit provisions for "non-culture-based test methods" [F11-S12], which is the regulatory hook for solid-phase cytometry and nucleic-acid methods in the biologics space.

**How the sterility test is removed (the only route found).** Parametric release under CPG 490.200 plus the 2010 CMC guidance, filed as a prior approval supplement under 21 CFR 314.70, 601.12 or 514.8(b)(2), with FDA approval required before implementation [F11-S02; F11-S03]. Scope in the US: terminally moist-heat sterilized parenterals only, expressly not filtration, radiation, dry heat or ethylene oxide [F11-S02]. Scope in the EU: moist heat, dry heat, and ionising radiation [F11-S05]. **UNCERTAIN whether a US applicant could obtain parametric release for a radiation-sterilized drug product on the strength of the 2010 guidance's "may also be applicable" sentence [F11-S03] against the CPG's exclusion [F11-S02]; a reviewer would have to resolve which instrument controls.**

**How the sterility test method is changed (not removed).** USP General Notices 6.30 permits alternative methods; validation guidance is USP `<1223>` [F11-S26 companion chapter; F11-S24]. FDA's 2004 aseptic guidance section X.D accepts the principle for release testing: "Other suitable microbiological test methods (e.g., rapid test methods) can be considered for environmental monitoring, in-process control testing, and finished product release testing" once demonstrated equivalent or better [F11-S01]. Annex 1 paragraph 10.11 requires validation "for the product(s) or processes concerned" [F11-S04]. This is a method substitution: the test still exists, still gates release, and still has to finish.

**USP `<72>` and `<73>` do not do what trade coverage says they do.** USP's own FAQ: the chapters "will become official in August 2025 but are not applicable until they are referenced in a monograph, in another applicable General Chapter, or in the General Notices. Therefore, `<72>` and `<73>` are considered alternative methods and must meet the requirements in the General Notices, 6.30 Alternative and Harmonized Methods and Procedures. Thus, for the methods applying `<72>` and `<73>`, a primary validation and a verification of the method is required prior to performing testing" [F11-S24]. The FAQ adds that "Regulatory authorities may require supplemental data in addition to the primary validation and suitability as per `<72>` and `<73>` prior to acceptance" [F11-S24]. **This directly contradicts the repository memo's section 4a**, which relays trade coverage describing `<73>` as elevating ATP bioluminescence "from alternative method to recognised compendial standard" [F11-S41]. The memo's underlying conclusion (buy-and-validate, negotiate the acceptance) survives; the compendial-elevation framing does not.

**Scope of `<72>`/`<73>`/`<1071>` is short-life products, and the definition is administration-time-based.** Per USP's FAQ, short-life products are those "with a short-shelf life or with a short time between manufacturing and administration and for which a sterility test requiring a 14-day incubation is not suitable", including CSPs, nuclear medicine products and ATMPs [F11-S24]. A conventional generic injectable with a 12 to 24 month shelf life is not in scope. **UNCERTAIN whether a shortage-driven short-dated presentation could bring a small-molecule injectable into that definition; nothing found supports it.**

**`<1071>` is drifting away from being a release chapter.** The Pharmacopeial Forum briefing proposes to "Change the title to “Rapid Microbiological Methods for the Detection of Contamination in Short-Life Products—A Risk-Based Approach”" [the em dash and the closed spacing are USP's rendering on the fetched page and are reproduced verbatim; this file otherwise avoids em dashes, so a verifier searching for a hyphenated form will not find it] and to "Redefine the application of rapid microbiological methods to short-life products" [F11-S27]. The word "Release" is being removed from the title. The repository memo cites the current official title, which is correct today, but the direction of travel is against the memo's reading [F11-S41 section 4a].

**What cannot be compressed regardless of route.** Annex 1 paragraph 10.10: "Environmental monitoring data and trend data generated for classified areas should be reviewed as part of product batch certification/release" [F11-S04]. FDA 2004 section XII: "Review of environmental and personnel monitoring data, as well as other data relating to acceptability of output from support systems (e.g., HEPA / HVAC, WFI, steam generator) and proper functioning of equipment (e.g., batch alarms report; integrity of various filters) are considered essential elements of the batch release decision" [F11-S01]. Annex 1 paragraph 10.3 requires a bioburden assay on each batch "for both aseptically filled product and terminally sterilised products and the results considered as part of the final batch review", and paragraph 10.4 requires it per batch specifically for parametrically released product [F11-S04]. So parametric release does not empty the release vector; it swaps a 14-day sterility component for a per-batch bioburden component plus a sterilization-record review, and leaves the environmental monitoring component untouched.

**The one relief valve Annex 1 provides, and its price.** Paragraph 10.7: "For some products it may not be possible to obtain a sterility test result prior to release because the shelf life of the product is too short to allow completion of a sterility test. In these cases, the additional considerations of design of the process and additional monitoring and/or alternative test methods required to mitigate the identified risks should be assessed and documented" [F11-S04]. Paragraph 10.10 extends the same logic to environmental data: "For products with short shelf life, the environmental data for the time of manufacture may not be available; in these cases, the compliance should include a review of the most recent available data. Manufacturers of these products should consider the use of rapid/alternative methods" [F11-S04]. Both reliefs are justified by short shelf life, not by process assurance or model quality. This confirms the repository memo's central regulatory finding [F11-S41].

**Sampling requirements that start the clock at t=0.** FDA 2004 section XI.B: samples should be taken "at the beginning, middle, and end of the aseptic processing operation" and "in conjunction with processing interventions or excursions" [F11-S01]. Annex 1 paragraph 10.6(i): for aseptically filled products, "samples should include containers filled at the beginning and end of the batch" [F11-S04]. Annex 1 paragraph 10.6(ii) sets a different rule for terminally sterilized product: "samples taken should be representative of the worst case locations (e.g. the potentially coolest or slowest to heat part of each load)", and 10.6(iii) for lyophilized product requires "samples taken from different lyophilization loads" [F11-S04]. The repository memo's citations to both instruments on this point are accurate [F11-S41].

**Sub-batch multiplication under terminal sterilization.** Annex 1 note to 10.6: "Where the manufacturing process results in sub-batches (e.g. for terminally sterilised products) then sterility samples from each sub-batch should be taken and a sterility test for each sub-batch performed" [F11-S04]. For a small plant filling one batch across several autoclave loads, this multiplies test articles. **UNCERTAIN how this interacts with parametric release, where per-load monitoring already replaces the test; a reviewer would need to state whether the sub-batch rule is satisfied by per-load monitors.**

**Sterility testing does not carry the assurance.** Annex 1 paragraph 10.5 [F11-S04]; Annex 1 paragraph 2.7: "Sole reliance for sterility or other quality aspects should not be placed on any terminal process or finished product test" [F11-S04]; USP `<1211>` [F11-S26]. FDA 2004 section XI.B gives the statistical reason: "Sterility tests are limited in their ability to detect contamination because of the small sample size typically used", and works the arithmetic: "if a 10,000-unit lot with a 0.1 percent contamination level was sterility tested using 20 units, there is a 98 percent chance that the batch would pass the test" [F11-S01]. **This cuts both ways for Telo and should not be quoted selectively.** It is the argument for parametric release; it is equally the argument that a small distributed plant cannot buy sterility assurance with testing.

**Citation hygiene correction to the repository memo.** The memo quotes CPG 490.200 as "It does not apply to products sterilized by filtration." [F11-S41]. The full sentence is "It does not apply to products sterilized by filtration, radiation, dry heat, or ethylene oxide" [F11-S02]. The truncation removes the fact that the US and EU differ on radiation and dry heat, which is a live design variable for any product that could be irradiated.

**A second and more serious citation problem in the same memo: a quotation that does not exist in the cited document.** `release-constraints.md` section 1 states: "FDA 2004 aseptic guidance section XI.B: indirect measurements in aseptic processing give limited information about whether a batch is sterile, so *"the sterility test is an essential element."*" [F11-S41]. The 2004 guidance was downloaded from `fda.gov/media/71026/download`, converted to text and searched in full. The word "essential" occurs twelve times, with no occurrences of "essentially". Three of the twelve are inside section XI, Sterility Testing, and do attach to the test: XI.A, "It is essential that the media used to perform sterility testing be rendered sterile and demonstrated as growth promoting"; XI.C, "The proper handling of deviations is an essential aspect of laboratory control"; and XI.C, "Accordingly, it is essential that all sterility positives be thoroughly investigated" [F11-S01]. So the guidance calls sterility-test media, deviation handling and the investigation of positives essential. It never calls the test itself an essential element of release. The only occurrence of the phrase "essential elements" is in section XII and refers to something different: "Review of environmental and personnel monitoring data, as well as other data relating to acceptability of output from support systems ... and proper functioning of equipment ... are considered essential elements of the batch release decision" [F11-S01]. Section XI.B says the opposite of what the memo's paraphrase implies about the test's power: "Sterility tests are limited in their ability to detect contamination because of the small sample size typically used" [F11-S01]. **The quotation should be struck from the memo.** The proposition the memo wanted is available and is better supported by Annex 1 paragraph 2.7 and by the CPG's filtration exclusion, both of which are already cited here. This matters beyond tidiness: the sentence is used in the memo to support the claim that the sterility test is structurally irremovable for aseptic product, and a diligence reviewer who searches the guidance for it will not find it.

**What the QbR FAQ does and does not settle about the scope of a parametric release approval.** FDA's generic-drug sterility-assurance FAQ describes an abbreviated filing route as available where "parametric release is being requested based on an NDA/ANDA previously approved for parametric release using the identical manufacturing facility, autoclave, container/closure system, critical process parameters, and load patterns" [F11-S46]. The passage sits under 2.3.P.3.3 Description of the Manufacturing Process and answers what a *new* application should file when it references a parametric release already approved at the same facility and autoclave. It is a documentation shortcut for a second application at one site. It says nothing about whether an approved programme can be extended to a second site. The same document requires that the finished-product specification and certificate of analysis be amended to state that parametric release is used in lieu of sterility testing, and to carry commitment statements including "indication that use of the sterility test will not be permitted for lot release when critical release criteria are not achieved" [F11-S46]. **UNCERTAIN, and this is the interpretation a qualified reviewer is most needed on:** [inference] the conditions the FAQ lists are facility-specific and autoclave-specific, which would be consistent with a parametric release program not travelling to a second site or a second autoclave without its own submission and approval. The FAQ does not say that, and no source read here does. If that reading is right, parametric release is a per-node regulatory asset in a distributed architecture, not a company-level one, and its cost scales with node count in the same way replicated process validation does. If it is wrong, the mechanism is far more attractive for a network than this file assumes.

**The 503B release bullets are narrower than the CGMP release bullets in one respect that matters.** Appendix A of the 503B guidance restates the release preconditions as: an appropriate laboratory determination of conformance to specifications; "A review of environmental and personnel monitoring data, if applicable, has been conducted to ensure that manufacturing conditions were acceptable during production of the batch"; review of associated data by the quality control unit; and authorisation by a designated qualified individual [F11-S45]. The sterility-test relief in Appendix A section B is conditioned on sterility testing being *initiated* before release plus a notification protocol on failure [F11-S45], which is structurally the same bargain as 21 CFR 212.70(e) for PET [F11-S11] and 21 CFR 211.165(a) for short-lived radiopharmaceuticals [F11-S09]. **No comparable relief exists anywhere in the sources read here for the environmental and personnel monitoring review**, whose only softening is Annex 1 10.10 for short-shelf-life product [F11-S04].

**Standards that would sit under a terminal sterilization gate.** ISO 17665:2024 (moist heat) is a single consolidated first edition. FDA's recognition record names exactly two superseded recognitions, "ISO TS 17665-2 First edition 2009-01-15 [Rec# 14-277], and ISO 17665-1 First edition 2006-08-15 [Rec# 14-333]", so it supersedes one part-1 standard and one technical specification. FDA recognises the new edition as Rec# 14-601 and will accept conformity declarations to the superseded recognitions "until July 04, 2027" [F11-S19]. ISO 11135:2014 covers ethylene oxide [iso.org/standard/56137.html, fetched 2026-09-02]. ISO 11137-1:2025 covers radiation and is the second edition, published 2025-04-02 [iso.org/standard/81721.html, fetched 2026-09-02]. These are device-scoped standards that FDA's drug guidances reference by analogy; **UNCERTAIN how much of ISO 17665 a drug applicant is expected to follow given that FDA's own sterilization process validation guidance for drugs is the controlling document.**

## 6. Adjacent-industry analogues

**Low-acid canned food, 21 CFR Part 113.** [inference] The food industry runs the closest structural analogue to parametric release at national scale. The characterisation is this file's, not a source's. The recordkeeping and release-review section carries the source note "[44 FR 16215, Mar. 16, 1979, as amended at 76 FR 11923, Mar. 3, 2011]", which dates the release-review requirement quoted below to 1979 [F11-S47]. There is no end-product sterility test. Commercial sterility is established by a scheduled process filed with FDA, delivered by a retort, and evidenced by recorded critical factors; deviation handling is codified: when the process is less than the scheduled process or critical factors go out of control, the processor must fully reprocess or set the product aside for evaluation by a competent processing authority, and "Unless this evaluation demonstrates that the product had been given a thermal process that rendered it free of microorganisms of potential public health significance, the product set aside shall be either fully reprocessed to render it commercially sterile or destroyed" [F11-S23]. Records must be reviewed "Not later than 1 working day after the actual process, and before shipment or release for distribution" by a qualified management representative [F11-S47, 21 CFR 113.100(b)]. The analogue is exact in structure and instructive in one respect Telo should notice: the release decision is a same-day documented review of process data by a qualified person, and the regulation names the person and the deadline rather than the algorithm.

**Blood banking and platelets.** Platelets are released for transfusion before culture completes, under a risk-control framework rather than a test-completion framework: "the bacterial contamination risk in room temperature stored platelet products can be controlled by bacterial testing or pathogen reduction methods" [F11-S22]. FDA's guidance defines primary testing (typically an aerobic culture sampled no earlier than 24 hours after collection), secondary testing, and pathogen reduction as alternative strategies, each mapped to a permitted dating period in appendices A to C [F11-S22]. This is the clearest existing example of a regulator trading detection latency against shelf life explicitly, in a table, for a sterile injectable-adjacent product. It is a better structural template for a shortage-response release policy than anything found in the drug CGMPs, and it is not cited anywhere in Telo's repository.

**Medical device sterilization.** Devices have run dosimetric release under ISO 11137 for decades and it is the precedent USP cites as the first process to replace sterility testing with in-process control [F11-S25]. FDA recognises the current moist-heat and radiation standards for devices and publishes the transition dates [F11-S19]. The relevant asymmetry is that device sterilization standards are written around a stated SAL and a validated dose or cycle, with no expectation of an end-product sterility test, while the drug CGMPs start from a mandatory test and carve exceptions out of it.

**Cell and gene therapy.** Terminal sterilization is impossible by construction: "Sterility testing of the ATMP is an important component in ensuring the safety of the cellular product prior to patient infusion, especially because terminal sterilization is not possible for live therapeutics" [Gebo and Lau, J Clin Microbiol, DOI 10.1128/jcm.01492-19, fetched 2026-09-02]. The field has therefore built the entire release-under-uncertainty apparatus that Telo is trying to invent: Gram stain plus an automated culture as the release gate, a 7-day readout with a 14-day confirmation, and a pre-filed protocol for what happens when the late result turns positive [F11-S38; F11-S21]. A commentary by USP and industry authors goes further and argues the disposition is already parametric in substance [F11-S29]. If Telo wants precedent for releasing on partial microbiological information with a notification protocol, this is where it exists, and it exists because time to administration is short.

## 8. Implications for the model

This family changes the model's structure more than its parameter values. The current release model in `config/global.yaml` encodes a single process archetype (aseptic fill) and a fixed component vector [F11-S42]. Family 11 says that vector is a function of the process route, and that the route is a design variable.

**8.1 New structural object: `process_route` as a strategy dimension.** Values `terminal_moist_heat`, `terminal_radiation`, `aseptic_fill`, `aseptic_lyophilized`, `aseptic_plus_terminal_heat_treatment`. Gate `G04` already exists as "Aseptic processing or terminal sterilization validated" with `model_effect: Hard constraint; determines process archetype` [F11-S43], but nothing downstream branches on it. It should. Sources that would let a reviewer populate the branch: FDA 2004 III [F11-S01], FDA 7356.002A Part III [F11-S15], EMA decision trees section 5 [F11-S06], Annex 1 8.34 [F11-S04].

That sentence is the only empirical quantification of the environmental-monitoring floor found in this search and it should replace the repository's modelled estimate.

**8.4 Mechanisms this family changes.**

- **Capacity.** [inference, no source] Terminal sterilization adds an autoclave and a load-scheduling constraint. Batch flow becomes fill then load then cycle then unload, with load size, not fill rate, potentially binding. No source read here states this; it follows from the unit operation being added. This is a new capacity mechanism the model does not have.
- **Commissioning delay.** Route selection changes what `G04` requires: media fills and aseptic process simulation for aseptic routes ("routine semi-annual qualification conducted for each processing line", FDA 2004 IX.A.2 [F11-S01]; Annex 1 9.38 [F11-S04]), versus sterilizer cycle validation, load mapping and biological indicator studies for terminal routes [F11-S06]. These are different qualification programmes with different durations.
- **Common-cause groups.** [inference, no source] A shared autoclave, a shared sterility suite, a shared RMM instrument and a shared validated method version are all common-cause couplings across a distributed network. A single method-revalidation event or a single instrument vendor issue would hit every node at once. Nothing read here documents such an event. The model's `common_cause_*` parameters currently represent physical and supply events only.
- **Release components.** Section 8.2.
- **Inventory policy.** For archetype A the hold shrinks, which reduces in-process inventory and pipeline stock. For archetype D the release vector lengthens. Route selection therefore changes the inventory-versus-capacity trade the model is optimising.
- **Contract terms.** Parametric release lives in the approved application [F11-S02]. In a contract-manufacturing architecture the application holder, not the operator, owns it. This bears on the `G01`/`G07` gates and on who can change a cycle.
- **Shelf life and distribution radius, which the study currently treats as fixed.** Table D makes dating a *function of the process route and of whether the test finished*, on a schedule FDA has already published [F11-S45]. That converts the release decision from a pure cycle-time question into a joint cycle-time-and-dating question, which is the shape the repository memo's section on strategic inversion argued for on weaker evidence [F11-S41]. A model that holds beyond-use date constant while varying release time is answering the wrong question for the 503B archetype.
- **Cost structure.** Two independent review systems for sterilization records [F11-S05 4.17], per-batch bioburden [F11-S04 10.4], and 100% CCI inspection for fusion-sealed containers [F11-S04 8.22] are fixed quality costs per node. They push against the small-node economics in the same direction as every other fixed quality cost the study has found.

**8.5 The strategic consequence, stated plainly.** [inference] The repository has been treating the 14-day sterility hold as a property of sterile injectables and looking for an analytical way around it. It is a property of the *filtration and aseptic* route. There is a forty-one-year-old regulatory mechanism that removes it, and its precondition is that the product survives an autoclave in its final container. For at least one product in Telo's beachhead class the chemistry work to make that true has already been done and patented by someone else [F11-S32]. The design question this family raises is therefore not "how do we release faster" but "why is the product class we chose aseptic, and what would it cost to choose a terminally sterilizable presentation instead". That question has not been asked anywhere in the repository, and `approved_generic_cmo_map.md` records the gap explicitly: whether sodium bicarbonate 8.4% is terminally sterilized or aseptically filled by current holders "is presentation-specific and must be read from labels and the reviewer's knowledge" [F11-S44].

## 9. Unconfirmed leads and gaps

**Compendial texts not read.** USP `<71>`, `<72>`, `<73>`, `<1071>`, `<1207>`, `<1211>`, `<1222>`, `<1223>`, `<1229.x>`, `<382>` and Ph. Eur. 5.1.1, 5.1.2, 5.1.6 and 2.6.27 are all paywalled. Only USP's public briefing pages, chapter introductions and the `<72>`/`<73>` FAQ were readable [F11-S24 through F11-S27]. **The "not less than 14 days" figure in `sterility_incubation_days` is still carried by the repository memo and by vendor marketing, not by a fetched copy of `<71>`.** It is corroborated by three independent commercial sources and by the CASSS roundtable [F11-S34, F11-S35, F11-S36, F11-S38], which is enough for modelling but not enough for an external claim.

**RESOLVED 2026-09-02: FDA's own copy of the 2010 parametric release guidance.** The legacy `fda.gov/downloads/.../UCM072180.pdf` path is dead; the live path is `https://www.fda.gov/media/71461/download`. The document was downloaded, converted to text and searched. The definition quoted in F11-S03 is verbatim, and two further sentences were confirmed there: FDA "has accepted the practice of parametric release for drug products terminally sterilized by moist heat since 1985", and the application must contain an "Acknowledgement that adherence to the critical parameters of the parametric release program will substitute for the performance of a sterility test as the primary release criterion for the product and that sterility test results from the finished product will not be used to overrule any failure to meet the acceptance criteria of the parametric release" program [F11-S03]. The third-party mirror is no longer cited.

**RESOLVED 2026-09-02: ISO 11137-1:2025 clause 1.2.3.** The clause is reproduced in full inside FDA's own recognized-consensus-standards record for ISO 11137-1 Second edition 2025-04 (Rec# 14-611, FR Recognition List 064, entered 2025-05-26), which was fetched [F11-S40]. FDA's rendering reads "a pharmacopoeia test for sterility"; ISO's own published sample and three distributor listings render the same clause as "a pharmacopoeial test for sterility". The one-letter difference is FDA's transcription, not a substantive variation, and the quote in the sources table is FDA's wording because that is the page that was fetched. One caveat that limits how far this can be carried: the clause governs *medical devices*, and clause 1.1 says so. It is evidence about the device regime, and about why USP describes radiation as the first process to replace sterility testing [F11-S25], not a statement about drug products. FDA's own drug-side policy still excludes radiation from parametric release [F11-S02] while EU Annex 17 4.3 permits it [F11-S05].

**PDA TR30 (revised 2012) and TR33 not read.** Only the PDA bookstore description and table of contents were fetched. TR30 is the industry anchor for parametric release programme design and TR33 is the anchor for RMM validation. Both are paywalled.

**Kakhi, Li and Dorantes (2023), J Pharm Sci 112(10):2604-2615, DOI 10.1016/j.xphs.2023.08.004.** Referenced by the repository memo and by the 2026 AAPS review. Only search-result highlights were obtained; the article itself was not fetched. The claim that of eight continuous-manufacturing case studies only three dissolution RTRT submissions were approved comes from the AAPS review's summary of it and was not verified against either primary text.

**No approved-product evidence found for RMM sterility release of a US small-molecule injectable.** bioMerieux says approved applications exist and that the details are confidential [F11-S35]; Eagle's page identifies no application and no drug master file, and claims only that ScanRDI "is an accepted alternative to the official compendial sterility test method" and "fulfills the method suitability requirement as outlined in USP <1223>" [F11-S36]; Novartis published its own acceptances in 2010 [F11-S28]. FDA publishes no list. **The repository memo's instruction to verify before external use stands, and this search did not resolve it.** The specific unanswered question: whether any currently marketed, aseptically filled, small-molecule sterile injectable in the US releases on a rapid method rather than USP `<71>`.

**Sterilization route for the candidate products is still not established from primary sources.** For norepinephrine, the Baxter premix is aseptically filled per Baxter's own platform description [F11-S33] and its FDA summary review confirms USP `<71>` release testing [F11-S20], while Sun Pharma holds patents on an autoclavable presentation [F11-S32]; whether Sun's product is approved and marketed in the US was not established. For sodium bicarbonate, a 2026 J Pharm Sci communication states that products are "either aseptically processed or terminally sterilized, depending on formulation stability" [F11-S31] and the Hospira 8.4% label does not state the route (labeling.pfizer.com/ShowLabeling.aspx?id=4605, fetched 2026-09-02). For furosemide and epinephrine, targeted searches of DailyMed and Drugs@FDA labels returned no statement of sterilization route; approved labels do not generally disclose it. **Resolving this requires either ANDA/NDA chemistry review sections or a qualified reviewer's direct knowledge, and it is the single highest-value unresolved input in this family.**

**Furosemide specifically, second attempt 2026-09-02, still unresolved.** Tried and fetched: the HPRA Irish public assessment report for Furosemide 10 mg/ml solution for injection or infusion (PA2315/163/001), which describes the product as "manufactured using conventional manufacturing techniques" and says nothing about sterilization route; three UK SmPCs (emc products 6275, 13171, 101893); the Baxter Canada and JAMP Canada product monographs; and DailyMed labels for ANDA207552 and ANDA212803. **None of these documents states the sterilization route.** Approved US and EU labelling for this product class does not disclose it, so label scraping will not resolve it at any scale. Two indirect signals were found and neither is sufficient: DailyMed's establishment listing for one furosemide ANDA lists "sterilize" among the site's business operations, which distinguishes nothing between routes; and CN103371967A (Shanghai Harvest Pharmaceutical, filed 2012, status Pending) describes a furosemide injection process with a terminal cycle of "121 degrees C 10 to 20 minutes" reaching F0 at or above 12, against a Chinese regulatory instruction that small-volume injections should use terminal sterilization. That is a machine-translated Chinese application, it is not a US approval, and the Google Patents description text could not be retrieved cleanly enough to quote verbatim, so it is recorded here as a lead rather than as a source row. **What would settle it:** the sterility-assurance section of a US ANDA chemistry review, an FDA establishment inspection report naming the autoclave cycle, or a qualified reviewer's direct knowledge. Given that furosemide is a simple alkaline aqueous solution in glass at pH 8.0 to 9.3, which the repository sources to a DailyMed label ("Formulation is furosemide + NaCl + NaOH/HCl + WFI, pH 8.0–9.3") [F11-S48], and that FDA, EMA and Annex 1 all make terminal sterilization the default where the product tolerates it [F11-S01, F11-S04, F11-S06, F11-S15], the prior should be that it is terminally sterilized, but a prior is not a source and the model should carry the route as an uncertain input, not a fixed one.

**Not investigated.** Whether FDA's 2026 distributed-manufacturing proposed rule (FR doc 2026-14073) addresses parametric release or sterility-assurance ownership across distributed manufacturing units; the repository's own memo records that the primary text has not been read. Whether an approved parametric release program can be transferred or replicated to a second site under a single quality unit, and what reporting category that change carries. Whether EU Annex 22 (AI in manufacture) interacts with load-monitor data review. Blow-fill-seal as a route that combines container forming with terminal-sterilization compatibility, which appears in Annex 1 8.96 to 8.106 and FDA 2004 Appendix 2 and was not examined here.

**Four claims in the repository that this family contradicts and that should be corrected before any external use.** First, the CPG 490.200 quotation is truncated in `release-constraints.md` and omits radiation, dry heat and ethylene oxide [F11-S41 versus F11-S02]. Second, section 4a of the same memo relays trade coverage describing USP `<73>` as a compendial standard; USP's own FAQ says `<72>` and `<73>` are official but not applicable, are treated as alternative methods, and require primary validation plus product verification before use [F11-S24]. Third, and worst, section 1 of the memo attributes to FDA's 2004 aseptic processing guidance a sentence that the guidance does not contain: "the sterility test is an essential element". The full text was searched and the sentence is absent. The word "essential" occurs twelve times. Three of them are in section XI and attach to sterility-test media, to deviation handling and to the investigation of positives, not to the test as a release element. The phrase "essential elements" appears once, in section XII, about environmental and personnel monitoring data [F11-S01 versus F11-S41]. This is a fabricated or mistranscribed quotation sitting in the memo's load-bearing paragraph and it should be struck. Fourth, the memo's section 1 treats the roughly seven-day environmental monitoring wait as an unconditional floor; Annex 1 paragraph 10.10 provides an explicit, if narrow, relief for short-shelf-life products, under which release proceeds on "a review of the most recent available data" [F11-S04]. The floor is real for a conventional product and is not unconditional. A fifth item is not a contradiction but a gap the memo should close: the memo never mentions Table D of the 503B CGMP guidance, which prices the aseptic-versus-terminal choice at 6 days against 14 days of at-risk beyond-use dating [F11-S45], even though the memo's own recommended strategy is to sell beyond-use dating rather than cycle time.
