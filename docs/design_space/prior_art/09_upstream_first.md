# Family 9: Upstream-first resilience

Prior-art and novelty review. Prepared 2026-09-02. Revised 2026-09-03 after an external citation audit; retractions and corrections are marked in place. Access date is 2026-09-02 for every web source below except F9-S3b, F9-S28, F9-S29a, F9-S29b and F9-S37, which were fetched 2026-09-03.

## 1. Scope

This family asks whether Telo's defensible intervention lies upstream of the fill-finish step rather than at it. The sub-ideas are API supplier diversification, critical component qualification, vial and stopper standardization, a shared supplier-risk graph, alternative qualified sources, strategic raw-material reserves, component demand forecasting, and maintained technical packages that allow a rapid switch of source. In this project's terms the family attacks the common-cause and supplier-disruption mechanisms in the simulation (`common_cause_events_per_year`, `supplier_disruptions_per_supplier_year`, `second_source_qualification_days`) rather than the capacity, release-time, or commissioning mechanisms that the microplant thesis attacks. The governing question for this dossier is narrow: if common upstream inputs neutralize a distributed plant network, is the defensible product supplier intelligence, qualification infrastructure, or physical upstream reserves, and has any of that already been built.

## 3. What already exists

**Upstream concentration is measured, and the measurement is a commercial product.** USP built a key-starting-material module on top of its Medicine Supply Map and published the finding that 58 percent of KSMs used for U.S.-approved APIs are sole sourced from a single country, that 41 percent are sole sourced from China, and that only four KSMs are made exclusively in the United States (F9-S5). The same analysis reports that China is the sole supplier of at least one KSM for 679 APIs, 37 percent of the APIs analyzed, and gives amoxicillin as the worked example where diversified API and finished-dose manufacturing sits on four Chinese KSMs including 6-aminopenicillanic acid (F9-S5). The 2025 Vulnerable Medicines List extends this to a ranked list of 100 drugs and reports that 48 of them have at least one KSM made in a single country (F9-S7). USP markets the platform to manufacturers, health systems, distributors, GPOs, and government (F9-S6). `[inference]` It is a commercial product, but the fetched product page states no pricing or licensing model and carries only a request-a-demo call to action, so the word "subscription" used in an earlier draft is withdrawn as unsourced.

**Commercial multi-tier supplier-risk graphs exist and are sold into pharma today.** Resilinc markets part-site-level multi-tier mapping plus event monitoring, and states plainly that most disruptions occur below tier one in raw materials, APIs, excipients, and specialty components (F9-S22). Vizient publishes a market resiliency score per generic injectable and reports coverage under its enhanced-supply and reserve programs (F9-S24) alongside the contracted inventory program itself (F9-S23). An earlier draft described this as a supplier-pedigree and risk-analytics platform; neither fetched page supports that, and the characterization is withdrawn. The category is occupied by USP and Resilinc; Vizient is an inventory and scoring counterparty, not a multi-tier mapping vendor.

**Government has already funded domestic API and KSM production with named awards.** BARDA awarded Phlow Corporation a four-year, 354 million dollar contract in May 2020, extendable to 812 million over ten years, to produce APIs and precursor chemicals for essential medicines (F9-S17). The contract document lists a distinct line item, CLIN 0004, to establish a Strategic Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient Reserve facility for the U.S. government (F9-S18). ASPR's industrial-base program awarded the API Innovation Center 13,968,691 dollars in October 2024 to build end-to-end domestic supply chains for all KSMs and APIs behind three priority essential medicines, with a projected drug master file filing date of December 2026 (F9-S19). APIIC announced an expansion in March 2026 covering propofol API and metoprolol tablets, described as the first use of Defense Production Act Title III authority for a finished dosage form (F9-S20).

**Government has already funded primary-container capacity.** Corning received 204 million dollars from BARDA and DoD in June 2020 to expand domestic Valor Glass vial and tubing capacity at Big Flats, Durham, and Vineland (F9-S14). SiO2 Materials Science received a 143 million dollar agreement in the same week, targeting 120 million vials per year by end of 2020 (F9-S15, F9-S16).

**Standardization of container format is an explicit, published industry recommendation, not a novel idea.** Civica Rx's then-CEO argued in the PDA Letter that relying on established ISO standard vial formats shortens fill-line adaptation and that revalidating new supplier locations can take up to two years (F9-S21). ISO standard vial geometry is the incumbent state, not a target state.

**Reserved inventory contracting exists at GPO scale.** Vizient's Novaplus Enhanced Supply obliges contracted suppliers to hold additional U.S.-warehoused inventory of essential medications against demand surge and disruption (F9-S23), and Vizient reports program coverage of 75 percent of adult essential generic injectables plus a market resiliency score attached to each generic injectable (F9-S24).

**Prequalified alternate-source networks with maintained technology transfer are a fifteen-year-old federal design.** BARDA's Fill Finish Manufacturing Network, funded in FY2013 alongside the Centers for Innovation in Advanced Development and Manufacturing, was built as a set of pre-qualified facilities that would receive transferred fill-finish technology and become alternate locations on manufacturers' licenses (F9-S32).

**Network-science methods for finding hidden critical suppliers are published and peer-reviewed.** Kim and Park introduce a critical-locus-of-supply measure and test it on twelve real pharmaceutical supply networks, six U.S. and six European, reporting that it outperforms conventional centrality at identifying nodes whose removal disrupts material flow (F9-S27). Eberhardt and colleagues build a pharmaceutical knowledge graph over German shortage data and use centrality to locate bottlenecks (F9-S26). Amico and colleagues quantify reroute flexibility over 40 billion reconstructed U.S. distribution paths and show the deficit-versus-complexity tradeoff explicitly (F9-S28).

## 4. What was tried, failed, or was discontinued, with documented reasons

**The 2020 glass-vial crisis was largely a forecasting and hoarding failure, not a capacity failure, and the predicted shortage did not materialize on the scale claimed.** Schott turned down requests from major vaccine developers for future delivery of 800 million to 1 billion glass vials in mid-2020 "because the company believed it was too early to make such a commitment" (F9-S29a). By January 2021 Schott "did not see any shortage of vials for bottling COVID-19 vaccines", and its chief executive said an order backlog of 600 million vials amounted to "roughly one tenth of Schott’s annual total output of medical glass vials" (F9-S29b). Experts cited by Van Trieste estimated that a coronavirus vaccine would raise overall demand for pharmaceutical glass and glass containers by approximately 5 percent, on a stated assumption of 4 billion doses per year filled in single and multidose containers (F9-S21). The 5 percent figure is not Van Trieste's own estimate and is only interpretable with that dose assumption attached. The genuine constraint reported at the time was tubing tightness and lead time, with Corning describing an eighteen-month sold-out condition and one CDMO reporting vial lead times up to twelve months (F9-S30). `[inference]` The lesson for this family is that component demand forecasting failed in the direction of over-ordering by buyers, not under-capacity by suppliers, and the corrective was transparency and format standardization rather than new capacity. A supplier-risk product that only signals scarcity would have amplified this failure.

**Purely upstream diversification does not touch the dominant documented causes of U.S. sterile-injectable shortages, and the dominant cause has moved.** FDA's own analysis found that of the 163 drugs in shortage between 2013 and 2017, "62 percent went into shortage after supply disruptions occurred that were associated with manufacturing or product quality problems" (F9-S1). The share is not stable. The same ASPE brief that repeats the 62 percent figure also reports that this "was also true in 2023, when 55 percent were due to an increase in demand and 30 percent were due to manufacturing or product quality issues" (F9-S4). In the most recent year either source reports, demand increase is the larger cause and manufacturing and quality is roughly half its 2013-2017 share. An earlier draft of this dossier said quality "continues to be a primary cause" and attributed the 62 percent to GAO; both are corrected here. The FDA Task Force's three root causes are lack of incentives to produce less profitable drugs, a market that does not reward mature quality management systems, and logistical plus regulatory barriers to recovery (F9-S1). None of the three is an API sourcing problem in the first instance. `[inference]` An upstream-first intervention that does not change quality-system performance or contract economics is orthogonal to the cause that dominated 2013-2017 and orthogonal to the cause that dominated 2023. Neither manufacturing and quality nor demand increase is an upstream sourcing problem in the first instance, so the causal-mass argument against this family holds on both breakdowns.

**Federal API onshoring has repeatedly been funded and has not yet demonstrably reduced shortage counts.** Phlow was funded in 2020 (F9-S17) and APIIC in 2024 (F9-S19), while ASHP counted 223 active shortages in the most recent quarter reported, "trending up for the second quarter in a row, but significantly lower than the all-time high of 323 in the first quarter of 2024" (F9-S25). The level sits below the 2024 peak and the current direction is upward, so there is no ongoing decline to attribute. An earlier draft called it a decline without the qualifier; that is corrected. No source I found attributes the fall from the peak to onshoring, reserves, or diversification. I could not confirm the current status, output, or option exercise of the Phlow SAPIR facility (see section 9).

**HHS lost its coordinating mechanism and has not yet replaced it.** GAO reports that the Supply Chain Resilience and Shortages Coordinator position was to end in May 2025 as designated funding expired, leaving HHS without a coordinating structure, and that HHS did not concur with GAO's original recommendation (F9-S3). The recommendation status on the GAO product page is more recent than the report and reads differently: HHS agreed with both revised recommendations, and "HHS indicated that the administration is examining both the need and placement of a centralized coordinating function for drug shortages" (F9-S3b). Both recommendations remain open as of the March 2026 status update. `[inference]` The federal coordination counterparty lapsed in May 2025 and its replacement was still under consideration in March 2026, so a private supplier-risk product cannot assume that counterparty exists on any given date. It is not permanently gone, which is what an earlier draft implied.

**A structural limit on any upstream data product: FDA itself cannot see volumes.** Woodcock testified that FDA can describe facility locations but not the volume of API produced or where it goes (F9-S2). GAO reports FDA officials' concern that CARES Act reporting will not identify suppliers with enough specificity, and that FDA sought further legislative authority in its FY2025 budget justification (F9-S3). `[inference]` Any supplier-risk graph built on public data inherits this ceiling; USP works around it with proprietary and trade data plus natural-language processing over trade flows (F9-S5), which is a data-acquisition moat Telo does not have.

## 5. Regulatory basis and constraints

**Changing an API supplier in an approved application is a major change requiring prior approval.** 21 CFR 314.70(b)(1) requires a supplement for any change in the drug substance, drug product, production process, quality controls, equipment, or facilities with a substantial potential for adverse effect, and 314.70(b)(2)(iv) names changes in the synthesis or manufacture of the drug substance that may affect the impurity profile (F9-S8). For ANDAs, FDA's comparability-protocol guidance states in a footnote that "For ANDAs, 21 CFR 314.70 is referenced in 314.97" (F9-S37), and FDA's PAS guidance states that "FDA regulations lay out requirements for making and reporting changes to approved ANDAs (see 21 CFR 314.70)" (F9-S12). I did not fetch 21 CFR 314.97 itself in this pass, so the reading of that section rests on FDA's characterization of it rather than on its text.

**Container and closure changes split across two reporting categories.** 21 CFR 314.70(b)(2)(vi) makes a change in the type or composition of a packaging component a major change where it controls the delivered product or may affect the impurity profile, while 314.70(c)(2)(i) treats a container-closure change that does not affect product quality as a moderate change reportable in a changes-being-effected-in-30-days supplement (F9-S8). UNCERTAIN: which side of that line a switch between two ISO-format Type I borosilicate vials from different converters falls on is exactly the judgment a regulatory reviewer must make, and it determines whether "vial standardization" buys 30 days or 6 to 10 months.

**Timing of an approved switch is bounded by GDUFA III goal dates, not by chemistry.** FDA commits to act on 90 percent of standard original ANDAs within 10 months (F9-S11). For prior approval supplements, section I(B) of the same commitment letter commits FDA to "Assess and act on standard PASs within 6 months of the date of PAS submission if preapproval inspection is not required" and to "Assess and act on standard PASs within 10 months of the date of PAS submission if preapproval inspection is required", with priority variants of 4 months where no inspection is required and 8 months where one is and a PFC is submitted not later than 60 days before the PAS, and 10 months for a priority PAS with an inspection where that PFC condition is not met (F9-S11). F9-S12 confirms the same structure and adds that a PAS involving a facility not approved in the original ANDA is initially treated as requiring an inspection. `[inference]` A supplement that adds an unapproved site therefore starts at the 8-to-10-month end of the range by default. This is a review-clock figure for a site change under an approved application, and the model's `second_source_qualification_days` is defined for a finished-dose site (F9-S36), so the two refer to the same object. An API-supplier change is a different object and is not priced by this parameter.

**The pre-agreed switching mechanism already exists in regulation and guidance, and the site-change carve-out is narrower than an earlier draft of this dossier stated.** 21 CFR 314.70(e) provides for comparability protocols. The Federal Register notice of availability states that "a comparability protocol is synonymous with a postapproval change management protocol in the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) Q12 guidance" and that approval of one "could result in moving a drug or biological product into distribution or facilitating a proactive approach to reinforcing the supply of a product sooner than if a CP were not used" (F9-S13). The notice contains none of the reporting-category language; that is in the guidance document itself, which is a separate source (F9-S37). An earlier draft cited both to F9-S13, which was wrong.

The guidance sets out the reduction: "A change that would ordinarily be reported in a PAS would typically be reduced to either a CBE-30 or CBE-0 supplement using an appropriately designed CP" (F9-S37). It then carves out site changes: "However, for certain changes, a reduced reporting category may not be justified (e.g., certain manufacturing site or other manufacturing changes that require a facility evaluation and preapproval inspection; certain major changes to difficult to characterize products)" (F9-S37).

The appendix is more specific than that carve-out sentence and it changes the answer. On whether a CP can cover a move to a different facility, the guidance says: "Changes in the location of an operation to a different facility can be proposed in a CP, but because these changes will generally involve a facility evaluation, they generally do not justify a reporting category other than a PAS or CBE-30 supplement" (F9-S37). It adds that such an evaluation "cannot be effectively conducted at the time of the CP submission, because certain factors at the time the change is to be implemented may be different from those at the time the CP is submitted", and that "If the facility is not currently operating in an acceptable state of compliance with CGMP for the type of operation subject to the change, a PAS will be necessary to gain approval for the facility change and any associated manufacturing process changes" (F9-S37). Read together: a site change is not excluded from a CP, and CBE-30 stays on the table; what the carve-out removes is CBE-0 and annual report, and the CBE-30 outcome is conditional on the receiving site being in an acceptable CGMP state when the change is made. UNCERTAIN: whether an alternate API supplier site with no relevant inspection history would clear that facility evaluation remains the load-bearing question for this family, but it is a question about the compliance state of a named site, not a categorical bar on the mechanism. That is a material softening of the position stated in the earlier draft.

**Drug master files carry the API data and carry a fee.** F9-S12 states that Type II API DMF holders remain subject to a DMF fee the first time an initial letter of authorization references that DMF in an ANDA or PAS. `[inference]` A second qualified API source is therefore not only a submission but a commercial arrangement with a DMF holder willing to issue a letter of authorization, which is a negotiation Telo would not control.

**Component qualification is a standing cGMP obligation, not a project.** 21 CFR 211.84(a) requires every lot of components, containers, and closures to be withheld from use until sampled, tested or examined, and released by the quality control unit (F9-S9). 211.84(d)(2) permits accepting a supplier certificate of analysis only if the manufacturer runs at least one specific identity test and validates the supplier's results at appropriate intervals (F9-S9). 211.94 requires that containers and closures not be reactive, additive, or absorptive, that the system protect against foreseeable external factors, and that depyrogenation processes be validated (F9-S10). `[inference]` Any "rapid source switching" product must produce the evidence these sections demand, at the receiving site's quality unit, which means a switching package is a validation-data artifact and not merely a document.

`[unverified]` The following have no source row, were not fetched in either pass, and are written from working knowledge. Nothing in this dossier rests on them and a reviewer should treat them as leads to check rather than as citations: USP General Chapters <660> Containers - Glass, <381> Elastomeric Components, <1660> Evaluation of the Inner Surface Durability of Glass Containers, and <1207> Package Integrity Evaluation; EU GMP Annex 1 (2022) sections on container-closure integrity and on suppliers of sterilized components. Section 9 item 3 carries the same flag.

## 6. Adjacent-industry analogues

**Federal prequalified surge networks in vaccines.** BARDA's Fill Finish Manufacturing Network was awarded in FY2013 to four companies, with the design intent that each partner with a pandemic influenza vaccine manufacturer, receive a fill-finish technology transfer, and become an alternate location on that manufacturer's license, giving 117 million doses of fill-finish in 12 weeks (F9-S32). This is the closest existing analogue to "maintained technical packages for rapid source switching" applied to sterile product, and it was built with public money against a pandemic scenario rather than a routine shortage scenario. GAO's 2014 assessment was that it was too soon to judge effectiveness (F9-S32). `[inference]` The analogue is instructive in two directions: the design is validated as fundable, and its outcome evidence is thin enough after a decade that a private version would face the same measurement problem.

**European public procurement as the demand-side lever for diversification.** The Critical Medicines Act proposal of March 2025 would require member-state contracting authorities to use most-economically-advantageous-tender criteria including stockholding obligations, a number of diversified suppliers, supply-chain monitoring, and delivery performance clauses, and would let authorities favor suppliers manufacturing a significant portion in the EU where a vulnerability evaluation shows third-country dependency (F9-S31). The proposal's contingency-stock provisions are a separate matter and concern obligations that member states place on supply chain actors to hold stocks of medicinal products; they are not a raw-material or key-starting-material reserve and do not establish an EU-held stock (F9-S31). `[inference]` This is upstream diversification implemented as procurement rules rather than as a product, and it suggests the buyer, not the manufacturer, is the party who can be sold resilience.

**GPO-contracted buffer inventory in U.S. health systems.** Vizient's Novaplus Enhanced Supply converts aggregated member demand into a supplier obligation to hold extra U.S.-warehoused stock of essential medications (F9-S23), with a published market resiliency score per generic injectable and program coverage figures per essential-medicine tier (F9-S24). `[inference]` This is the commercial proof that hospitals will pay something for assurance, and it also means the obvious first customer for an upstream-risk product already has an incumbent relationship covering the adjacent need.

**Distribution-network rerouting research from the opioid supply chain.** Amico and colleagues reconstructed 40 billion distribution paths from ARCOS data and showed that reroute flexibility reduces deficit severity and delays the time to a critical deficit, at the measured cost of longer paths and slower distribution (F9-S28). `[inference]` The transferable result is that alternative-path capability has a quantified price, which is the shape of argument this family needs and currently lacks for API sourcing.

## 8. Implications for the model

This family changes the disruption and switching mechanisms, not the capacity or release mechanisms. Concretely:

**Common-cause structure is the mechanism most affected, and the gap is the tier at which groups are keyed, not the absence of correlation.** `common_cause_events_per_year` (base 0.1, low 0.02, high 0.3), `common_cause_duration_days` (base 60), and `common_cause_capacity_impact` (base 0.5) are tier-5 illustrative values traced to a workbook cell (F9-S36).

**Retraction.** An earlier draft of this dossier claimed that the model treats suppliers as independent draws and that strategy S2's advantage is an artifact of that assumption. That claim is false and is withdrawn. The model already carries shared-cause structure, and sharing is the default. `strategies.py` builds S2's second site with `api2 = "api_2" if independent_api else "api_1"` and groups `("cc_api_2" if independent_api else "cc_api_1", "cc_vial_1")`, with the code note "second source shares the API supplier unless independent_api_supplier=1 (H5)"; `independent_api_supplier` is a declared optimizer decision variable; `CommonCauseGroup` objects are assembled from those group ids with a `kind` validated against a pattern that includes api_supplier, excipient_supplier, vial_supplier, stopper_seal_supplier and geography (F9-S38). The frozen protocol requires S2 to represent "Two qualified sources with explicit shared-cause mapping" and lists `independence_constraints` as a design variable, and the illustrative baseline gives S2 a `common_impact_factor` of 0.65 rather than 1.0 (F9-S39). The S2 result itself was produced under sharing: the research log records the tau = 0.98 run as feasible for both products "both at the 120-day safety-stock bound and a shared API supplier", and credits "a second qualified finished-dose source" (F9-S40). Existing dual-sourcing results are not untrustworthy on this ground.

**What the gap actually is.** Common-cause groups are keyed at the API-supplier tier (`cc_api_1`, `cc_api_2`), so two nominally distinct API suppliers receive distinct groups even when they draw on the same key starting material. F9-S5 is the reason to care: it reports that 58 percent of KSMs used for U.S.-approved APIs are sole sourced from a single country, that China is the sole supplier of at least one KSM for 679 APIs, and that "This case shows that diverse API or FDF manufacturing offers little protection when all depend on the same upstream source." `[inference]` What the model lacks is a tier above the API supplier: a `shared_ksm_group_id` that can place `api_1` and `api_2` in one group even when `independent_api_supplier = 1`. That is an addition to the correlation structure rather than its introduction, and it binds only on the `independent_api_supplier = 1` branch, which is exactly the branch a KSM-concentration argument should attack.

**Parameters each source could replace:**

- `second_source_qualification_days` (low 180, base 365, high 730, evidence_tier 5, source_locator "study placeholder; gate G07 lead time to be elicited"): the parameter is defined as "Days to qualify a second finished-dose source in an existing application" at entity_level site (F9-S36), and the research log reads S2 the same way (F9-S40). It can be re-anchored with the PAS review clock from F9-S11 and F9-S12, which applies to a finished-dose site change: 6 months without a preapproval inspection, 10 months with one, and the inspection path as the default for a facility not in the original ANDA. Adding development, validation batches, and stability, a defensible base moves toward the upper half of the 180 to 730 day range. An earlier draft proposed re-pricing this parameter with API-site timelines; that would silently change what the parameter means. An API second source needs its own parameter, for example `api_second_source_qualification_days`, and its own evidence.
- A new parameter, call it `switch_pathway` or `qualification_reporting_category`, taking values {PAS_inspection, PAS_no_inspection, CBE30_under_CP, CBE0_under_CP}, sourced from F9-S8 for the statutory reporting categories and F9-S37 for the reduction a comparability protocol can obtain. An earlier draft sourced the reduction to F9-S13, which contains none of that language. This is the only lever in this family that changes switching latency by a factor rather than a margin, and its availability is UNCERTAIN for the uninspected-site case.
- `supplier_disruptions_per_supplier_year` and `supplier_disruption_duration_days` (both study placeholders): F9-S1 and F9-S4 give population-level cause shares and F9-S25 gives a time series of active shortage counts for calibration. None of them gives a per-supplier hazard, so this stays an elicitation target.
- `common_cause_capacity_impact` and a new `shared_ksm_group_id` per supplier, sitting above the existing `cc_api_*` tier rather than replacing it: F9-S5 and F9-S7 supply the empirical basis for grouping API suppliers by shared upstream parent. The `CommonCauseGroup.kind` pattern would need a new admissible value for that tier (F9-S38).
- `inventory_carrying_rate` applied to an upstream reserve rather than finished goods: F9-S18 establishes that a strategic API reserve is a real, contracted asset class, which makes an API-held-inventory policy a legitimate strategy variant rather than a hypothetical.
- Cost structure: nothing in this family reduces `node_commissioning_days` or any release component. F9-S33's settled position is not that the 14-day hold is binding. That memo was amended, and its section 1a is headed "Partial reversal: the 14-day wall is softer than §1 implies", recording that rapid sterility testing is commercially available and marketed as an FDA-accepted alternative to USP <71> returning results in as little as one business day. The accurate statement is that the 14-day USP <71> incubation is the dominant parallel constraint in the default case and is partially reversible by method choice. `[inference]` Family 9 is orthogonal to release latency either way, and it does not touch the sub-5-dollar-per-vial margin problem documented in F9-S35, so the orthogonality conclusion survives. An earlier draft called the 14-day constraint binding on the authority of the amended file, which misreported it.

**What the model would show if this family were correct.** Upstream-first would have to move shortage days through the common-cause channel. `common_cause_events_per_year` is 0.1 per year in the base case (F9-S36). `[inference]` At that rate the expected annual contribution of common-cause events looks small relative to routine capacity and release constraints, so a supplier-diversification strategy should score poorly unless the common-cause rate or the KSM-tier grouping is raised. That comparative-magnitude statement is my reasoning and is not backed by any run or output; nothing in the repository quantifies the relative contribution, which is itself a gap. The ablation to settle it: sweep `common_cause_events_per_year` and a new KSM-tier grouping variable jointly with `independent_api_supplier`, and look for the region where upstream diversification beats added capacity. If no plausible region exists, the family is dead for Telo's stated objective regardless of how real the KSM concentration is.

**Business-model implication.** The two non-derivative sub-ideas both point at a service rather than a plant. `[inference]` If Telo pursued this family the product would be a maintained switching package plus the qualification data behind it, sold to ANDA holders or to a purchaser consortium, with revenue tied to holding an alternate source current rather than to manufacturing. That is a different company from a microplant operator, and it competes with nobody I identified in the exact niche, but it also has no demonstrated willingness to pay and depends on an unresolved regulatory question.
