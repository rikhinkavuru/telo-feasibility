# Competitive landscape: CDMOs with US sterile fill-finish capacity

*Prepared 2026-09-02 for the adversarial design-space review. Category: `cdmos`. Nothing here has been reviewed by a regulatory or commercial professional. Nothing here is a recommendation. Access date for every external source is 2026-09-02.*

## 1. Scope

**In scope.** Contract development and manufacturing organisations that sell aseptic or terminally sterilised fill-finish services on US soil to a third party who holds the application. Named targets from the assignment: Pfizer CentreOne, Catalent, Baxter Bloomington / Simtra, Grand River Aseptic Manufacturing, Jubilant HollisterStier, Curia, Thermo Fisher / Patheon, Recipharm, Vetter, Emergent BioSolutions, Ajinomoto Bio-Pharma, Berkshire Sterile, Lifecore, Cognate, Samsung Biologics, Renaissance, Piramal, PCI, Sharp. For each: what US sterile capacity actually exists, what unit of work is sold (line, batch, campaign), what is published about minimum campaign size, whether any reservation product is offered, and whether any price is public.

**Out of scope.** Companies that sell finished product under their own NDA or ANDA (covered in `landscape/sterile_injectable_manufacturers.md`), nonprofit manufacturers (`landscape/nonprofit_manufacturers.md`), 503B outsourcing facilities, drug-substance-only CDMOs, and government reservation contracting mechanisms (already covered in `prior_art/03_virtual_network.md` and `prior_art/07_warm_standby.md`, whose SEC-exhibit analysis of reservation contract forms is not repeated here).

**What this file adds over the repository.** `prior_art/03_virtual_network.md` §9 closes with an explicit gap: "Sharp, Berkshire Sterile, Lifecore, Recipharm, Vetter, Ajinomoto Bio-Pharma, Catalent, Thermo/Patheon: not individually researched beyond the FFMN and Catalent VMIC mentions ..." (LCDM-S34; the source sentence continues "; no published reservation terms were expected and none were sought"). This file closes that gap and adds three things the repository does not have: an ownership map current to 2026-09-02, the two published units of capacity that matter to a shortage model (batches per year and vials per batch, not units per year), and the direction of travel of merchant capacity, which is negative even while total US sterile capacity grows.

**One repository correction.** `prior_art/03_virtual_network.md` states Pfizer CentreOne McPherson has "125 million plus units per year" from a site PDF [F3-S62] (LCDM-S34). Pfizer CentreOne's current network page states "190+ million units of capacity" for the same site (LCDM-S19). Both are Pfizer's own figures at different dates. The lower one should not be carried forward as current.

## 4. Recent events 2023-2026 that changed the landscape

**2023-10-02. Baxter BioPharma Solutions becomes Simtra.** $4.25B cash to Baxter, approximately 1,700 employees, Bloomington IN and Halle Germany, new headquarters in New Jersey (LCDM-S4). The largest merchant US sterile campus passed to private equity and began a $250M+ expansion (LCDM-S3) aimed at "GLP-1 drugs and oncology product candidates" (LCDM-S26).

**2023-10-05. Sharp acquires Berkshire Sterile Manufacturing.** The main US small-batch isolator CDMO was absorbed into a packaging and clinical-supply group (LCDM-S12). The independent small-batch option disappeared as an independent counterparty.

**2024-05-01. Emergent announces it will shut down Baltimore-Bayview and Rockville.** "As part of the operational changes, Emergent will shut down both its Baltimore-Bayview Drug Substance manufacturing facility and Rockville, Maryland Drug Product facility" (LCDM-S30). Rockville was a drug product site; Bayview was drug substance.

**2024-06-20. Emergent BioSolutions sells Baltimore-Camden to Bora and exits CDMO.** Four commercial aseptic fill lines with lyophilisation, approximately $30M, approximately 350 employees (LCDM-S10). The largest recipient of US federal fill-finish reservation funding left the merchant business entirely.

**2024-12-18. Novo Holdings acquires Catalent; Novo Nordisk takes three fill-finish sites.** Completion announced that day; "Novo Nordisk A/S will acquire from Novo Holdings Catalent’s three fill-finish sites located in Anagni, Italy; Bloomington, Indiana, USA; and Brussels, Belgium" (LCDM-S31). Catalent now states plainly that Bloomington "is no longer part of Catalent's network" and is "fully owned and operated by Novo Nordisk" (LCDM-S1). This is the largest single subtraction from US merchant sterile fill-finish capacity in the period. It also removes one of the four original BARDA Fill Finish Manufacturing Network sites (Cook Pharmica, Bloomington) from any future federal network, since the site is now captive.

**2025-03-17. Curia announces a $200M Albuquerque expansion and a Glasgow expansion**, with Glasgow batch sizes rising to 20,000 vials (LCDM-S8). LCDM-S8 states no figure for Glasgow; the $20M figure circulating in trade press is recorded in §6. The stated demand drivers are GLP-1 agonists and new biologics.

**2025-05-01. PCI completes the Ajinomoto Althea acquisition.** Ajinomoto exits US sterile fill-finish; PCI gains a San Diego prefilled syringe, cartridge and high-potency hub (LCDM-S11).

**2025-06-26. Vetter breaks ground on a $285M US clinical site in Des Plaines IL** (ceremony 2025-06-26; release dated 2025-06-27), ready for media fill "by the end of 2029" (LCDM-S17). US commercial capacity is not part of the plan.

**2025-06-25. Piramal breaks ground on a $90M US expansion** (ceremony 2025-06-25; release dated 2025-06-30), taking Lexington KY from 104 to more than 240 product batches per year by late 2027 (LCDM-S15).

**2025-07-16. Thermo Fisher agrees to acquire Sanofi's Ridgefield NJ sterile site**, continuing to manufacture a Sanofi portfolio there while opening the site to other customers. LCDM-S9 is an announcement of an agreement and states the deal "is expected to be completed in the second half of 2025."

**2025-09-02. Thermo Fisher completes the Ridgefield acquisition.** The site is now part of Thermo Fisher's pharma services business and joins Greenville NC and Plainville MA (LCDM-S33). Capacity nominally becomes merchant while remaining partly committed to Sanofi.

**2025-10-10. Jubilant HollisterStier starts its third Spokane line**, $132M, adding 50% capacity, with a fourth line planned to double Spokane sterile capacity (LCDM-S6). Management explicitly attributes the demand to US tariff policy rather than to shortage policy (LCDM-S6).

**2025-11-07. LTS Lohmann completes its acquisition of Renaissance Lakewood.** Announced 2025-10-13 and closed 2025-11-07 "following regulatory approvals" (LCDM-S23).

**2025-11-20. FDA warning letter to Catalent Indiana, LLC (Bloomington), now Novo Nordisk.** Based on a 23 June to 14 July 2025 inspection. FDA records that contamination deviations began in August 2023 under Catalent ownership and states: "We are concerned that release of potentially contaminated drug products continued to occur well after the Novo Nordisk acquisition" (LCDM-S2). A change of owner did not reset the site's compliance state.

**2026-03-31. Samsung Biologics completes its first US acquisition**, GSK's Rockville MD biologics drug substance site, 60,000 L (LCDM-S16).

**The market-structure event underneath all of this.** L.E.K. states the generic-side dynamic directly: "as products go off-patent, suppliers may find themselves filling requests for generics from many smaller customers instead of working with one larger client" (LCDM-S21), and that new demand persistently outpaces capacity growth "for the next five to 10 years" (LCDM-S20). Every capacity announcement above is aimed at the large single client, not the many small ones.

## 5. Unoccupied or weakly occupied intervention points

**5.1. No US CDMO sells a shortage product.** Across every 2023-2026 announcement collected here, the stated demand drivers are GLP-1 agonists, antibody-drug conjugates, biologics, prefilled syringes and tariff-driven onshoring for innovator pharma (LCDM-S3, LCDM-S5, LCDM-S6, LCDM-S8, LCDM-S11, LCDM-S15, LCDM-S18). None names drug shortages, hospital generics, or FDA's shortage list. Several do use adjacent supply-security framing without naming shortage: Jubilant says the expansion means "the Company is contributing to US national health security and helping to reduce reliance on offshore supply chains" (LCDM-S6); Thermo cites "the critical production capacity needed for essential medicines" (LCDM-S9); the Simtra carve-out was said to "help ensure supply of critical pharmaceutical products worldwide" (LCDM-S4); and Curia cites "on-going supply chain reviews by our customers" (LCDM-S8). That framing is about onshoring and customer risk reviews, not about the shortage list. [inference] The intervention point "merchant US aseptic capacity contracted specifically against shortage risk" is unoccupied, and it is unoccupied because nobody has found a payer, not because nobody has thought of it. Section 5.3 is why.

**5.2. Merchant capacity is shrinking as a share of US sterile capacity, even as total capacity grows.** Subtractions in the period: Catalent Bloomington to Novo Nordisk captive (LCDM-S1); Emergent's four Camden lines out of Emergent's CDMO network (LCDM-S10) and its Bayview and Rockville sites shut down (LCDM-S30); Curia Burlington closed (see §6); Sanofi Ridgefield arrives at Thermo Fisher already committed to a Sanofi portfolio (LCDM-S9). Additions are real but format-specific: GRAM's 50 million syringes and cartridges (LCDM-S5), Jubilant's third and fourth Spokane lines (LCDM-S6), Curia Albuquerque (LCDM-S8), Piramal Lexington (LCDM-S15), Simtra's new buildings (LCDM-S3, LCDM-S26). [inference] A capacity model that counts US aseptic units without separating merchant from captive, and vials from syringes and cartridges, will systematically overestimate the capacity available to a shortage intervention. This is a concrete correction to make in the feasibility model's supply-side assumptions.

**5.3. The commitment asymmetry is the real gap, and it is documented.** Lifecore's own SEC filing describes its largest customer agreement as one that "includes annual committed capacity obligations on the Company without minimum purchase obligations on Alcon, and the Alcon CMA also includes annual committed capacity obligations on the Company, and both agreements permit Alcon to source from other third parties," and states that for some products "our major customers do not have minimum purchase obligations" (LCDM-S14). [inference] That is the standard shape of a US CDMO capacity relationship: the CDMO carries the reservation risk and the customer carries none. A shortage intervention needs the mirror image, a buyer who pays to hold a slot that may go unused. The repository already establishes that this instrument exists in government contracting (BARDA reservation fees, LCDM-S35) and in innovator-CDMO contracts (SEC exhibits, LCDM-S34). What is missing is a commercial buyer for hospital generics. The unoccupied point is not the contract form. It is the counterparty.

**5.4. The unit of scarcity is the batch slot, not the unit of capacity.** Piramal's Lexington fill-finish site runs 104 product batches per year at peak utilisation and will reach more than 240 by late 2027 (LCDM-S15). Curia Glasgow's expanded batch size is up to 20,000 vials (LCDM-S8). [inference] Combining these, a dedicated US fill-finish site's annual output is measured in low hundreds of campaigns, and each campaign has a changeover, cleaning-validation and release burden. Any multi-product portfolio node or campaign-rotation architecture in this design space must be dimensioned in batch slots per year, with changeover time subtracted, not in units per year. The repository's strategy configurations should carry batches per year as an explicit capacity variable. This is a modelling correction, not a business opportunity.

**5.5. Small-batch commercial aseptic filling is thinly served and consolidating.** Sharp's Lee MA operation is the clearest US "small to mid-scale commercial production" isolator capability found (LCDM-S13), and it lost independence in 2023 (LCDM-S12). Curia's Glasgow small-volume commercial ambition is in the UK, not the US (LCDM-S8). [inference] If a shortage architecture depends on short, frequent, small campaigns rather than large ones, the US merchant supply of that specific service is one or two sites deep. That is a weakly occupied point, and it is the one most compatible with a distributed or postponement architecture. It is also the point where a new entrant would be competing on cost per unit against sites with far higher throughput.

**5.6. Continuous compliance surveillance of third-party sites is weakly occupied and is the hard problem of any virtual network.** Two 2025-2026 warning letters in this file show why. FDA told Jubilant HollisterStier that the same aseptic process simulation deficiency had been cited in February 2023 and June 2024 and "You have failed to adequately correct this deficiency" (LCDM-S7). FDA told the Bloomington site that release of potentially contaminated product "continued to occur well after the Novo Nordisk acquisition" (LCDM-S2). [inference] Compliance state is persistent across inspection cycles and survives changes of ownership. A network that prequalifies capacity once and then activates it years later is qualifying a moving target. Nothing found in this pass is a product that continuously monitors third-party sterile-site compliance state for a capacity buyer. That is an unoccupied point and it is software-shaped, which makes it relevant to the OS-first family in the assignment. Two cautions before treating it as an opportunity. First, the raw inputs are already public (warning letters, Form 483s, EIRs, import alerts), so the defensibility rests on interpretation, not access. Second, the repository's own release-assurance work (LCDM-S25) shows how easily a monitoring claim outruns its evidence.

**5.7. The regulatory acceleration channel for domestic sterile generic capacity is open and unused.** FDA received more than 80 PreCheck requests and selected seven, none of them a merchant sterile fill-finish CDMO (LCDM-S22). Eligibility required a proposed new domestic facility and a commitment to file an NDA, BLA, ANDA or supplement relying on it. [inference] A shortage-focused entrant with an ANDA plan and a new US aseptic site fits the stated eligibility criteria and would face little competition from CDMOs, which mostly do not hold applications. This does not make a plant economic. It affects only lead time, which per LCDM-S24 is currently an unelicited gate (G02, G07) in the model rather than an assumption.

**5.8. Price discovery is absent.** No published price, rate card, minimum order value or reservation fee was found for any entity in this category, and the repository's earlier pass found reservation economics redacted in SEC exhibits (LCDM-S34). [inference] An independent, verifiable index of US aseptic campaign cost and lead time does not appear to exist publicly. It would be genuinely new. It is also a data business with no obvious source of ground truth, since the holders of the data are the parties who benefit from opacity. Record it as unoccupied but do not treat unoccupied as attractive.

**What is emphatically occupied.** Building and financing US aseptic fill-finish capacity. At least five separate nine-figure US expansions are documented above: Simtra Bloomington $250M+ (LCDM-S3), Curia Albuquerque $200M (LCDM-S8), Vetter Des Plaines approximately $285M (LCDM-S17), Jubilant Spokane $132M (LCDM-S6) and GRAM $100M (LCDM-S5). Piramal's $90M is eight-figure and spans two US sites (LCDM-S15); PCI's "$1 Billion Investment Strategy" spans the US and Europe and is not a US site figure (LCDM-S11); Thermo Ridgefield and Samsung Rockville have undisclosed terms. Adding a small amount of aseptic capacity is not a differentiated act. It is the most crowded activity in the category.
